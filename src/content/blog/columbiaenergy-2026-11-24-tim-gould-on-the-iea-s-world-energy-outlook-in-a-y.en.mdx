> When a thing reaches its extreme it turns back; this is called circulation.
>
> —— *Heguanzi*, "Circulation" chapter (Warring States; my own translation)

In the Columbia Energy Exchange episode released on 24 November 2026, host Jason Bordoff brings in Tim Gould, chief economist of the International Energy Agency, to discuss the World Energy Outlook published the day before. Jason runs this year's arithmetic past him first: with the Strait of Hormuz — a route carrying a fifth of the world's oil supply — choked by war for most of the year, 2.8 billion barrels of oil and more than 60 billion cubic metres of gas vanished from the system, the largest supply shock on record, and global oil demand fell by 2.6 to 2.7 million barrels a day, about 2.5%. Tim says the reading holds, and adds the line I take as the weight of the episode: transitions should not be driven by crisis, and a crisis can only add weight to the long-term options. He also notes every conclusion is tentative, because the crisis isn't over.

## How big the shock was, and what it failed to do

On scale there's no argument. Tim adds up the missing barrels: 2.8 billion of oil, over 60 billion cubic metres of gas. The drop in Middle East gas exports alone was larger than Russia's 2022 cut to pipeline deliveries to Europe. And the disruption ran well past oil and gas — he lists fertiliser, helium, sulphur, aluminium.

The system held, and how it held is worth recording: inventories were full going in, IEA member countries agreed on a 400 million barrel emergency stock release early, Atlantic Basin producers (the US role mattered most) brought extra oil and gas to market, and China's crude imports ran well below normal mid-year. Together that kept the largest outage on record from becoming the largest demand destruction on record.

![A very tall bar on the left represents the supply that vanished from the system, and after it hits a thick wall built from inventories, American supply growth and reduced Chinese buying, all that remains on the right is a very short bar representing the drop in demand.](/figures/supply-shock-vs-demand-drop-en.svg)

Only here did I catch how hard Jason's question actually was. He's saying: you take a strait carrying a fifth of global supply, shut most of it for close to a year, and you collect a 2.5% fall in global demand — so how much is left of the argument, the one being had in the US right now, for driving a transition by keeping oil and gas supply off the market?

## Diesel reaching a $200 crude equivalent, and demand still barely moved

Answering that starts with using the right ruler. Tim says that if you're measuring oil markets by the crude price, you're looking in the wrong place. In October, excluding Iran, Gulf crude exports were back to around 90% of pre-conflict levels — on bypass routes available to the Saudis and the UAE, plus US protection, ship-to-ship transfers and other risk mitigation.

Products are a different story. On the same basis, products sit closer to 50%. And what consumers buy is products. Jason converts diesel into its crude equivalent and gets $170, $180, running toward $200; Tim says that's right. Russia is pushing the same way: Ukrainian attacks on Russian oil infrastructure have refinery runs down 30% from last year, with Russian diesel exports falling from north of 800,000 barrels a day to closer to 200,000. The refineries hit were mostly the big export-oriented ones, so even with a resolution they take time to bring back.

![Two bars compare how far the recovery has gone: crude is back to nearly full at ninety percent, products only reach half, and an arrow beside them points up to mark the diesel-equivalent price heading toward two hundred dollars.](/figures/crude-vs-products-recovery-en.svg)

Then there's diesel's own character. Tim calls it the most price-insensitive of the oil products, because a modern economy runs on it: goods move on it, extractive industries and agriculture depend on it. So it takes very high prices to bring diesel consumption down, and consumption is now falling in some parts of the world — a signal that economic activity is contracting.

That gives 2.5% two layers of explanation, and you need both. On supply, stocks, bypass routes and American barrels filled a large part of the hole. On demand, the hole sits in the one product that won't compress. Put them together and the explanation "prices weren't high enough to get a response" doesn't survive — diesel's equivalent price already ran toward $200.

## What actually took 1.5 million barrels out was started a decade ago

So what did change demand? Tim's example is Chinese electrification: cars, and also trucks, buses, two- and three-wheelers. In their estimate that took 1.5 million barrels a day off China's 2026 oil demand. He adds that this is not something you can call on in an emergency; it accumulated over years and now eases strain on the Chinese system.

That's the comparison I find decisive. What the crisis delivered in months was 2.5% off global demand; what one country's decade of structural choices delivered on its own was 1.5 million barrels a day. Now look at the new Additional Response Case in this year's Outlook — built on what governments have announced or are visibly considering because of this crisis. By 2035, oil imports run about 5 million barrels a day below the stated policies scenario, which is also about 5 million below 2025, and gas demand takes a 300 billion cubic metre hit. Tim says you can draw a direct line from the shock to both, because both come from countries trying to cut their reliance on imports.

![A very short timeline on top stands for a crisis lasting months and ends in a small block, while a much longer timeline below stands for a decade of electrification and ends in a clearly larger block.](/figures/crisis-months-vs-structure-decade-en.svg)

So the crisis works on choices that pay off a decade out, and the size of those choices is set by how hard policy pushes and how long the disruption lasts. He gives two examples: Thailand revised its long-term power development plan this year, with less gas than the previous version, a bigger push on renewables and longer-term hopes for nuclear — already in official planning, so it goes into stated policies. Indonesia's announced 100-gigawatt solar target isn't part of its formal power planning yet, so that kind of clear intent goes into the Additional Response Case. His own summary: the longer this lasts, the deeper the market and policy responses get.

Lay the three scenarios' emissions side by side and it gets plainer. Jason reads out the numbers: 2035 emissions roughly flat or slightly up under current policies, 6% lower under stated policies, 9% lower in the Additional Response Case. Tim says that's right, and adds that in fuel-importing countries the alignment between security and transition objectives has never been stronger.

## Where this conclusion stops holding

The first limit is who gets to decide. Tim puts fuel-importing countries at around 60% of global emissions, so their preferences can't determine the global trajectory on their own.

The second limit runs against intuition: a security preference doesn't automatically favour low emissions. A bias toward homegrown energy helps renewables and nuclear first, and it also sends countries back to look at domestic fossil resources — and what the worst-hit Asian importers have at home is coal. So in the Additional Response Case, oil and gas both come in below stated policies while coal sits slightly higher, which Tim says reverses the normal order of things. His read on coal goes further: in a world worried about energy security, you don't want to move assets out of the system that could be useful in an emergency, so coal sticks around longer than many expect. The global trajectory still turns on China, at 55% of world coal demand; their view is that the Chinese renewables and nuclear build-out is strong enough to shift coal's position inside the Chinese system, from bedrock to a flexibility play, which pushes the global number into decline.

![Three arrows sit side by side, with oil and natural gas pointing down while coal points up, showing how a preference for security flips the usual order.](/figures/security-flips-the-order-en.svg)

The third limit is money. Tim says the crisis pushes importers toward a more electrified system, and that's capital-intensive: you pay more upfront and recover later on fuel costs you no longer have. In a world of higher interest rates and cost of capital, that choice gets harder to make.

Writing this I thought of the question readers ask most often: the news says this energy crisis will accelerate the transition — how much of that should I believe? The episode hands you a usable answer: check which layer the claim is about. If it's prices and short-term behaviour, 2.5% is the order of magnitude, so expect no more. If it's official plans, power development planning and national targets being rewritten, something is moving — then go find which document it was written into (Thailand, written into the plan, and Indonesia, announced, are two different objects in the IEA's own taxonomy). The mistake I've made sits in between: seeing a price spike and concluding the structure was changing, then watching prices fall back with nothing left behind. Now I ask whether the change got written into a plan someone has to execute for ten years.

## A few other things worth keeping

- **The LNG wave is a timing problem.** Around 350 billion cubic metres of annualised LNG export capacity growth is already approved for the next few years, adding about 50% to the total LNG market. Tim says the last big wave was absorbed by Europe (switching off Russian pipeline gas) and China; this one needs Asia outside China — India and Southeast Asia. But about half of those new markets have to build re-gas terminals, build pipelines and find the customers first, and current uncertainty makes those decisions hard to move along. Delay the decisions and the surplus grows, and the wave may pass some countries by.
- **Cooling drives global electricity demand, not only AI.** Tim says 85% of emerging and developing economies outside China face high and rising temperatures, while only 20% of households have air conditioning. In their projections, about ten years out, cooling is 12% of total electricity demand and something like 30% of peak demand. Heatwaves put the strain on the system right there.
- **Put the AI numbers back in proportion.** Data centre electricity demand is around 500 terawatt hours now (half of Japan) and goes to 1,000 terawatt hours (Japan's current consumption), moving from about 1.5% of global electricity demand to close to 3%. Tim notes 80% of data centres built between now and 2030 are in the US, China or Europe, so don't generalise a few jurisdictions' debates to the world.
- **The forgotten fuel is still the cheapest one.** Over the last decade, efficiency gains in home appliances and white goods saved about 600 terawatt hours of electricity a year — more than all the world's data centres consume today.
- **If you need it fast, the menu is short.** Order a new gas turbine today, Tim says, and you'll be quoted delivery in the early 2030s; grid transformers are similar. To mobilise new power quickly, what can be delivered is solar plus batteries with some wind. That's one reason they see robust growth in those technologies in the United States too.
- **Low-emissions fuels run on a second clock.** Low-emissions generation (renewables around 33%, 42% with nuclear) could reach 60% by 2035; low-emissions fuels are about 8% of total supply now and look set for 10% by 2035. The 2030 forecast for low-emissions hydrogen keeps getting dialled back — 10 million tonnes was last year's estimate, now it's closer to 6 million. Nuclear goes the other way: 90 gigawatts under construction, the most in four decades.

## Further reading

- Columbia Energy Exchange, 24 November 2026, Jason Bordoff with Tim Gould, produced by the Center on Global Energy Policy at Columbia University
- The 2026 World Energy Outlook itself (500-plus pages), where the scenario definitions and country-level numbers live; the summaries don't carry those numbers
- The IEA's annual Global Hydrogen Review, for the path from a 10 million tonne estimate down to roughly 6 million
- The IEA's renewables report, for why global additions came in below last year and how China's new business model exposes project developers to more merchant risk
- Thailand's revised long-term power development plan and Indonesia's 100-gigawatt solar target, as a way to tell "written into the plan" apart from "announced"

## The one thing to take away

**The number you watch is often not the thing you care about.** Crude and products in this episode are the specimen: crude exports back to 90% of pre-conflict levels looks like the crisis easing, while the products consumers actually buy sit at 50%, diesel's equivalent price runs toward $200, and diesel consumption starts falling in places. Anyone watching only the crude price would think it was over. Tim's closing line — if anyone comes to you saying there's a single answer to affordability or security or decarbonisation, be skeptical — rests on the same thing: one indicator makes you miss the other moving parts.

Here's something I've tried. Take a number you check on a schedule, write it down, and write a second line beside it: what I actually care about. Weight and the thing you care about — stamina, blood pressure, whether your clothes fit — are two different objects; a kid's grades and whether they sleep at night and still tell you about school are two different objects. If the two lines don't match up, go find a second number and watch both for a month.
