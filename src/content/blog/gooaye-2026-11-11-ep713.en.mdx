In EP713 of Gooaye, published 11 November 2026, host Hsieh Meng-kung spends half the episode on one news story about Nvidia's CEO, and he handles it by sizing what he calls the radius of damage. Consensus, he says, puts roughly 10–20% of Nvidia revenue as eventually reaching China, and he expects the company to cut the grey portion itself while regulators are watching, which could leave guidance short of the high end of estimates. The heavier weight sits elsewhere: foreign brokers have started saying, in careful language, that one-to-two years of AI server demand is being pushed out by about 20% — deferred, not cancelled. He put his own deepest drawdown this round near 17%, and July's near 30%. All of it is spoken estimate and market chatter from that day, with no filings behind it.

![Two downward bars compare the drawdown depths, showing that July's roughly 30% drop was nearly twice as deep as the current roughly 17% drop.](/figures/gooaye-713-drawdown-depth-en.svg)

## His case: size the radius first, then wait for the market to speak

The strongest stretch of the episode is how he takes the story apart. He asks three questions in order: does the export channel get cut, does the component shortlist change hands, do the products and the order book change?

On the first question his answer carries a counterintuitive observation. The instinctive script says the CEO, now exposed, stops lobbying for China sales, and the US–China break becomes clean. His version is that the cut has been underway for a while, and that a year or two ago Washington was doing the cutting, while lately it is Beijing that prefers American product stay out — because once it does, domestic money floods into local chip design and integrated manufacturing and pulls their own schedule forward. Which means that inside China, the people arguing against letting Nvidia in openly have money riding on that position. One personnel story does not redirect something already moving.

The second layer is the supply chain. He thinks the "great reshuffle" Taiwanese investors keep talking about is unlikely, and his reason is concrete: the people who chase components and decide which part goes into a server are the managers and buyers below the CEO. A wholesale change in component preferences requires those managers to be replaced — new people, new tastes. As long as effective control stays put, the vendor list does not reorder itself over a headline.

The third layer he handles in a sentence: a founder's private news does not change product performance or order guidance. With the radius measured, the story goes into his book as a little pessimistic colour on sentiment, carrying less weight than the server deferral. His own phrasing: knocking out the numbers layer by layer beats telling stories about gossip.

![A two-row, three-box comparison shows only the rightmost box filled in the top row and all three boxes filled in the bottom row, revealing the difference in weight between the two news items.](/figures/gooaye-713-three-boxes-en.svg)

Only after sizing does he talk about acting, and here he says the part most listeners dislike. Asked how he spots the next leading group early, his answer is that before a thing goes up, you do not know it will go up. He switches to an e-commerce analogy — how would you pick the next hot product out of everything listed on Shopee? You would not; whoever guesses right once goes online and claims a method. Then he reclaims the phrase "chasing highs": get on once something has started running, and if you get on fast enough, there is still meat on it.

Two filters sit underneath. The first is the market speaking, applied per stock through the position of the lows: names whose November low held above their July low have a better chance of staying strong; names that printed around the same level and snapped back are still watchable; names that went below July may have finished a leg. The second filter is the list written beforehand. His example: twenty names he has picked as good, ten of which also look cheap. If one of those ten starts running and he has to pay the limit-up price, he pays it, because on his radar it is still cheap and somebody is finally willing to buy it. His line about rating agencies landed for me — people who complain that Michelin grades badly are doing the same grading job, without Michelin's credibility. Respecting how the market votes means admitting your favourite name may not be the one the big money wants.

![A horizontal dashed line marks the July low, while the bottoms of three price paths fall above, on, and below it.](/figures/gooaye-713-low-filter-en.svg)

## The other side: where these three layers break

The first cut lands on the lows filter. To use it you have to wait for the November low to form and for the bounce to develop before you can see who held July. Yet in the same episode he says you have to wait until the correction ends and watch who makes new highs before you can identify where the money went. Read together, the filter prints a reading later than the moment you need the decision. During the days when you are still guessing whether this bounce clears the quarterly moving average, it is blank. He also owns that Wednesday's dip-buy was "wrong in outcome, right in judgement" — separating outcome from judgement holds up methodologically when the invalidation conditions were written before the trade, and no such pre-written card appears in the episode, which leaves both readings defensible after the fact.

![On a timeline, the point when a decision must be made comes before the filter produces a reading, leaving a long blank interval between them.](/figures/gooaye-713-filter-lag-en.svg)

The second cut lands on the cheap list. That layer is the airbag: paying up at limit-up stops being a gamble because the name still has valuation room. In the same episode he hands you the reason the airbag could deflate — he calls this the stage where revenue has to cash the story's cheque, says no story in the market is unknown to anybody, and the difference is how much revenue the story actually produces, with the laggards getting taught a lesson. Cheapness is computed off estimated revenue; estimates get cut during the cashing stage, the cheapness quietly leaves the list, and the speed rule — it's moving, I'm buying — does not pause to wait for the valuation to refresh. The two update at different rates, and the gap is the risk.

The third cut lands on who can copy this, which matters most to a reader. He says he has to move early when adjusting positions, because liquidity in some Taiwan names cannot absorb him exiting at once; avoiding price impact means feeding it out slowly, and some things, he says, would take a year to clear. His "reduce early" is therefore a behaviour imposed by size. A retail account exits with one click, so copying "move early" becomes an unprompted early sell, right into the stretch he can still sit through. On the other side, his momentum buying has research depth and diversification underneath it; he says his hit rate is higher because he watches closely and is hard to con. Copy the first half of that sentence without the second and what remains is buying limit-ups.

![Two lines diverge from the same point, with the share price rising and valuation headroom falling, while the opening at the right represents risk.](/figures/gooaye-713-two-clocks-en.svg)

One inference chain deserves a label. The 10–20% China contribution is a market-estimated range, and he says himself that nobody knows the real number, because some units ship to third parties and slip in, or local operators rent the compute. Going from that guessed range to "the company will tighten and guidance will miss the high end" stacks an inference on an inference. I accept the direction; I would not size a position off the figure.

## My read: the method sorts people by size and by list

With both sides on the table, I think his case stands, under narrower conditions than he states: the list has to exist before the move, and the position has to be small enough that your own entries and exits do not move the price. Whoever satisfies both can copy the speed half safely. Whoever satisfies only the second and has no list copies nothing but chasing.

That answers the question a lot of people carry around: why does bad news sink some stocks and leave others alone. The tool this episode offers travels well — three boxes. Does this news change cash flow, does it change who places the orders, does it only change the mood? Run the Nvidia story through it: first box empty, second box empty, third box holds something, which is exactly why it is worth a dab of pessimism in his book. Run the server deferral through it the same week and the first two boxes fill, so it belongs ahead. Most people sort news by vividness and then adjust their positions against the loudest item, and vividness has nothing to do with cash flow.

![On a two-axis plane, two news items occupy opposite corners: the sensational item sits at the lower right and the cash-flow item at the upper left, showing that the two ranking methods conflict.](/figures/gooaye-713-vivid-vs-cashflow-en.svg)

"I'm underwater, do I cut or hold" gets a translation here too. His trimming hangs off liquidity; yours should hang off the list — is the name still on the cheap list you wrote, and have the reasons behind it already been revised away? Translated into retail language, "move early" means write the rules early and let the rules decide the exit.

The most interesting inference I saved for last, because it shows how to find the other side of a story. Talking with friends, he notes that if AI takes a short intermission, the casualties are the shortage-and-price-hike names, and the beneficiaries are procurement teams worldwide — for two years they have been chased by customer demand to the point of exhaustion, with every supplier raising prices and warning that not buying now means not buying later. Once the tempo slows, buyers finally get to sit down to a meal, and then they have time to meet new suppliers. That is where second-tier supply-chain opportunity comes from. Hearing it, I thought the question generalises to any bad news: who gains time, budget, or a meeting they could not get before.

One more passage stayed with me. He describes a photo from an industry event years ago where his wife spotted a small gesture and declared on the spot that the company would run into trouble; he piled up counterevidence against her at the time and later conceded she is often right. His own self-assessment is blunter — without her, carried away by fame, he might have done something similar himself. The criterion she used barely looks like company analysis: someone who can betray the person closest to him can betray anyone. I would not run a screen on that. It does remind me that a criterion simple enough to apply in three seconds survives a room full of hype better than a framework that takes half an hour to compute.

## One Thing to Take With You

**Fast hands get paid out of a list; with no list written, speed only scales the mistake.**

Every thread in this episode loops back to the same place. He can pay limit-up because those ten names were priced as cheap last week. He can call a headline worth a dab of pessimism because he measured the three layers that day. He trims before most people because he has long known what his liquidity allows. It looks like fast reactions; underneath, the homework was already done and the reaction merely spends it.

Here is something I have tried that works outside markets too. Take one decision coming up in the next few weeks — a job change, a move, whether to finally say the thing to someone. On paper write two columns: three entries under "if this appears, I say yes," three under "if this appears, I step back." Make them specific enough that a stranger could judge them — write "they propose a timeline themselves," not "they seem warmer." Then put the paper away, and on the day it happens, take it out and read it.
