Over October 12 and 13, 2026, I reshot our lab short film *Daijoubu* from scratch with MiniMax H3, a model that turns a still photo into a video clip with sound, running on a Google Colab A100. Before shooting, I tested three quality settings on two shots. Rendering directly at full size took about 590 seconds per clip; rendering small and upscaling took 245 seconds and looked sharper. The setting I picked, small-then-upscale plus a detail pass with SeedVR2, took about 340 seconds. The finished film is 74.7 seconds, 1080×1920 vertical, made of 13 video clips and one still card. All timings are for the 80GB A100 and clips of roughly five seconds.

## Why nobody could follow the first cut

The feedback on the first version was blunt: people had no idea what was going on. When I went back through it shot by shot, I found four reasons, and image quality wasn't one of them.

The three characters lived in three places and never visibly connected. They never shared a frame, there were no text messages on screen, and we never saw the card change hands. The props the story depended on (the card, the flowers, the stage) never appeared at all; the subtitles had to carry them. All 15 shots were a single face changing expression, and at three seconds a shot, viewers lost track of who they were looking at. And the title, Japanese for "it's okay," only got explained at the very end, so for most of the film the word meant nothing to anyone.

I'd assumed that three photos plus some camera moves would give me enough variety. It turns out pretty pictures don't help much if the viewer can't tell who wants what and what's in the way.

## What changed in the second cut

I threw out the script. The story now runs through one person, Shen Wan, who writes novels. Her friend mio is going on stage for the first time tonight, and their friend Lin Yun has handed Shen Wan the job of writing the good-luck card. She spends half an hour stuck, and what she finally writes is the phrase mio always says to herself after a fall. The other two characters come in through text messages, objects, and shots from behind, with no more than one visible face in any frame.

**Every shot gets its own starting photo.** The first cut recycled three photos. This time each shot got a new one with its own angle, action, and framing. I generated them on my home machine, three candidates per shot, 42 in total, and went through them one by one checking faces, fingers, and backgrounds. Plenty got rejected. One had a second person reflected in the mirror, one had sprouted black wings, and all three candidates for the stage shot came out as a cinema screen, so I rewrote that prompt and tried again.

**Props go on screen.** The blank card, the crumpled drafts, the flowers, the backstage mirror, and the stage lights each get a shot of their own. In shot 5, mio trips at dance practice and says "daijoubu" out loud with the meaning shown beside it, so by the end the phrase pays off without needing any explanation.

**Text on the card is added in post.** H3 turns lettering into gibberish, so every card in the starting photos is blank and every phone screen is dark. Shot 11 skips the video model entirely: a photo of an open blank card, enlarged, with handwritten-style text laid over it in post, followed by a slow 3.5-second push-in.

Two small prompt lessons carried over. In the first cut, one shot came out with what looked like a fake subtitle line along the bottom; the cause was putting framing words like "close-up" right after the scene description, so this time I kept them apart. And because this setup has no negative prompt, writing "no text" can actually draw text. I describe only what I want, as in "the card is blank."

## Three quality settings, tested on two shots

My first guess was that the first cut looked soft because I'd used a pruned, compressed model at a native short side of 768 pixels. The official documentation says otherwise: its own examples use the same compressed model at the same native size. I was already using what the official examples use, so any fix had to come from how I rendered.

So I picked shot 8 (a rain-wet street at night) and shot 10 (mio reading the card) and rendered each three ways, 124 frames, about five seconds per clip. All three settings dropped the four-step speed-up add-on the first cut used and ran the full 30 steps.

- **A**: render directly at 768×1344 for 30 steps.
- **B**: render at 384×672 for 30 steps, double the size inside the model, then redo the last 10 steps at 768×1344. The ready-made pipeline I started from redoes only 2; I went with 10 to see whether more detail would grow back.
- **C**: take B's output and run it through SeedVR2, an open-source model built for upscaling video and restoring detail, enlarging it 1.5× to 1152×2016.

| Setting | Output size | Shot 10 (s) | Shot 8 (s) |
|---|---|---|---|
| A, full size directly | 768×1344 | 590 | 595 |
| B, small then upscale | 768×1344 | 245 | 245 |
| C, B plus SeedVR2 | 1152×2016 | 340 | 350 |

All six clips together cost 17.40 compute units.

I went in thinking A was the "proper" method and would look best. Side by side, A was slow but native, B was much faster, and C was the sharpest. B surprised me most. Doing most of the steps at a small size cut the time by close to 60 percent and lost nothing I could see.

## Why I went with C

Watched side by side on a phone, C's faces and hair were visibly cleaner. It costs about 100 seconds more than B per clip, and in exchange the output is already 1152×2016, close to a full vertical phone screen, so post only needs a small resize to 1080×1920.

For a 13-clip film, the extra time still fit inside one night. For something with dozens of clips I'd probably pick B and spend the saved time reshooting the clips I didn't like.

## What the full shoot cost

Shots 8 and 10 came straight from the test as C versions, and the other 11 were new. Each new clip took about 275 to 360 seconds for the B stage and about 345 seconds for the C stage, 6,985 seconds of cloud generation in all, or roughly two hours. It started around midnight and finished a little after 2 a.m. Compute units went from 148.72 to 134.24, so the shoot cost 14.48.

Joined end to end with no subtitles and no card, the 13 clips ran 67 seconds. Post added the shot 11 card, the subtitles, and transitions. I only faded where the script calls for a break: into the flashback, back to the present, around the card, and at the end. Everything else is a hard cut. Shot 5 is the flashback, so it's warmed up with slightly darkened corners. The result is the 74.7-second film above.

## A first run that produced nothing

The quality test actually took two attempts. The first ran from about 4:30 to a little past 7 p.m., used 14.32 compute units, and produced zero clips.

The cause was mundane. One notebook cell checks that every required component is present and stops if anything is missing, so the GPU isn't wasted. A newer version of ComfyUI, an open-source tool for building image and video pipelines out of nodes, had changed the format of the list it reports. My check misread it, decided the upscaling model was missing, and stopped, exactly as designed. The GPU stayed on, though, and sat idle until Colab disconnected it. I had a watcher script looking for errors, and it never fired.

The second attempt hit two more errors, but I caught them faster. Across both runs that day I spent 31.72 compute units. Most of what I learned is about watching the run rather than the code itself:

- **Before trusting a watcher, feed it a known error and confirm it goes off.** Mine scanned the whole page, but Colab clears the output of cells that scroll out of view, and some errors render inside an embedded frame in the cell where page text can't reach them.
- **A red error marker only means something failed at some point.** Once I dismissed a real error as a leftover marker from an earlier run and lost another twenty minutes. To tell whether a run is stuck, check whether anything is still executing and whether the clip count is still going up.
- **Set up the automatic shutdown the moment you start, and run it detached from the chat session.** Background jobs have a time limit, and when it hits, the watcher dies with it and nobody turns the GPU off.
- **Check which card you got right after starting.** Both are called A100. My first machine was the 40GB version; I deleted it and requested again to get 80GB.

## Letting the characters speak

In the first cut I removed the original audio entirely. In shots where nobody had any lines, the model had made up strings that sounded like Chinese and meant nothing. This time I went the other way and wrote into the prompts that mio says "Daijoubu" in Japanese, so she had something real to say.

After generating, I ran speech recognition over the audio. Shots 5, 6, and 12 all came back with "daijoubu," and those are exactly the three shots where she says it. Shot 13 came back as a Japanese "thanks for watching," but that stretch is nearly silent; the recognizer was guessing at silence, and the model hadn't said anything. A transcript like that only means something next to the volume of the same stretch.

I kept the original audio at 0.55 of its level, put the music at 0.35, and normalized the whole film to -14 LUFS. Her voice sits under the music where you can hear it without fighting the subtitles.

Looking back, the model behaved the same both times. The difference was whether the prompt gave it a line. Give it one and it says it; leave a gap and it fills the gap itself.

## The one thing to take with you

**Before spending real money or real time, try the smallest possible version of a few options side by side.**

Had I shot all 13 clips with A, each would have taken over 200 seconds longer and come out softer. Two shots and about US$1.80 settled the direction for the whole film. None of this is specific to AI. Today, if you're trying a new soup, a new route to work, or a new bag of coffee beans, make the smallest batch you can, put two or three versions next to each other, and write down which one wins and why. Then decide whether to do it that way for real.
