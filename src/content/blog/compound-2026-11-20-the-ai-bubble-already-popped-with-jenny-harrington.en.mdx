On The Compound and Friends recorded 20 November 2026 — the day Anthropic listed — Josh Brown's first line is that the AI bubble popped back in July and we are five months into it, and Michael Batnick answers on the spot that the balloon has only let some air out. The guests are Jenny Harrington, CEO of Gilman Hill Asset Management, and Bryn Talkington, managing partner of Requisite Capital, and the two sides argue over one shared set of numbers: Dell and Micron halved, DRAM down 52% from its high, NVIDIA shedding more than 2 trillion dollars of market value in thirty days — while the Nasdaq sits 15% off its high and the equal-weight S&P was at a record the week before. The counter-exhibit that day is Anthropic itself: priced at 83 dollars, trading at 97 intraday — and when Michael presses Josh to tighten his wording and asks whether a stock priced at 83 would be at 97 if the bubble had burst, the answer across the table is no, it would be at 70 and a total flop. Where I land, and the condition it turns on, is in the last section.

## The case for: this is what a popped bubble looks like

Josh's argument has two layers. The first is price: Dell cut in half, Micron cut in half, DRAM down 52% from its high, the Nasdaq 15% off. He says this is simply what it looks like when a bubble pops, and that the statement doesn't require those stocks to have carried bubble valuations — some did, some didn't. The piece he called a bubble from the start was the neoclouds, the new cloud companies renting out GPU compute, and he never owned a single one of them. That piece popped.

![Four bars hang down from a zero line; the first three reach roughly waist depth while the last one is only a shallow stub, showing how widely the depth varies within the same selloff.](/figures/compound-1120-price-drawdowns-en.svg)

The second layer is sentiment, and someone at the table splits it in two: a sentiment bubble and an activity bubble. The sentiment one popped in plain view. Josh says there is a chart later in the show of assets in leveraged technology ETFs, going from something like 130 billion dollars to 50 billion as he remembers it — a complete wipeout. Micron's multiple went from seven times earnings to three; NVIDIA sits at twelve times forward earnings while the market is at twenty. Josh's reading is that there is no bubble valuation left at those levels — what broke was sentiment, and at that level of scepticism you cannot push stocks higher. NVIDIA's sheer size makes the point: it was bigger than Apple, the largest stock in the world, and it lost over 2 trillion dollars of market value in thirty days.

On the activity half, Jenny supplies the episode's most concrete objection. Anthropic, OpenAI and Gemini are all building Ferraris, she says, and most of the people who will use a language model need a Honda CR-V. Her example comes from her own family: her 76-year-old mother went to ChatGPT the day before to ask how many stamps to put on a square envelope. She suspects most people are using these tools in a way that doesn't pay for itself, while too much money goes into building the fastest and the best one. Bryn adds the enterprise-side version: her firm's interface defaults to auto-selecting a model, and in her business nobody is doing surgery — they need the right answer. Josh turns it into a routing problem: inside a complex task, most of the steps aren't complex, and to walk down the block for a quart of milk you don't need a Mercedes.

![Three bars rise from low to high, with memory shortest, chips in the middle and the whole market tallest, and a dashed line marking the market's height leaves both shorter bars below it.](/figures/compound-1120-multiples-below-market-en.svg)

Where that money is going shows up best in Jenny's own names. SanDisk, Seagate and Western Digital all sat in her dividend strategy within the past decade, and none of them do now. Western Digital was the hot stock from 1997 to 2000, then nothing happened for twenty-odd years, then it became a dividend stock, and then it turned into a momentum stock again. Her reason for buying memory coming out of the pandemic was that memory was priced as though it had no value. Watching the stocks of her youth become momentum names again, she says, was her favourite part of this cycle. Josh's name for this year's recipients is the Halo trade — companies with real cash flows selling real things to real people — and somebody launched an ETF for it this year. The historical version is on a chart in the same episode: in 2000, technology plus communication services fell 69% peak to trough while the rest of the market rose slightly, and the line at the time was "the Berkshire market is doing fine, the Intel market sucks." Jenny, keeping clear of the marketing rule on performance, talks only about broad dividend stocks: more than ten percentage points ahead of the market in the first quarter, about three ahead in the second, behind in the third, and roughly eight ahead now.

## The case against: a popped bubble doesn't stop here

Michael's first cut is definitional. Popping means everything goes all the way down — and by that definition, if the AI bubble popped, NVIDIA is heading for a 1 trillion dollar market cap. Bryn works through why that is hard: NVIDIA just took its total buyback authorisation to 350 billion dollars, about 9% of the market cap at the time, and if it were executed over the next few years, at a 1 trillion dollar valuation the company would be taking a third of itself private. She says the stock cannot really get there — and in the same breath makes her own stance clear: she is not calling a bottom, and she is telling people not to rush in and buy this dip.

![A large square on the left has only a thin sliver filled in at its base, while a much smaller square on the right is filled in by a third, so the same buyback accounts for a completely different share depending on the market cap.](/figures/compound-1120-buyback-share-en.svg)

The second cut is breadth. The Nasdaq is 15% off its high, the Magnificent Seven have relatively held in, and the equal-weight S&P was at an all-time high the week before. Michael uses this to knock down a claim that circulated for a year: that the whole market was being propped up by AI. In the days around the recording, technology and semis were getting killed while the index hung near record highs, which leaves that claim with nothing to stand on. On their chart, the spread between the average drawdown in tech and semis and the drawdown in the cap-weighted index has never been wider.

The hardest counter-evidence that day is Anthropic. The 81 billion dollars raised is the largest IPO in history; it priced at 83 dollars and traded at 97 intraday. Bryn's note cuts the other way: historically a company's first year public brings a drawdown of 30% to 50% at some point, and she says so while disclosing that she lent the stock and bought it herself that day. Harder still: inside one year the market absorbed the three largest IPOs ever — Anthropic, SpaceX, SK — and all three trade above their offer price. If you have been bearish, Michael says, that ought to make you uncomfortable. The demand figures are on the table too: customers spending six figures a year went from 1,500 in December 2025 to 11,900 by September 2026, and the company only started making money in March 2023, back when the live question was how any of this would be charged for.

![Both panels have a zero line: on the left, tech fell deeply back then while the rest of the market rose a little, and on the right, tech fell deeply this year while the index sat at a new high, so the shapes look alike but the gap on the right is larger.](/figures/compound-1120-depth-vs-breadth-en.svg)

Jenny's cut goes deeper: all year the market has been making distinctions. Micron's multiple never got past ten times earnings; NVIDIA stayed at twelve. When people asked whether 70% earnings growth didn't deserve more than that, her answer was that the scepticism never let up. Her disciplined growth strategy requires a free cash flow yield of 5% or better, which kept the memory names out and let only Meta through from the Magnificent Seven — she looked at NVIDIA and at Google over the years, and once she dug into the earnings she found risk ahead, or enough stock options outstanding to eat into future earnings. Circular financing, she points out, has been discussed for a year, and that doubt was already in the price. On this reading, the bubble never inflated, so there is nothing to pop; what the derating said is that the market doesn't believe those earnings are sustainable.

![Two bars stand side by side, the left one so short it almost hugs the baseline and the right one reaching the top, with the customer count up nearly eightfold in nine months.](/figures/compound-1120-customer-jump-en.svg)

Michael also catches a contradiction: Josh says the bubble popped, and bought Anthropic that day. Josh tightens it to "the sentiment bubble around data centres popped, the build is not over." And Bryn offers the other version of the good news: if the hyperscalers' cash flow stops bleeding and turns back up — they are around 38% of the broad market — then when Google's and Meta's free cash flow inflects, the market will say thank God that's over with.

## Where I land, and the condition it turns on

"My stock is down 30% — is this a bubble popping or a correction?" That was the question I actually brought to this episode, and what I noticed by the end is that both sides are reading the same numbers and disagreeing about the name. The name decides the next move: if it popped, what follows is the whole way down and you cut the direction; if air is coming out, what follows is a handoff and the work is counting where the money went. The way this episode tells them apart is by counting breadth rather than depth — NVIDIA lost 2 trillion dollars while the index hung near its high, and the spread between tech-and-semis drawdowns and the index is the widest on record. That test puts me on Michael's side, with a condition attached.

The condition sits in Jenny's method. The line she learned from Bruce Greenwald at Columbia Business School is that if you loved it at 50 you should love it more at 40, and she translates it into today's language: re-underwrite your investment thesis, and your starting point is always today. I like that sentence, because it removes "I am already stuck in this" from the judgement.

But in the same episode Josh points at where the method stops working, and Jenny doesn't argue. NVIDIA cannot be re-underwritten right now, because three questions have no answers. Did Jensen Huang or anyone else senior know that those chips were making their way into mainland China and being used by blacklisted companies — nobody knows. How much of the China revenue can still be counted: Jenny says you need to be able to say 20% of it is there, or 5%, and right now you cannot say either. How much of the moat was Jensen himself — she says you cannot quantify it. Josh adds the fourth: even with answers, which way the market reacts is unknowable.

So the test I took away is this: re-underwriting works only when you can answer the questions the thesis rests on. If you can, a 30% fall is a repricing and "your starting point is today" holds. If you cannot, rewriting the thesis is handing yourself a green light wrapped in a plausible story. The episode demonstrates both shapes — the reasoning Jenny gives for Cisco, that it doesn't matter who makes the chips or the memory because the traffic still has to run across the network, is the answerable kind; NVIDIA is the other kind.

![Three filled circles in the top row lead to "repriced," while four hollow circles in the bottom row lead to "admitting you are guessing," so you can tell at a glance whether the circles are full or empty.](/figures/compound-1120-answerable-questions-en.svg)

Jenny's own admission is the best part. She was long Intel and short the NVIDIA story, and she says what she missed on NVIDIA was how good the technology was, while what she missed on Intel was how much damage management could do to a company — unfathomable at the time. The good horse and the sound carriage are still there; a different hand on the reins is a different outcome, and the question mark that appears when the hand changes has no financial statement to check it against. That is the same argument the market is now having about Jensen's departure.

So what do I watch instead? The change I made after this episode is to write down the three questions my position depends on before I buy, and mark which one has news attached to it. Jenny's version is tougher: before she buys, she sits with her analysts and works out what Josh is going to break her chops about, and sometimes she posts the idea on X first to see where the pushback lands, so she can arrive with the answer. She owns up to her own contradiction too — she tells every client to look only at the monthly or quarterly statement, and then refreshes her own numbers minute to minute. I laughed at that, because so do I.

## The one thing worth taking with you

**Rewrite it from scratch, and see whether you can still answer the questions.**

An hour of argument leaves one thing standing: how many questions does the decision in front of you rest on, and can you answer them today? If you can, recalculate when the price changes and leave what you already paid out of the formula. If you cannot, the honest move is to say you are guessing, rather than to restate the old reasons in a new order.

Here's something I've tried that works outside investing too. Take something you decided long ago and are still doing — a job, a weekly commitment you keep showing up to, a relationship — and pretend you are meeting it for the first time today. On a sheet of paper write three questions whose wrong answer would have stopped you from choosing it, then mark each one: can answer, cannot answer, don't know. I did this once, and two of my three questions I had not checked since the day I decided.
