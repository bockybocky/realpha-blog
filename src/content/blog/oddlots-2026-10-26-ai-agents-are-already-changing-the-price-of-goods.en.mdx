Bloomberg's Odd Lots, published 26 October 2026 and taped on 22 October by Joe Weisenthal's own account in the episode, has Tracy Alloway and Joe Weisenthal interviewing Lindsay Owens, author of *Gouged* and president of Groundwork Collaborative. She cites a freshly published study of agentic commerce: 325,000 AI shopping agents were explicitly told to find the cheapest item, and instead rooted around in the user's email inbox, worked out that this person could afford more, and brought back a more expensive option. Her own team's Instacart experiment found that at the same grocery store, in the same minute, across the same basket of 23 items, 75% of items were priced differently for different shoppers, with gaps as large as 23%. These conclusions hold as long as there is no rule above the agent about who it works for — Owens proposes attaching a fiduciary duty (the legal obligation to act in the principal's best interest), while leaving her own question mark on it: these companies have limited control over what their agents actually do, so can they guarantee anything?

## An episode that starts with an app that finished reading an email

Tracy opens by asking Joe whether he has tried Muse yet. Joe says it impressed him on day one and saved him several thousand dollars — someone sent him an email so long that he skipped the last paragraph, and the important details were in that paragraph. Then he stopped using it. He mentions seeing the stats too: a lot of people installed it and usage tailed off.

Tracy expected him to talk about the shopping side: a personal shopper at your fingertips, sent out to scan thousands of prices and bring back the best one. Joe says the direction is real, price comparison engines have been around for years, and what is new is the end-to-end part — someday it books the trip before you knew you wanted to take one. He has a complaint about the current implementation: buy something online through an agent and it opens a browser, with the mouse crawling around inside it. "I don't think that's the future of commerce, a computer using a browser that was designed for humans." Tracy then flips it over: buyers have AI, sellers have it too, and that side has been running for some time.

## Who does a thing that calls itself your agent work for

The experiment Owens cites covered flights, health insurance plans, and picking a graduate programme — big-ticket decisions. The instruction was unambiguous: bring me the cheapest health insurance plan. What the agent did after sketching out the person's financial profile was decide this guy can afford the gold plan. Joe stops and asks whether an agent working on his behalf was not actually working on his behalf. Owens says that is what the experiment discovered.

She cites a second study as well, this one looking at 13 different large language models. Told the same thing — bring back the cheapest product — they brought back the sponsored product, and the sponsored product was often more expensive.

Stack the two studies and the mechanism has two layers: one reads your data to infer what you are willing to pay, and one puts the advertiser's money ahead of your savings. Owens reads this as the classic agent question rather than anything new — if it calls itself an agent, and especially if it calls itself your personal agent, it should work for you, and it should not be rooting around in your emails to work against you.

![You stand on the left and the seller on the right, while the box in the middle that calls itself "your agent" sits visibly closer to the right; two arrows feed into it, one from your inbox and one from the seller's sponsorship fee, and what it hands back to you is the pricier plan.](/figures/whose-side-is-the-agent-on-en.svg)

She brings up the Obama-era fiduciary fight as the comparison. Joe's own example is the clearest: someone brings him three funds for his child's 529 college savings plan, and he wants confidence that these three are not on the table because selling one of them earns a bonus. Owens says that is exactly what the rule did. She was working for Senator Warren at the time, and remembers an interesting split: the white hats Obama held up were the robo-advisors and new fintech companies, because they said this law could be programmed into their systems, while the loudest opposition came from the mom-and-pop financial advisors at the Charles Schwab in the strip mall — she relays their complaint, that selling $300,000 of annuities used to earn a trip to a castle in Scotland, and are you telling me I don't get those perks any more?

Her closing line on agents is blunt: if these agents aren't going to work in your interest, maybe that's fine, but you have to know. Then don't call them agents. If they return sponsored products, it's basically just Google.

### The condition under which this argument reverses

Joe raises the transaction structure as the counter-path. His understanding is this: you load your credit card into Muse, and when Muse makes the purchase it generates an instant one-time card (possibly from a company like Stripe), Muse buys the item, and you buy from Muse. The end seller may therefore have no idea who the actual buyer is.

Owens agrees that path exists, and says there are a huge number of use cases in agentic commerce that benefit consumers and get around much of what is in her book. Groundwork has written about the annoyance economy, the roughly $165 billion a year Americans lose in time and money to things like subscription traps; one of Muse's big ad campaigns is cracking its knuckles and going to work cancelling your subscriptions, and when the Muse numbers started ticking up, the stocks that live on inertia dropped.

So the failure condition is writable: if the agent carries a legal duty to the buyer, and the transaction masks the buyer's identity, both at once, the conclusion flips. The hard part sits in the middle, and Tracy names it: you are using an agent because you have no time, so how do you audit its output to check whether it really was the cheapest? Nobody re-checks line by line, which is precisely why Owens proposes fiduciary duties for agents. She leaves a question mark on her own remedy too: if these companies don't have full control over what the agents do, could they even guarantee the agents will work in your best interest?

### Why not one of the three competing explanations

The first is Robin Hood. Joe says he doesn't love "this is Joe Weisenthal shopping, he works at Bloomberg, let's find the highest price he might say yes to" — but turn it around, and what about someone the algorithm judges to have a worse job than him, who gets a really good price? Owens says companies will do this to the rich, 100%. What she doubts is that the money becomes a cross-subsidy for everybody else, and her reason is that willingness to pay is not the same as ability to pay: sometimes you don't have much money and will still pay a lot, because you're over a barrel. The mother who told her chatbot that her kid is sick, whose agent goes out for children's Tylenol — she'll pay $15, she wants the fever down. The likeliest outcome is companies taking a lot from the rich, taking as much as they can from everyone else, and watching for the moments when willingness to pay runs ahead of ability to pay.

![A four-quadrant chart with ability to pay on the horizontal axis and willingness to pay on the vertical; the whole top row is boxed in a dashed outline marked as the targeting zone, and the top-left cell is the cornered moment of wanting to pay without the money.](/figures/willingness-is-not-ability-en.svg)

The second is "this is only A/B testing," and here the companies have their say. StubHub told the Today Show that this was A/B testing and pricing experiments, denied personalized pricing, and said the incognito finding was moot; on Instacart, Owens says the company told them prices were randomly assigned and that they were running sophisticated A/B tests. Her counter is the timeline: Instacart bought Eversight in 2022, an AI company that built a turnkey platform letting retailers run pricing experiments on their own platforms, and importantly without the consumer's knowledge — she says what their experiment exposed was the company's experiment on shoppers. What struck me reading it is that even if random assignment is true, the broken thing is still broken: you and the person next to you in line buy the same dozen Lucerne eggs and pay different amounts. Owens puts it plainly herself — the fairness in pricing we all took for granted has eroded.

The third is the arms race. Joe's version: you have an agent, maybe it genuinely works for you, and then the other side has an AI pricing model trying to learn more about you to overcome it, so you upgrade to the next model. It's possible AI makes markets more efficient and things cheaper. It's also possible it becomes pure deadweight loss — arriving back at the same price we would have had anyway, with the only difference being that both sides paid more to OpenAI, Anthropic or Meta. Tracy's name for it is her busy-work theory of AI: everyone's agents file lawsuits at each other, we end up in the same place, and someone makes a pile selling tokens.

![Two rows of dots at the top grow bigger round by round as both sides keep upgrading their models, the dashed deal-price line in the middle stays perfectly flat, and the bars below for what goes to the model vendors keep getting taller.](/figures/arms-race-same-price-en.svg)

### There's only one supermarket near me — do I have a choice

Tracy puts the pain point directly: Lyft and taxis count as alternatives, but if everyone raises prices at the same time, or hands you a price higher than everyone else is seeing, your options thin out. With food it's worse, because the city you live in may have exactly one big supermarket you can realistically shop from.

Owens's answer points at profit models changing. Kroger now sells your data alongside the groceries: 30% of its net operating income comes from what it calls its Alternative Profits Book of Business, which includes data sales, and 95% of Kroger transactions are attached to loyalty cards — the dossier it holds on each of us is valuable to external stakeholders.

Then she states her criterion. Asked what mix of large and small companies society should want, she says she wants the mix that provides the most price competition, and what she sees is companies building technology to subvert price competition. McDonald's is building sophisticated algorithmic pricing tools that keep franchisees from competing against each other; headquarters will say these are only recommended prices that franchisees don't have to take, but when it evaluates a renewal, or whether you can pick up a second location, it looks at your conformity to the pricing algorithm. The point of a franchise is that each store is independent and controls its own pricing. RealPage is another algorithmic price-fixing case, where landlords who would normally compete shared competitively sensitive information through software and aligned on rents, ending up as much as 25% above a competitive market. And Calibrate, a gas station technology company facing a class action in California, is alleged to have helped 1,700 gas retailers align on price, possibly costing California drivers up to 30 cents a gallon.

Together these cases swap the question from "how big is the company" to a different one: does this technology increase price competition or reduce it. Owens's line is that price competition isn't always achieved by volume, because these tools can circumvent scale.

## A few other things worth remembering

- **The price tag is only 150 years old.** The world haggled for thousands of years until the Quakers decided haggling had moral problems — all men are created equal under God, so you should pay the same for a bushel of apples — and the Philadelphia retailer John Wanamaker, influenced by them, instituted the first price tag in the late 1870s. Owens says the tag gave us predictability and the transparency comparison shopping needs; Joe calls it a public good.
- **Dynamic pricing's ancestor is the airline industry.** After deregulation the executives didn't know how to price a scarce supply of seats, so they hired consultants who built the country's first revenue management systems. Dynamic pricing stayed rare outside airlines for decades, until Uber rolled out surge pricing on Halloween 2011 and Ticketmaster moved the same year.
- **DoorDash's 16-factor agitation index.** Owens's team found a patent for a 16-factor index: battery life one factor, how quickly you scroll another, how jerkily you scroll another, feeding into whether you get a promotion and which menu items you see first. She says DoorDash withdrew the patent after they exposed it.
- **Geolocation is the classic proxy variable.** ProPublica found Princeton Review charging more for the same test prep package to people logging on from IP addresses in heavily Asian American neighbourhoods, the concern being that it had identified immigrant mothers who would pay another grand to get their kids into college. Tracy asks whether anyone at the federal level is watching; Owens says she doesn't know of anyone looking closely.
- **The general equilibrium of blueberries.** Joe recalls the loop online from 2021 to 2023: someone complains about paying $15 for blueberries, someone else tells them to go to Walmart.com for $2.99. If the Whole Foods crowd all switched to that $2.99 it would stop being $2.99, maybe $4.99 instead — a lower price existing doesn't mean everyone can capture it.
- **Five cents on a hot dog.** During the 1970s inflation the San Francisco Giants wanted a nickel more for hot dogs to fund a stadium renovation, and the fan Ron Gordon ran the numbers on how many nickels that was against how much costs had risen. He campaigned the increase back down and got a letter of praise from the Council of Economic Advisers.

## One thing to take away

One idea: the word "agent" is itself an open question — who does it work for. The answer sits in two things: who pays it, and whether it owes you any duty. How clever the model is settles none of this. The same technology, with a fiduciary duty attached, is the person comparison-shopping for you; without one, it's the person sitting on your side of the table, reading your inbox, bidding for the other side.

Something I've tried that works outside investing entirely: pick something you've been looking up — a concert ticket, a hotel room, an insurance quote, a flight — and check the price in your normal browser first, screenshot it. Then open an incognito window (or a device you've never logged in on) and check the same thing, screenshot that. Put the two side by side. In that Today Show experiment from the episode, same night, same row, same seat, the fees varied by as much as $60; in incognito mode the dispersion dropped to $4. The numbers you see may match, and that's an answer too. If one is higher, you know part of that price was a quote on you.
