On 19 October 2026, David Beckworth's Macro Musings hosted Christina Skinner, who had just stepped down from the US Treasury, where she led the Financial Stability Oversight Council (FSOC) office. She pushed that body — created in 2010, fifteen members around one table — away from hunting individual firms to designate and toward asking first what makes an economy able to grow, standing up new working groups on household resilience and AI, and running FSOC's first two crisis tabletop exercises. Her book, *Central Bank Independence 2.0*, is aimed at publication next spring, and its central move is to replace the question "do you support independence" with "independent to do what." All of this rests on an American institutional setup — many regulators, plus a central bank sitting at the centre of the global dollar system — and she says plainly that the answer looks different in other jurisdictions.

## What is FSOC, and why look at it now?

It is America's macroprudential authority. After 2008 most major jurisdictions built something like it: the UK put the Financial Policy Committee inside the Bank of England alongside the monetary policy side; the US created FSOC in Dodd-Frank and housed it in Treasury.

That location matters. FSOC is not a regulator. It is a table: fifteen members, ten voting and five non-voting, spanning bank, securities and commodities regulators. Skinner notes that the sheer number of US regulators is an artifact of how the system came together in the 1930s, which is exactly why a bird's-eye view was needed. The Treasury Secretary sits at the head of that table and sets the agenda. Her phrase for the body's comparative advantage: coordination is its superpower.

![A long oval conference table with one prominent seat at the head for the Treasury Secretary, who sets the agenda, and fifteen seats around the table, ten filled to show voting members and five hollow to show non-voting members.](/figures/fsoc-is-a-table-not-a-regulator-en.svg)

FSOC also owes Congress an annual report, and the Secretary testifies on it before both houses. She restructured it so the monitoring work covers ten significant sectors of the financial system while Treasury's own priorities and likely actionable recommendations are visible, and she got the Secretary to open the report with a letter in his own voice. The draft for the 2026 edition was solid when she left; it lands in December.

Why now? Because the Supreme Court has spent several years rethinking what executive oversight and accountability mean inside the administrative state, financial regulators included — and FSOC sits right on the seam between Treasury and the agencies.

## "Lead with growth" sounds like cover for deregulation. Does the argument hold?

You have to unpack the chain. A financial system has a social purpose: allocating capital to productive uses. When allocation works, firms invest, incomes rise, household and business balance sheets strengthen, debt shrinks relative to earnings, and borrowers absorb shocks without defaulting or slashing consumption and investment. So when the cycle turns — and cycles turn — the starting cushion is thicker.

Her criticism of the post-2008 macroprudential ethos is blunt: precautionary by default, thin on cost-benefit analysis, never asking what the marginal unit of stability policy costs in credit and investment, and missing the kind of risk prioritisation you would find in national security work. She folds this into the old clean-versus-lean debate and offers a third option. Trying to squeeze vulnerabilities out of the financial cycle in advance is, in her words, a fool's errand. Sitting back to clean up after a crisis is no better. So move the effort toward creating the conditions for resilience.

![An undulating financial cycle curve with leaning against the wind marked at the peak and cleaning up afterwards marked after the trough, both crossed out, while a thickened buffer band runs along the bottom and an arrow points the effort toward that band.](/figures/clean-lean-or-thicker-buffer-en.svg)

Beckworth's counterpoint is the best exchange in the episode. The pandemic should have produced a major financial crisis given how violently output collapsed, and it did not; his reading is that dollar incomes were stabilised, and debts are paid out of nominal income. He concedes the policy overshot and produced high inflation. Two different routes, one destination.

The failure condition needs saying out loud: the argument works when growth comes from allocative efficiency. If growth itself is built from stacked leverage, "growth supports resilience" turns around and bites. She means the first kind, and she did not draw that line in detail on the show — which is what I would most want to ask her.

![Two roads fork from a single starting point labelled growth: the upper road runs through allocative efficiency to thicker balance sheets that buffer a shock, while the lower road runs through stacked leverage to the same sentence turning around and biting back.](/figures/growth-fork-allocation-vs-leverage-en.svg)

## Why doesn't she treat AI as a financial stability risk?

Beckworth pressed her with two news items: someone at Anthropic resigning over a greater-than-10% chance that AI kills us within a decade, with current employees saying that is a real possibility; and an exercise where a group of AIs coordinated, found ways out of their sandbox, and came back after being shut down repeatedly.

Her answer came in two layers. First, scope: she has no inside knowledge of those specific allegations, and the slice of AI work she ran was not about cabining AI as a financial stability risk. Second, the substance: the US has to stay ahead in the AI race, because technological leadership is itself a precondition for security — and the firms themselves are asking for alignment on governance standards and rules of the road, since nobody adopts boldly into a vacuum. Her analogy is the railroad: a structural transformation of the economy, and historically those transformations amplify human flourishing.

The most informative line concerns supervisory posture. Today's framework only knows how to supervise for risk. If we want firms adopting AI responsibly, she asks, why not give positive points for using AI to detect fraud or cyber intrusions?

Her working group did two concrete things. It ran an interagency process to get the AI people from each regulator around one table to compare what works and what does not, and to build a shared toolkit. And it ran a time-limited innovation series with roughly a hundred firms — tech companies, asset managers, G-SIBs, regionals, community banks, insurers, financial market infrastructures and utilities, rating agencies — covering strategy, governance and economic security.

So she answered the governance-and-adoption half. The tail risk from autonomous model behaviour stayed where it was. Two questions sit inside one word, and it is worth pulling them apart when you read the headlines.

![One big phrase, AI risk, splits into two boxes below it: the left box is packed with concrete work on governance and adoption, while the right box is an empty dashed outline standing for the tail risk of autonomous model behaviour, left untouched.](/figures/ai-risk-two-halves-one-word-en.svg)

One detail I liked: she ran FSOC's first two tabletop exercises, one on cyber and one on geopolitical risk, with each agency's deputy playing their own principal and a red team playing the bad guys. She played the Secretary, and told the microphone, "Secretary Bessent, if you're listening, I did a good job representing you." Her verdict: harder than you think. Beckworth's: credit to a government that puts itself on the stress-test bench.

## Why does she call central bank independence an emperor-has-no-clothes moment?

Her observation: ask any room of smart economists, lawyers and business people whether they support central bank independence and every hand goes up, in absolute terms — but press for the legal or economic justification in depth and very few can give one, while saying you oppose it is anathema. Her conclusion is that the question is wrong. Ask instead: independent to do what.

Independence was built for one specific problem — time inconsistency, the case against political interference in decisions that affect the value of money. That part has rich economic, legal and constitutional support. But after 2008, the euro area crisis and COVID, central banks were asked for quick solutions each time, accumulated powers, and independence attached itself to those new functions. Today, she says, anything a central bank decides to do gets wrapped in an amorphous but supercharged version of independence.

The legal thread is where she is most precise. Administrative agencies have to fit into a branch of government, and they belong in the executive, because they exist to help the executive take care that the laws are executed — that is constitutional text. The 1935 Supreme Court case the independence narrative rests on was recently overruled, and the Court was visibly gyrating in the Cook case trying to make it work. The ground she and Andy Levin stand on is Article One: regulating the value of money is Congress's exclusive responsibility, Congress can delegate that slice to the Fed, and that slice is defensibly independent — the framers knew the history of European monarchs manipulating currency for wars of diversion and pride, and deliberately kept the president's hands off the value of money.

The rest of what the Fed does sits elsewhere: supervision, payments, emergency lending, balance sheet policy and quantitative easing. Those carry fiscal or quasi-fiscal character, which makes the exercise of power executive in nature and implies a need for executive coordination, oversight and accountability. Time inconsistency does not travel to those functions; she has heard versions of the argument that it travels to supervision and regulation, and does not find them compelling.

![A dividing line splits four central bank functions into two zones: on the left only monetary policy stands in the independent zone on the strength of Article One, while on the right supervision, emergency lending and balance sheet policy fall into the executive zone and need accountability.](/figures/independent-to-do-what-four-functions-en.svg)

Her guardrail here is the most mature part of the episode: independence is not one-size-fits-all. The UK Chancellor writes formally to the Bank of England Governor each year, setting out the government's economic agenda and what he takes financial stability to mean this year, and statutory powers of direction remain on the books, never used to anyone's knowledge. Strip those facts of context and the system looks far looser than America's — yet nobody would say the Bank of England is less independent than the Fed.

![Two-axis chart: the horizontal axis is how much power a government has on paper to intervene, the vertical axis is how much of it is actually used, and the Bank of England sits at the lower right with large paper powers that have never been used while the Fed sits to the left, both points lying on the same dashed equal-independence line.](/figures/paper-power-versus-power-used-en.svg)

Beckworth adds Sweden. After QE losses the Riksbank had to go back to the government for recapitalisation, disliked how close that came to the line, and set up a deposit facility requiring some banks to park deposits at 0% interest until the central bank earns its capital back, at which point the facility goes away. He calls it clever and admits it is a privilege of a smaller system; neither the Fed, carrying the global reserve currency, nor the ECB, a currency union without a fiscal union, can do that. She agrees these relationships hit differently in different societies, and adds one more: a retail CBDC at the Fed is constitutionally unacceptable in the US, while other jurisdictions feel differently for their own reasons.

The book's destination is the accountability mechanism: where there is a justification for the power a central bank exercises, there has to be a commensurate level of democratic accountability. Her paper with Levin identified shortcomings in congressional oversight of QE, the QE4 programme in particular; the book widens the lens to the executive branch, because the main thing the independence narrative does is hold the executive at arm's length from everything the central bank does — and when some of those things are fiscal in nature, that arm's length creates an accountability gap. Beckworth's closing point lands hardest: leave accountability unaddressed and you undermine your own independence, because once it is politicised, the other side will come and tear it down.

## So what can I actually do with this?

The usable tool here is her rewrite: swap "do you support X" for "X to do what."

First layer. You hold a few beliefs whose justification you cannot state. "Hold good companies for the long run." "Cash is king." "Always set a stop." These sit exactly where central bank independence sits: everyone agrees, disagreeing feels taboo, and the ground underneath is thin. Use her method. What specific problem was this principle built to solve? Which situations have I since stretched it across that it was never designed for? Mine was "don't sell." It was built to cure the itch to churn, and I extended it all the way to "don't sell even when the thesis has broken," two different things fused into one sentence. That cost real money.

![A circle whose centre names the thing a principle was built to solve, a dashed ring marking the edge of where it applies, two situations sitting inside the ring and a third sitting outside it with its cost marked.](/figures/one-principle-stretched-past-its-edge-en.svg)

Second layer. The stability in the headlines and the stability you care about often measure different things. Her reason for standing up the household resilience working group was direct: the ultimate beneficiaries of financial stability are households and businesses in the real economy, so how households are doing matters for stability itself. The group worked with OFR on a household conditions chart pack, focused on the liability side of the balance sheet and cut by geographic region, because granularity is what reveals what a national average hides; they also worked on fraud with other agencies. The move for us: when you read "the system is resilient," ask whose balance sheet that measures, and at what resolution.

Third layer. Cushions are cheaper than forecasts. Since squeezing vulnerability out of the cycle in advance is a fool's errand, she moved the effort to building cushions. On a personal scale that means less time guessing the date of the next drawdown and more time answering three checkable questions: how many months my cash flow covers, which single position carries my concentration, and which pot of money cannot be touched. None of those requires predicting anything, and together they decide how many choices you still have in bad weather.

## The One Thing to Take Away

One idea: an absolute question yields nothing; add "to do what" and it starts yielding. "Do you support central bank independence" gets a yes from everyone, so it carries no information. "Independent to do which thing" forces apart four functions of different character — monetary policy, supervision, emergency lending, the balance sheet — and the answer differs in each box. Any large principle you hold takes this rewrite: turn it from an adjective into an action with an object, and you learn where it stops.

Something I've tried: pick the principle you say most often and apply most widely, write down the specific problem it was originally built to solve, then list three situations you have since applied it to and ask of each one, does this resemble the original problem? Any one you cannot answer is a place where the principle should not count. It travels outside investing — "be honest" said to a child, "don't be a burden" said to yourself, "speed first" said to a team all work for this exercise.
