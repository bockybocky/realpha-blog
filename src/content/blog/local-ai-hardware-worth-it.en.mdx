> *The wren nesting in the deep forest needs no more than a single branch;*
> *the mole drinking at the river takes no more than a bellyful.*
> —— Zhuangzi, "Free and Easy Wandering" (c. 300 BCE); translation mine

Three videos went up this week answering the same question: if you want to run AI at home, what hardware should you buy.

The three answers contradict each other.

## Three answers

On August 21, the channel 小天fotos reviewed a mini PC called the Beelink SEi13 AI. His verdict was yes, worth it. Not because it's fast — because the accelerator card inside draws barely a dozen watts, so you can leave it running all day and forget about it.

The same day, 掄錘者 published a hands-on test of Qwen3.8 27B running locally. His verdict: buy two 7900 XTX graphics cards, one for text and one for video.

And on August 20, one day earlier, that same reviewer had said: "Generally I don't recommend buying those little mini PCs."

The little mini PC is exactly what the first reviewer recommended.

## They aren't arguing. They're measuring different things.

My first instinct was to work out who was wrong. Neither of them was. Both sets of numbers hold up, and they aren't measuring the same property.

The first reviewer measured whether you can leave it on. He kept coming back to the same figures: a dozen-odd watts, 40 degrees, a fan you can't hear. His strongest example was speech recognition — an hour of audio transcribed in two and a half minutes.

The second measured how fast it computes. He put the mini PC's compute at roughly the level of a 4060, with far less memory bandwidth, and described running dense models on it as "very slow, very painful."

Same box. One passes, one fails. The first question was "can this idle cheaply for months?" The second was "how long do I wait after I press go?"

That isn't a hardware question. It's a question about what you want it to do.

## An arithmetic problem

Two numbers sit next to each other in the second reviewer's video and they're more interesting together than apart.

Before the recent price rise, he says, cloud AI cost him one to two yuan a day. Now the same light workload runs five to six. One day he configured a script once and it cost him eight.

Then he recommends two graphics cards. The figure quoted is somewhere north of ten thousand yuan.

At six yuan a day, that's about a hundred and eighty a month. Paying for two cards out of that saving takes over five years.

We all know what a five-year-old graphics card looks like.

So what he's actually buying isn't performance and it isn't savings. He says as much himself: the price rise came without warning, and the cloud service went down for nearly an hour. He's buying insurance against somebody else's outage.

Insurance was never supposed to pay for itself. It's priced on how much the bad day costs you.

## I tried this myself once

Earlier this month I ran a local speech recognition model on my own machine, to see whether I could stop depending on a cloud service.

No dedicated graphics card — just the processor grinding through it. A ten-and-a-half minute recording took eighteen and a half minutes. Longer than the recording.

It also mangled the technical vocabulary badly: GGUF came out as GDUF, HuggingFace as HackingFast. The same audio sent to a cloud service came back faster and with the terms intact.

The conclusion was short: at my current volume, buying hardware means buying something I won't use.

That sentence has an edge to it worth noticing. It doesn't say local AI is useless. It says I transcribe a few recordings a month, and that volume can't justify a dedicated machine. The second reviewer's premise is daily heavy use. The first reviewer's premise is a box quietly collecting data around the clock. Change the premise and the answer changes with it.

## So what should you ask instead

All three videos work hard at answering "which configuration." None of them answers "do I need one."

That's not a failing on their part. By the time you click on a hardware review, you've usually already decided to buy.

But if you're still on the fence, "which configuration" is a decoy. It leads you into a maze of specifications, how many gigabytes and how many lanes and one card or two, and every one of those questions has a clean answer, and not one of them tells you whether to buy.

What actually decides it are three boring things: how many hours a day you'd really use it, what an outage genuinely costs you, and how much of your life you're willing to spend fiddling.

The third is the one people underestimate. On dual graphics cards, the second reviewer said something I thought was the most honest line in the video: "The moment you try to make them cooperate, you're in for endless fiddling." His measured result for two cards working together was around one plus one equals one point seven.

That missing zero point three is your weekend.

## One thing to take away

Specifications will answer the question you asked. They won't tell you whether it was the right question.

An exercise: think of the thing you've been meaning to buy — it doesn't have to be a computer, a course or a gym membership counts. Before comparing any specs, write down two numbers. How much money or time you spent on this activity last month, and what you actually lost on a day you didn't have the thing.

If you can't write the second number, what you're buying isn't a tool. It's reassurance.

Reassurance is a legitimate purchase. But once you know that's what's in the box, the price gets a lot easier to read.
