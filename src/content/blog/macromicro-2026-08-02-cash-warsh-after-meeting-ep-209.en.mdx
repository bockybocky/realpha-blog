> The best ruler is one whose existence is barely noticed. Next comes the one who is loved and praised; next, the one who is feared; next, the one who is despised.
> Where trust is insufficient, there will be no trust in return.
> He is careful, and holds his words precious. When the work is done and the task complete, the people all say: it happened naturally, of itself.
> — *Tao Te Ching*, Chapter 17

Laozi is describing four tiers of governance. The highest kind of ruler is one the people barely register; below that come the beloved, the feared, and the despised. And the mark of the highest kind is that he "holds his words precious" — he treats speech as expensive, so he uses very little of it. The work gets done, and everyone assumes it was simply the natural order of things.

Some two and a half thousand years later, a central bank chair is doing exactly that. He has voluntarily put down the single most powerful instrument he owns: the promise about what comes next.

## What the episode covers

Two subjects that look unrelated. By the end, they turn out to be two faces of the same coin.

The first is the Federal Reserve. The July meeting left rates unchanged and the statement almost untouched — on paper, a dull meeting. Yet the press conference still ran the full forty-five minutes, and what was said in it was different in kind from before. The new chair no longer tells the market what he intends to do. What he keeps returning to is this: without forward guidance, the market's signals are clean, and the Fed can read those signals back to judge how much of its own job is already done.

The second is Big Tech earnings. Revenue, cloud, advertising, backlog — the numbers were almost all excellent, and yet the share price reactions diverged sharply. The question the market is asking this quarter is no longer "how much did you earn," but "how much cash do you have left."

On one side, a central bank talking less so the data can speak. On the other, investors who have stopped listening to stories so that cash flow can speak. The episode title puts the two together, and it is the right pairing: **this is a season in which claims are being devalued and evidence is being repriced upward.**

## The main points

**On the Fed**

Rates, asset purchases, and the statement were all essentially unchanged. Everything of consequence was in the shift in how the Fed communicates. The single sharpest observation in the episode: the policy rate did not move, but the 10-year Treasury yield went from under 4.4% in late June to above 4.6% — **the market completed part of the tightening on its own**, which in turn bought the Fed more time to watch the data.

On inflation, the June report was a clean one. Core goods stayed in monthly decline as the tariff effect continued to fade; shelter inflation decelerated; and the component people had worried about most — services excluding shelter — finally flipped negative, ending a first half of steady warming. All three components cooled at once. That is why the Fed still had the confidence to hold even with oil prices rebounding.

The caveat: three voters already favoured a hike in July, and members who had leaned dovish have visibly drifted toward neutral-to-hawkish. The episode offers a line you can check your answer against — if core prices hold around 0.2% month over month (roughly 2.4% annualised), the disinflation trend can be considered intact; readings persistently above that line raise the odds of a hike materially. The next meeting is in September, and the report available by then will be August's — so energy prices and the Middle East over the coming month will do a lot of the deciding.

The rosters for the five reform working groups were also published. Reading fifteen members' past positions, the direction looks like this: **communication will shift from managing expectations to explaining the reasoning** — you will be told clearly what the reaction function is, but you will no longer be handed precise forecast points. As for the balance sheet group, the three members hold genuinely divergent views with no consensus in favour of shrinking it, which lowers rather than raises the odds of aggressive quantitative tightening.

**On Big Tech**

Core businesses were broadly strong: Microsoft's cloud revenue grew 43% year over year, Meta's advertising 27%, Google's advertising 15% with Google Cloud up 82%. Enterprise AI demand is converting into real orders — backlog at both Microsoft and Google rose by roughly $50 billion in a single quarter.

With that backing, capital spending went up again. Google raised this year's figure to around $200 billion; Meta lifted the low end of its range. Microsoft looks like a cut — from $190 billion down to $175 billion — but the reason is a ten-year extension of the depreciation schedule for data centres and office buildings, not a genuine reduction in investment; next year is still expected to rise. In aggregate, Big Tech capital spending is estimated at close to $800 billion this year, with a shot at $1 trillion next year.

The bill shows up in the cash flow line. Operating cash flow remains strong, but free cash flow is under pressure: Google and Tesla turned negative in the second quarter, and Meta came close to zero. The gap has to be filled externally, which is why the market has started worrying about a circular pattern — ever-larger debt raised to fund ever-larger AI capital spending.

So the narrative flipped completely within six months. Apple, penalised with a weaker multiple two quarters ago for moving slowly on AI, is now favoured precisely because it has not spent heavily. Microsoft, sitting on more cash, held up better after earnings than its peers.

The episode also answers the question of whether open-source models will puncture the bubble. Plot the inference cost of models at the same intelligence tier over time and you get a staircase descending steadily since late 2022 — open and closed source alike. Falling cost is the normal path by which any new technology becomes widespread, and global AI adoption currently sits somewhere around 15% to 20%.

As for Tesla, automotive revenue beat expectations, but on the back of price cuts, with gross margin falling from 21% to 16%. Research and capital spending keep expanding into projects that will not contribute meaningfully to near-term profit. The more concrete signal is software subscription: paying users of the driver-assistance package grew from roughly 950,000 a year ago to about 1.4 million.

## Extended thoughts

**1. Without forecasts, volatility rises — but most of it is noise, not structure.**

The direct consequence of dropping forward guidance is that the market loses its official anchor for expectations, so every data release now moves prices harder than before. Harder is not the same as more important. This episode is unusually clear about where the structure actually sits: it is the 0.2% monthly line on core prices, and the August report that lands before the September meeting. Most of the daily movement in between is the same fact being repriced over and over.

The cheapest way to separate noise from structure is not to adjudicate daily. It is to **write down in advance which numbers would require you to change your mind**. Once the lines are drawn, everything else can be safely ignored.

**2. The market's measuring stick drifts, so the anchor has to be your own.**

Six months ago the market punished companies for not spending on AI. Today it rewards them for not spending recklessly. Same companies, same financials — only the variable the market cares about changed, and the valuation treatment inverted.

Microsoft's depreciation change deserves particular attention here: the same investment and the same cash outflow, run through a different accounting assumption, produce a different reported profit. It is a useful reminder that **valuing assets with whatever yardstick the market currently favours leaves you permanently half a step behind — and reading only reported earnings can leave you unsure whether you are even looking at the same thing period to period.**

**3. A beautiful income statement and the cash flow statement can tell different stories.**

The contrast most worth writing down from this episode is strong operating cash flow alongside negative free cash flow. Both are true at once because the money went into capital expenditure.

Negative free cash flow is not itself a sin — capital-intensive investment phases look like this by construction. What has to be distinguished is two very different situations. One is an investment phase: the gap is voluntary, supported by visible orders, and can be throttled at will. The other is bleeding: the gap is involuntary, sustained by external financing, and cannot be stopped. The evidence to weigh sits in order visibility, in the form and cost of the financing, and in whether management has articulated the conditions under which it would slow down. This episode contains evidence pointing both ways — which is precisely why the market is divided.

**4. With cost falling and adoption still low, the money concentrates in whichever layer breaks first.**

Inference cost at a given intelligence tier keeps stepping downward, and adoption sits at only 15% to 20%. Put those together and cheaper prices look far more likely to call out additional demand than to destroy it.

That does not mean every participant captures the value. **Close to $800 billion this year, possibly over $1 trillion next year, will settle into whichever layer is genuinely supply-constrained** — not spread evenly across every company with a related name. So rather than asking whether the move can still be chased, the more useful question is: if demand doubles again, which layer snaps first? That layer has pricing power. Links without pricing power simply pass cost pressure downstream, however fast the industry around them is growing.

**5. Positions that look diversified can be a single risk when it matters.**

The market recap at the top of the episode quietly contains a portfolio lesson. In July, Korea's KOSPI fell nearly 30%, the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index fell more than 20%, Taiwan's market moved with them — while European equities held up comparatively well.

A portfolio holding Taiwanese electronics, Korean equities and US semiconductors looks like three markets and three currencies. Functionally it is one exposure to one AI supply chain. **Diversification is measured by sources of risk, not by counting holdings or counting markets** — correlations that look low in calm conditions have a habit of converging on one under stress.

**6. An expectation is only useful if you can check the answer.**

The episode supplies several checkable markers: the 0.2% monthly line on core prices, the August report that arrives before the September meeting, and Taiwan's export orders leading actual exports by roughly one to three months. A statement of the form "at this date, look at this number, and if it exceeds this level I was wrong" is worth far more than any directional assertion.

Writing expectations down and returning to score them does not, over time, mainly build forecasting skill. It builds something more valuable: **knowing which categories of your own judgement fail systematically.** There is no shortcut to that. It only comes from the record.
