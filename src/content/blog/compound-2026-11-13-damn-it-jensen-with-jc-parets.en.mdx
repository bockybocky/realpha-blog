In episode 268 of The Compound and Friends, recorded on 13 November 2026, Josh Brown and Michael Batnick are joined by Trend Labs founder JC Parets, and they open with Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang's departure. Josh's numbers on the show: the stock fell 23% in ten days, which puts the stretch in the worst 2% of all rolling ten-day periods since it came public, and market cap went from roughly $5.6 trillion to under $4 trillion. Over the same stretch the S&P 500 stayed within 2% of its record high. JC puts his own falsifiers up front — the S&P bank index has to hold its January 2007 high and the semiconductor index has to hold the July low, and if those two break, his bullish script is void.

![Under one shared dotted all-time-high line, the left side shows a deep 23% drop for the leading stock while the right side shows only a thin 2% gap for the S&P 500, and the two shaded areas differ enormously in size.](/figures/one-stock-fell-not-the-market-en.svg)

## First came August to October, when tech walked off on its own

JC flipped back through four stretches of the rotation table. From August to October technology led and utilities, real estate and consumer discretionary lagged; from June to August the leaders were financials, healthcare and real estate, with tech dead last; in the first quarter tech was the worst and energy the best. His line is that rotation is the lifeblood of a bull market. Then the midterm result landed, the Nasdaq 100 fell 6% that week, and it was recovered inside the same week. Michael added a comparison: Brazil fell 30% in a day a few weeks ago, deeper than the 1987 crash, and the Dow closed up about 200 points that day with nobody caring. Fifteen years ago, he said, that would have taken S&P futures down on the spot.

![Three periods sit side by side as three columns, with tech climbing from the bottom row all the way to the leading row while energy and financials fall from the top, and the two paths cross.](/figures/sector-rotation-three-legs-en.svg)

## Then came the ten days that took 23% off the leader

You have to go back to 2022 for a stretch like that. The reason given on the show is a relationship between the CEO and an employee, with the employee reportedly tied to China, and the White House aware of it beforehand. Josh set the episode beside two older cases, and the account is his: at Hewlett-Packard in 2010, Mark Hurd was pushed out over expense reports attached to a relationship, his successor paid $11 billion for Autonomy, $9 billion of it was written down, and the stock fell 70%. At Intel in 2018 the CEO left over a relationship with an employee, his successor pushed the company hard into the foundry business, and the stock later hit a fifty-year low. He also raised a comparable case at McDonald's, which turned out fine.

![Three paths branch from one starting point: two fall to different depths and one returns to its original height, and the branch point is labeled with the successor's decision rather than the scandal itself.](/figures/successor-decides-the-fork-en.svg)

So his conclusion lands somewhere I had not considered: more scandal no longer moves him, and what sets the stock from here is who the board hires. Run the older cases through that frame and the 10% drop on the day HP announced the change looks, in hindsight, like an underreaction. That is part of why he is holding — if the street likes the new CEO, the stock could jump 12% in a day, and he does not want to be out of it for that day.

## On the day it landed, the sell side changed its price targets to other numbers

Oppenheimer removed its price target outright, citing no obvious successor and warning about brain drain. Bernstein cut from 400 to 200. Another firm went from 500 to 200. Melius moved 315 to 185. Key Bank dropped its rating to neutral. Josh read the whole list and said the numbers are made up, that price targets are gossip columns for adults.

![In the left column the price-target bars from five sell-side shops are halved or dropped altogether, while in the right column the earnings estimates and the order book remain two full-length lines that never moved.](/figures/price-targets-cut-earnings-unchanged-en.svg)

I stopped on that. The earnings estimates hanging on the same company that day still called for 95% growth this year and 70% next, with a forward multiple around ten. What got revised 60% in a single day measures the sell side's confidence, and the company's order book did not move by one unit. Josh made the same point from the other side: there is no CEO premium to come off, because the stock has traded at a discount for three years and got cheaper every quarter. JC pushed back on one piece of it — the growth rate may not stay at 90%, and that is precisely why the stock trades at ten times instead of twenty-four.

## The other half of the tape that same day

Broadcom rose 6% that day and AMD 5%. The money stayed in the AI trade and moved out of Nvidia into AMD and Broadcom. On JC's chart AMD is up 123% over twelve months against Nvidia's 15%, and the market cap AMD has added this year has carried it into the trillion-dollar tier. Josh added the other route this year: the only one of the Magnificent Seven up 30% is Apple, a large-cap tech name nobody files under AI, and it became the answer to "where does the money go after I sell Micron and Nvidia" — buy it and you skip data-centre financing and you skip capex worries, and there is a phone cycle running. It trades near 40 times earnings, Berkshire has been selling, and everyone else keeps buying.

![One shrinking circle sends two arcs out to two growing circles, and below them two bars of very different heights show the twelve-month gap between 123% and 15%.](/figures/money-changed-cars-same-road-en.svg)

JC's framing is that the market carries no sentimentality. It gets greedy and frightened, excited at peaks and despondent at lows, and it spares no person and no event. He pointed at the Dow closing green on 6 January 2021 and said the question on the desk stays the same one: where will my money be treated best.

## Where this sits now: two lines underfoot

The first is the S&P bank index, breaking out above its January 2007 high, a high that is nearly twenty years old. JC flagged the detail that gets skipped: back then the bank index peaked nine months before the whole market did. So the bear story, he said, has to start with that chart failing. The second line is the semiconductor index's July low — it gapped below last Thursday, stayed below for four sessions, and gapped back above the day before taping. If that line goes again and rolls over, his own script stops working.

![Two stacked panels each hold a price line and a horizontal threshold line, with the upper line having just crossed up through it and the lower one gapping below, closing under it for four days, then jumping back above.](/figures/two-lines-underfoot-en.svg)

He also floated a view nobody in the room would second. At a conference in Nashville he asked the room who thought a US housing boom was just getting started, and not one hand went up; some people got annoyed with him. Existing home sales that same week were the lowest since August 2010, down 5.5% on the month and 8.8% on the year. Yet homebuilders like Toll Brothers are pressing against all-time highs. He is not forecasting a boom — he wants confirmation, and for him confirmation is the US home construction ETF, ITB, breaking out.

## "I am stuck in a stock that is in the headlines too — do I cut or hold?"

Four people on set that day had all owned the stock, and two sold while two held. The sellers' reason was opportunity cost: this gets political, it can drag on for a year, the CEO has to testify before Congress, and rather than sit tied up here for months the money can go somewhere else. Josh's reason for holding is twelve years in the position and an average cost so low that selling would be idiotic, plus he already sold on the way up everything he wanted to sell. He said himself that this reason is of little use to a listener.

JC answered with the line I keep coming back to: what market commentary misses most is that everyone is in a different situation. In the same minute one person sells and another buys, and the two sides have different goals, horizons and risk tolerance, and none of it settles who is right. What I do on a day like that is write down three things first: why I bought it, where my cost is, and which number appearing would mean I was wrong. When I can write them, what other people do stops mattering. When I cannot, the only thing holding the position up is the news.

## "Bad news everywhere — so why does somebody keep buying?"

In the same week's US retail investor survey, 51.4% expected stocks to fall over the next six months, with the index 2% off its record. Josh also read the past five days of ETF flows, around $12 billion a day, with the top seventeen all equity. The interesting part is that the complaint a while back was poor breadth and a falling median stock, and this month more than 85% of stocks rose, 70% got back above their fifty-day average, the average stock rose 5% in October and the median rose 7.5%. Josh's reading: stocks have been ripping, and American investors chose to own other things and parked the money in tech.

![A single half-filled bar on the left stands for the share of people who are bearish, while three nearly full bars on the right stand for the share of stocks actually rising, so the two sides point opposite ways.](/figures/sentiment-versus-the-tape-en.svg)

Then step out a ring. Germany, Switzerland, Belgium, the Netherlands, Italy, Spain, Canada and Australia are all at record highs, and so is the Indian bank index — India being the country everyone said a year ago would have its jobs eaten by AI. JC says nobody looks at these charts, which is why he brought them.

What stays with me from this stretch is the stack of old magazine covers he brought. The 1974 cover with a bear smashing through glass landed on that year's low. Newsweek's "Worries of Wall Street" in 1965 landed on a low too. The 1979 one with the world tipped over a barrel of oil landed on the top in oil. His use of them is simple: covers show up once a story has been told to death, so they tend to sit on turns. He also pulled out the bowling bubble of 1960 and 1961 — automatic pinsetters had just been invented, the related companies were priced on multiples of bowling hours per family per week, Brunswick rose 1,600%, and the whole thing collapsed when everyone worked out that nobody cared about bowling.

Josh followed it with a book published in 1870 called Ten Years in Wall Street, and the letters Charles Dow wrote in the Wall Street Journal through the 1880s and 1890s, hundreds of which he has read. Strip out the dates and the company names and it reads like any ordinary trading day, except the subject back then was railroads. He closed with a line: the cemetery is full of so-called indispensable men.

## The one thing worth taking with you

Whether a role can be replaced is answered by the successor, not by the person who left. The gap between those two older cases came from the decision the next person made, rather than from how ugly the scandal was — same type of event, and one company wrote down $9 billion while the other was fine.

Something I have tried: take ten minutes and find the role in your own life that stops everything when one person is missing — the bills and insurance only one person at home can handle, the process at work only one person can run, or that person being you. Write down the three things that would jam tomorrow if that role changed hands, then take one of them and write it out as steps someone else can follow, and hand it over.
