## What the episode is about

In one line: **the industry barely changed, but every price did — and once good news starts getting punished, the thing to recompute is not your thesis, it is how long you can last.**

The opening does not sound like a finance show at all. The first Taiwanese indie game the host had invested in went live, and he describes himself as a wreck about it — proud that the work finally shipped, terrified about bugs, about players not biting, about what happens to the next few titles if the first one misses. Then he bought his own game, cleared the first boss, the second, the third, told his wife he was too busy to come to dinner, drank coffee through the night and kept going into the morning — and knew it had landed. He also asked listeners writing reviews to please skip the fan-service comments and just review the game.

The soulslike becomes the running metaphor for the whole episode, and he pushes it onto the market: this tape feels like walking into the town where everyone is waiting for a saviour to return, and the saviour does not return. **"The more you do, the more you get wrong." "You run back to pick up the money you dropped, die a few more times, and recover none of it."** That is his summary of trading this stretch.

The middle section is market and industry: a selloff with no single findable cause, good earnings sold anyway, memory price increases starting to converge, power semis and passives still rising. The listener questions circle back to life — young with a perfect body and no money, old with money and a body that no longer cooperates — and to the line I remember most: **the market cap you lose can be earned back; the day you lose cannot.**

The Laozi passage fits. Muddy water does not clear because you stir it. It clears because you stop. And "does not wish to be full" is precisely the instruction for a moment like this: do not run yourself at maximum.

## The main points

**One: when a selloff has no findable reason, strip out the benchmark before inventing a story.** A listener asked what happened to a particular process-node group — was someone talking it down? His answer was sharp: tell me what is *not* down. The semiconductor index was down close to 30% at the low, so a tech name down 30% is still inside the market's range and needs no bespoke explanation. **Only when something falls far more than its benchmark does "what happened" become a real question.** Skip this step and you will invent a script for every position, then act on the wrong script.

**Two: good earnings getting sold means the information is not in the earnings.** In this stretch the leading foundry printed excellent numbers, a cloud giant demonstrated that AI genuinely makes money, and a large analog and power semiconductor vendor guided to a broad recovery on its call — all of them fell. His stance has two layers: respect what the price is doing, but keep your own verdict. Because **"if the market were always right, nothing cheap would ever fall into your lap."** He also concedes something the market does not yet know may be brewing, much like last March, when nobody knew what was being sold until the news finally broke and the selling was already over. He refuses to commit fully to either reading.

**Three: he converts "I don't know" into a position rule, not an opinion.** His own book is down a bit over 20%, and he has set the trigger in advance: at 25–30% he moves to a more conservative posture and starts raising cash. Notice the shape of that sentence — **it executes without requiring you to first understand why things are falling.** In the same breath he says he dislikes reaching for leverage into a crash; someone always catches the bottom levered and comes back to say this was obviously the spot, but he would rather not stand in the dangerous place, because "at least you get to live longer."

**Four: negative free cash flow is not an indictment, it is a route choice.** On the cloud giant being punished for capex blowing cash flow negative, he wants to push back: first, everyone expected cash flow to turn — that is not news; second, a cloud service provider that does not invest today simply loses later. The exception is the handset company that runs on faith and channel control, which has enough end-user leverage to ride other people's models instead. He quoted a listener comment I think is the best line in the episode: **in Monopoly, the player who buys houses until they are nearly bankrupt is the one who ends up collecting rent until it explodes.**

**Five: AI ended zero-marginal-cost software, and the change is structural.** Software used to be built once and copied forever at almost no cost. Now, if a model sits behind the product, **every single run is a fresh cost**, and margin compression is inevitable. His own example: he had a high-end model write up notes on an earnings call, and the run cost about NT$400 — while reading a transcript himself would have taken twenty or thirty minutes to reach the same understanding. So the "always use the most expensive model" phase will recede. **Unless your end output is genuinely high-value and you can price it higher, a better model just eats more of your margin.**

**Six: memory price increases will flatten, but not because demand died.** The path he lays out: roughly doubling in the first half, converging to somewhere between 10% and 30% in Q3, under 10% in Q4, then broadly flat. The cause sits on the supply side braking itself — with vendor gross margins pushing toward 80%, that becomes hard to justify to customers and to Washington, while China starts refusing the increases and shifting to domestic supply. **He pre-announces it explicitly: you will eventually see headlines saying memory pricing has gone flat, the market will probably sell it, and that is profit normalising rather than demand breaking.** Elsewhere — passives, power devices, substrates — prices are still climbing. On the industry, in his words, "no change."

**Seven: the market cap you lose can be earned back; the day you lose cannot.** He says this replying to a listener lamenting that enthusiasm fades with age. His read on the contradiction: young, you have a perfect body and no money; later you have money and a body that is failing, and everything you finally get to try lands as "well, that's all it was." Hence his standing advice to younger listeners — go live first, rather than rushing to take investing seriously in college. It has nothing to do with the tape, and it is the line from the episode I kept.

## Going further

### One: "the industry hasn't changed" only answers one of three cells

The episode contains what looks like a contradiction and is actually maturity: he says the industry picture is unchanged, and also that at 25–30% down he raises cash. Plenty of people would call that bullish talk and bearish hands.

It is neither. **Those are two different questions.** Any holding should be split into three cells and answered separately.

The first cell is **is the thesis right** — does the company still make money the way you thought, is the demand still there. Industry decides this one, and industry trends rarely reverse in a few weeks.

The second is **will the market pay for it** — what multiple the same earnings get today. Liquidity, risk appetite and the leverage in the system decide this one, and it shares almost no parts with the first. The episode's sharpest evidence sits here: equipment names trading under ten times next year's earnings, some at seven or eight; substrate and board names around ten. **The earnings did not move. The multiple did.** Not observing the second cell means assuming it is fixed, and it just moved a long way.

The third is **can I survive the path** — unrelated to the other two, determined entirely by your leverage, single-position weight and cash level.

Why can't you go straight from cell one to "add here"? Because cell two sits in between, and cell two is neither yours to control nor computable. He supplies the counterexample himself: in the shipping cycle, revenue was still printing records while the stock had already broken. **Revenue is a cell-one signal, price is a cell-two outcome, and using the former to deny the latter is how people average down all the way out of the game.**

Which means the definition of a stop has to change too. He uses moving averages for stops and take-profits, and says plainly: I don't know what it is discounting either, but I have to protect my gains. **There is no cell-one judgement in that sentence at all.** A stop is not an admission that the thesis was wrong; it is an admission that cell three has run out. Blend the two and you will hold to prove yourself right, right up until you have no choices left.

### Two: cost is back on the income statement, so software has to be repriced

The NT$400 example is small; the chain it implies is long.

Software used to have near-zero marginal cost, so each extra unit sold was nearly pure profit — which is why the sector earned its multiples. Now, with a model behind the product, **every use is a real expense**, and three things follow.

First, **falling gross margin is not the same as a deteriorating business**. He recalls the old server-ODM argument: take the full-system route and gross margin necessarily drops, but unit prices are higher and you make more money — it turned out to be a maths game. Software is now being misread the same way, with one difference: this time the cost is real, not an accounting artefact.

Second, **pricing power moves from a bonus to a survival condition**. If your cost scales with usage and your price to the customer will not move, then a stronger model kills you faster — which is exactly his point: the better your AI, the higher your cost and the more of your profit disappears. So the question about a company is not whether it has bolted on AI. It is **whether its compute spend is attached to an output it can actually charge for**.

Third, and the step most often skipped: **why can't you wave this away with "AI costs will keep falling"?** He believes they will, long run, the way each semiconductor generation delivers more per dollar. But he flags the timing mismatch — memory, passives, power devices, substrates are all rising right now, and in that environment costs cannot be squeezed down. He compares it to the fibre built out during the dot-com era, and says outright that a cooling period is coming. **Fibre did eventually get cheap; not everyone got to see "eventually."** So "costs will fall" is a claim about the destination, not about the path — and your position lives on the path.

### Three: name the noise before it happens

His handling of the memory section is worth isolating.

He did not simply say he likes or dislikes memory. He described the future event first: **you will see reporting that pricing has gone flat, the market will likely sell it, and that is profit normalising rather than demand breaking.** That is a pre-named judgement, and its value is not whether the call is right. It is that a news item which has not happened yet has already been filed.

This matters because noise does its damage at the moment it lands. What is expensive is having to do three things at once right then: judge whether it is true, judge whether it changes your reason for holding, and suppress the physical reaction to a red screen. **Those three compete for the same attention, and two of them can be finished in advance.**

A good pre-written note has three parts: what I expect to see, what I think it means, and what would make me admit I was wrong. The third gets dropped most often — and without it the first two are a prediction, not a test. With it, when the headline finally arrives, all that is left is a comparison instead of a fresh chain of reasoning.

Which loops back to the day you cannot earn back. **Your attention and your time are the same position, and the market asks for it daily.** A listener asked whether he would start a new show with no fixed schedule; he admitted his favourite parts are the opening and the Q&A, and that he has grown allergic to being treated as a stock-tip show — because second-hand quoting on social platforms distorts what he said, and the moment the tape turns, people go hunting for someone to blame, having thanked nobody on the way up. That drain has nothing to do with prices, and it reliably takes away the judgement you needed for the one decision that mattered.

Laozi's line is the prescription. **Muddy water clears on its own — provided you take your hands out of it.**
