The October 18, 2026 episode of Acquired is about the business of Seinfeld. Host Ben Gilbert says he went to Nielsen to verify one number: through the first eight months of 2026, 72 million Americans watched a comedy that stopped production in 1998 — roughly one in five people in the country. Once the two hosts trace the money, the split is stark: NBC made about $2 billion in advertising profit across nine years and owned none of the show, while the ownership side of the ledger has collected roughly $5 billion in licensing over three decades. All of it rests on a rule that no longer exists — the FCC's 1970 Fin-Syn regulations, which forced networks and production studios to be separate buyers and sellers.

## The network that was number one owned nothing

My picture of the television business was thin. NBC aired Seinfeld, won Thursday nights, sold the most expensive ad inventory in history — so NBC was the big winner, and the production company was a contractor that got paid to deliver tape.

The relationship runs the other way, and the law put it there. In 1970 the FCC barred the networks from holding a financial interest in the shows they aired. The mechanism that grew around it was deficit financing: NBC greenlit a show and paid roughly 80% of what it cost to make, buying one airing plus one summer rerun. The studio ate the gap, and in exchange it owned the show.

So for nine years, Castle Rock lost money on every episode of Seinfeld. The more they made, the deeper they went — the shape of an early-stage venture fund, burning for five years and collecting in the sixth. Ben's phrasing is the one I kept: what they bought was the right to lose money on each episode.

NBC's ledger looks nothing like that. In 1996 the show produced about $150 million in profit for the network; in 1997, $200 million. NBC's total annual profit at the time was around $1 billion. One half-hour comedy, a fifth of an entire broadcast network's earnings. And that undercounts it, because ad inventory was sold in bundles — if you were General Motors and wanted $30 million of Seinfeld, NBC made you buy $200 million of its prime time that season.

Nine years later NBC had banked around $2 billion in advertising profit and held zero residual value. Castle Rock, the contractor in my old picture, started in 1987 with five partners and $30 million that The Coca-Cola Company had put in through Columbia Pictures.

## The half-billion-dollar clause nobody thought was worth anything

So how did Jerry Seinfeld and Larry David end up on that cap table? The answer has little to do with talent and everything to do with the fact that nobody in the room thought the thing was worth much.

At the November 1988 meeting, what NBC agreed to was a single 90-minute special — and the people across the table were the late-night and specials department, not the comedy department that ran sitcoms. Comedy had The Cosby Show and Cheers, plus a full apparatus of script reviews, notes, and committees. Specials had a calendar slot.

What NBC promised was this: go find a production company, cut your own deal with them, and we guarantee we'll make it. Hollywood normally runs the other way — studios fund pilots and beg networks for a greenlight. With production and airing guaranteed, Jerry and Larry could walk that promise into any production company in town. Ben's analogy: you're writing a Series A check with someone already committed in writing to mark it up 10x at the C.

They picked Castle Rock, and they essentially wrote the terms. The contract has never been published, but reporting puts Jerry and Larry at about 15% of the show each. At the time, the consensus value of that 15% was: one special, one rerun, how much could it be.

A second cut mattered even more. In November 1989, Sony bought Columbia Pictures from Coca-Cola and inherited distribution rights to Castle Rock's output — while the underlying copyright stayed with the independent Castle Rock. Meaningless in the moment; thirty years later it is the hinge of the whole story.

Castle Rock was later bought by Ted Turner and eventually landed inside Warner. So the largest ownership slug of Seinfeld sits with Warner today — but distribution sits with Sony. Warner cannot simply park the show on its own HBO Max; every cycle has to go out to a real bid. In the 1998 cable cycle, Turner already owned Castle Rock and still had to bid against itself through Sony: $1 million per episode, four years, nearly $200 million.

The control group is right next door. Friends was also made in the Fin-Syn era, by Warner Bros., but Warner held distribution too. The Office was made after repeal, and NBC owned it outright. When streaming turned over in 2019, Warner pulled Friends back to HBO Max and NBC pulled The Office to Peacock — the announced $425 million and $500 million are internal transfers, disclosed because guild residuals have to be computed from something. Which left Seinfeld as the only free agent in the top tier. Netflix's 2019 deal with Sony — reported at well over $500 million for five years of worldwide exclusivity — was the largest sum a streamer had ever paid an outside studio for a series no longer in production. This year's renewal came in slightly lower, narrowed to the US and Canada, with Sony planning to sell international streaming separately.

Stack the licensing: $500 million-plus in the 1995 cycle across a record 224 local stations; $1.1 billion in 1998 once cable and international were added; roughly another $500 million from DVDs. Run it to today and about $5 billion has flowed into that cap table, on top of NBC's $2 billion of first-run profit. Total cost to make the show, including the absurd final-season salaries, was around $250 million — all of it paid by 1998.

One transaction in here still makes me wince. Castle Rock sold to Turner at the end of 1993: $100 million in cash plus about $300 million of assumed debt, call it $400 million. The timing looked smart, since repeal of Fin-Syn meant networks would make their own shows and the independents' path was closing. But the roughly $2 to $2.5 billion of syndication money that later flowed into that position went to Turner and then Warner. None of it reached the five original partners.

Then there's the brunch, late 1997, on the 38th floor of Trump Tower in the NBC CEO's apartment. Jack Welch brought slides and argued to Jerry Seinfeld that the show had not yet peaked. As people stood up, he pulled Jerry aside and wrote three numbers on a piece of paper: $5 million, 22, $110 million — a tenth season. That was 11% of NBC's entire annual profit, offered to one person who was already the highest-paid actor in television history at $1 million an episode, and who also held the backend.

Jerry said no. His reason came from the stage: you've been up there a long time, they're still screaming, and if you can get off now they'll never forget you. The episode's claim is that this protected the next thirty years. A Nielsen figure from 2021 captures it: 41% of the show's audience at that point had been toddlers or unborn when it ended in 1998.

In 2017 Harvard Business Review asked him why he hadn't found a more efficient, more sustainable way to make the show — what would McKinsey say? Jerry: "Who's McKinsey?" A big consulting firm. "Are they funny?" No. "Then I don't need them." And then the line: if you're efficient, you're doing it the wrong way; the right way is the hard way.

## How I read "this company is hot" now

When I look at a business now I add one question: is the heat rented or owned?

That question connects to something I used to find genuinely confusing — revenue hits a record, the coverage is glowing, and the stock does nothing. Seinfeld puts both extremes on the same table. NBC won the ratings, sold the priciest inventory in history, had nine great years of cash flow, and held nothing the day it ended. Castle Rock lost money on every episode for nine years and then collected for thirty. Both income statements looked fine; one was a lease running out, the other was an asset. So I now separate two things when reading financials: who receives this period's cash, and who holds whatever can still be sold after the activity stops. It doesn't make me right, but it stops me reading "renting well" as "owning well."

The second common worry is how long something hot stays hot. The episode offers a sharp comparison. The Seinfeld pilot tested terribly; the research report said no segment of the audience was eager to watch again and rated overall performance weak. Jerry and Larry saw that memo years later and hung it on the bathroom wall at the production office. The first-season order was four episodes — the smallest in television history, funded by canceling a Bob Hope special. Larry David's reaction: that's fine, four episodes is all I've got anyway. At the end of season three the show was 42nd on television. In February 1993 it moved to Thursday and went from 45th to 5th in a single week.

Larry fought that move. His line was roughly: if they didn't watch us on Wednesdays, I don't want them watching us on Thursdays. The episode's inference is that a slow burn with a small obsessive core is more durable than fast mass exposure. I can't verify that one — the slow burns that died don't get counted, and survivorship bias is hard to strip out here. The weaker version I can live with: a slow burn guarantees nothing, but a show that keeps getting renewed through four unnoticed years has already proven someone will pay for it.

A word on the limits of all this. Most of the dollar figures come from a handful of outlets' reporting and estimates; the contracts have never been published, and Jerry and Larry's 15% is "as reported." And the value created by walking off stage has no counterfactual — nobody can show that a tenth season would have dulled the asset or cost two licensing cycles. I treat this episode as a structural example rather than a set of books.

## One thing to take away

The idea: returns follow residual value. Heat only decides how many times that residual can be sold, and for how much.

Something I've tried, and it works outside investing too: take the one thing you put the most hours into this week and write two lines on paper — "the day this stops, what's left in my hands," and "the day this stops, what's left in someone else's." Then leave it. When I did it, two or three of the things I was pouring the most time into went to zero the day they stopped, and for the rest, the residual wasn't on my side of the table.
