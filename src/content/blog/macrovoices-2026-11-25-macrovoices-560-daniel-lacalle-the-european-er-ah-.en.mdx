>
> —— Wen Qiao, in *Book of Jin*, "Biography of Wen Qiao" (Tang, 648; my own translation)

On MacroVoices episode 560, released 25 November 2026, host Erik Townsend brings back Daniel Lacalle, chief economist and fund manager at Tressis, recorded early the previous Monday before the New York open. Daniel declines to call the prior week's selloff an AI bubble bursting: he says the largest hyperscalers turn net cash in 2027 and repay debt without much trouble, and the hard part is that the market cannot yet tell which companies land on the other side of that line. His sharpest words are about Europe — the euro is the only world reserve currency carrying redenomination risk, and the arithmetic of a French exit does not close (he reckons those bonds at 50 cents on the current 100 at best, with pensions cut 70 to 80%), so the destination is a much weaker euro. All of it rests on one shared condition: what the Federal Reserve does in December is still unknown, and he expects markets to stay choppy until that clears.

## Before the recording started, they laughed

Erik mentions on air that the two of them had a laugh beforehand, because Daniel called it "the European, er, ah, situation." A man who manages money in Europe needed a pause to find the word for the continent under his feet. I noted that down because he answers everything else that hour — the United States, China, oil, gold — without hesitating, and Europe is the one topic where his phrasing is "it looks scary from afar and probably scarier from within." He takes a long route before he walks back to that laugh.

## He puts the hottest question aside first

Erik opens with the bloodbath of the prior week and the bigger question behind it: is AI capex busting dot-com style?

Daniel steps around the word bubble, and his reasoning is roundabout but I buy it: everyone discusses whether it is a bubble at every available occasion, some have already called it one, and all of that sits inside the valuations and inside the yields these companies pay to issue debt. He calls it a risk to be clarified over the next couple of months.

He wants us to be more granular. On cash flow expectations, the largest hyperscalers generate net cash in 2027 and can repay their debt; that route does not exist for everybody. What the market is doing now is admitting it cannot separate who develops faster and more profitably from who faces falling margins, so it marks the whole group together. He expects a better-informed split after the next earnings season.

![One line starts below the zero axis, crosses it and keeps rising, while another line branches downward from the same starting point, the two drifting further and further apart.](/figures/cloud-capex-cashflow-split-en.svg)

Then Erik raises an angle I had not considered: even if the money is there, could data centre moratoria block construction? Daniel's answer is the cleanest cut of the episode — if you worry about the scale of the capex and also worry that the capex will not be deployed, you are double counting the same risk. He frames moratoria as a political problem: whether a centre goes up in Illinois or Texas is decided by local politics, and if it cannot be built there it gets built elsewhere. Asia, North Africa, several Latin American countries would be glad to host them, with ample power and lighter regulation. What politicians create for themselves is lost revenue, lost investment, lost jobs.

## On energy he gives the funniest and most practical answer of the hour

Erik presses: you say the United States, China and Russia will cooperate on refined product supply, but refineries keep getting blown up and nobody has the capex to build new ones, so how does that cooperation work? Daniel grants that if Ukraine keeps hitting Russian refineries the cooperation is a vague promise; what he reads between the lines is that the United States can tell Ukraine that absent a peace deal they may strike other things, just not refineries. He says the world's largest military powers are able to tell leaders in the middle of a conflict to throw their missiles over there and not at a refinery, and that this is not so hard to arrange — China can influence Iran away from Gulf refineries, and Ukraine's second largest investor is also China.

European natural gas gets no good news: Germany's decision to shut down nuclear, plus dependence on imported gas, has not improved in any form, and prices do not return to pre-2022 levels. From here the camera starts moving toward Europe.

## The dollar is strong enough that he says nobody wants it

The dollar index is pushing toward 104, pressing on a level that has mattered technically in the past.

Daniel traces the cause to the Fed. Rate hikes create a vacuum effect: once the environment turns uncertain, hoarding dollar assets becomes more profitable, or at least less risky, than the alternatives elsewhere. Money taken off gold, tech and equities needs somewhere to wait — and he notes nobody decided to shelter from uncertainty in euro area assets, because that would mean hoarding French bonds.

Then comes the line most likely to get quoted. He says the monetary world is a contest over who loses first. The dollar wins because in the shared debasement of fiat currencies it is the least bad: the euro carries redenomination risk, a fiscal crisis and no growth; the yuan has capital controls and a managed exchange rate; sterling, the Swiss franc and the yen cannot absorb that liquidity. He calls it a flight into the least uncomfortable asset.

![Five bars extend downward from the same top line, with the dollar bar clearly shorter and the other three equally deep, differing only in their labels.](/figures/least-bad-currency-en.svg)

Then he says something that sounds contradictory: the dollar cannot strengthen much further. What followed previous sharp dollar appreciations was a yen crisis, a French debt crisis, the Asian financial crisis, the Latin American tequila bubble. A reserve currency's job is supplying liquidity, and priced too aggressively it cannot do that job, while the tightening the Fed engineered both herds the market into dollars and hits the countries that need dollar liquidity to fund their own currencies. Erik asks whether that boxes the Fed into a corner, and Daniel moves the problem one layer over: the trouble is that it uses rates as its only tool, hikes do little to inflation, and they do a lot to financial conditions for families and small businesses. His closing line on that I wrote down — the Fed cannot print anything the government has not printed earlier.

![A rising dollar line crosses a falling liquidity line, and the whole area to the right of the crossing point is marked as the zone where things break.](/figures/reserve-currency-too-strong-en.svg)

## Then he walks back to that laugh

Daniel sums up the European Union's problem in two words: arrogance and ignorance. A group of politicians, in many cases unelected bureaucrats, set energy, industrial, agricultural, technology and financial policy believing the whole world would follow them into an interventionist, directed economy. Nobody followed, and those policies failed miserably.

He wants listeners to hold on to a few facts: the European Central Bank ran negative nominal rates, it ran massive stimulus packages, and the euro area is stagnating in the middle of the largest stimulus plan in years, the Next Generation EU fund. The bloc now holds no technological advantage, no energy advantage and no financial sector advantage. He leaves a little hope: businesses and voters are saying enough is enough, and political change may come. Until then, China is not stopping and the United States is not stopping.

So Erik brings up what their mutual friend Louis-Vincent Gave said on the same programme: Europe may reach a fork where some countries — France, with Italy perhaps not far behind — are forced to abandon the euro, having no other way to deal with the problems in their own economies.

Daniel says the risk exists, that the euro is the only world reserve currency with redenomination risk. Then he runs the arithmetic. France leaving the euro means Germany enters a depression: the TARGET2 imbalances — the internal IOUs the euro area's central banks run against each other — make it impossible for one nation to leave and another to absorb the resulting hole in its own banks. Look at the liability side and it is a very big challenge for France; look at the asset side and it is virtually impossible.

![A normal-length bar sits on the left, while on the right two groups of bars shrink to about half and to under three tenths of it.](/figures/france-exit-asset-side-en.svg)

He spells out the impossibility. Pension funds, investment banks, commercial banks, and in many countries the social security treasury, all hold bonds that would be redenominated into a new currency, and his optimistic mark on those is 50 cents on the current 100. French citizens complain about the pension system today; he asks you to imagine them waking up to pensions slashed by 70 to 80% — and it would happen from one day to the next. Japanese pension funds have spent years hoarding European debt for a little yield, so this road takes Japan down with it.

I liked his image: the euro area is like a shopping mall where everyone is allowed to finance each other and nobody is allowed to leave — something like the Hotel California, you can check out any time but you cannot leave, and in fact you do not leave. He adds that even the UK will find itself pushed back toward the European Union despite the referendum.

![A box closed on all four sides holds four storefronts, each wired to a central IOU node, the exit on the right wall is crossed out, and the UK outside the box is pushed back in by an arrow.](/figures/eurozone-mall-no-exit-en.svg)

## Run the numbers and one road is left

If it cannot be dismantled, how does the account balance? Daniel's answer is the currency.

He says the end game for the euro is a currency much more devalued than today, and that the ECB, Germany, France and Italy are all heading there, against the design intent at the euro's inception — a strong currency meant to hold down inflation in nations that had lived with it for a long time. He calls the euro a bull market project: it works well while economies grow, people can buy a home and make money investing, and it stops working when economies stagnate and things get as hard as they are now. The debasement itself then becomes another problem for the area's inflation expectations.

Erik's last topic is gold, and he asks it with some frustration: the debasement arguments keep piling up, so logically gold should be at all-time highs, and instead it crashed last week. Daniel says that fits his framework: gold is priced in dollars, and this stretch of dollar strength does not last forever. He points at a second source of the correction, a gold rush mentality that had ETFs building up, pulling the paper market away from a physical market that is exceedingly tight. Supply and demand he reads as bullish — central banks buying, demand rising, supply constrained. The way he closed was this: fiat debasement is a policy, which he separates from a discussion, an idea or an expectation.

![Two lines leave the same point, the paper gold line climbing higher and higher while the physical line stays nearly flat, opening an ever-widening gap between them.](/figures/paper-gold-vs-physical-en.svg)

As the episode wraps, London has just opened and Erik notes gold is up $125 from Friday's low, crossing his fingers for the gold longs in the audience.

## Everyone is calling it a bubble, so is it still one?

This question shows up under the news every day: if everybody is shouting AI bubble, does that mean it will not pop?

Half of Daniel's reasoning is usable. A risk discussed over and over has entered valuations, and entered the yields these companies pay on their debt — that half holds. But "already priced" describes the narrative, while the mechanism is still sitting there; he calls it a risk to clarify over the next couple of months, and what needs clarifying is concrete: which companies produce enough cash flow to repay what they borrowed.

Since he says the market cannot separate the individual names and marks good and bad together, the only question left for me is whether the cash flow at the company I hold covers its debt — and that one has filings to check, no guessing required. I have gotten this wrong myself, taking "everyone says this is expensive" to mean "so it will fall," and then it did not fall for a long time while the things I let go of got more expensive. It looked like judgement; taken apart, it was using other people's volume as my own evidence.

## The bull case holds and the price goes the other way

The second question hurts more: my reason for owning this still stands, so why is it falling?

Gold this past week is the specimen. Central banks buying, mine supply constrained, debasement as policy — not one of those reasons changed last week, and the price crashed anyway. The mechanism Daniel gives is the unit of account: gold is quoted in dollars, the Fed pulled money into dollars, the denominator moved first and the numerator had nothing to do with it.

![Two fractions labelled before and after sit side by side, the numerator blocks exactly the same size while the after panel's denominator block is clearly wider, so the quotient shrinks.](/figures/denominator-moved-first-en.svg)

What I take from that is to split the reasons into two piles before buying: one pile belongs to the thing itself (supply and demand, cash flow, competitive position), the other belongs to pricing and funding (exchange rates, interest rates, where liquidity runs). The second pile has no relationship with your bull case and it moves faster than the first, and while Daniel says dollar strength will not persist he gives no timetable — your reasons holding up does not mean your holding period survives. These days I write one extra line into my notes when I buy: if this is still falling three months from now, which pile will I think caused it?

## The one thing to take away

**Some things stay in your life because the alternatives are worse, which has nothing to do with whether they are any good.** The dollar this past stretch is the specimen: Daniel says it wins because in the shared debasement of fiat currencies it is the least bad. Turn that around and it gets uncomfortable — you think you are holding the strong one, and what you are holding is the one that has not fallen over yet. Those two look identical on paper, and they separate on the day you need it to hold.

Something I have tried: take a choice you made recently — the job you have, the place you live, the person you see every week — and write two lines. The first line is "I stayed because of what is good about it." The second is "I stayed because the other options are worse." If you can fill in both, look at which line runs longer and more specific. If the second one runs much longer, there is no rush to change anything, but go take a look at what the runner-up looks like now — the reason it came second may have expired.
