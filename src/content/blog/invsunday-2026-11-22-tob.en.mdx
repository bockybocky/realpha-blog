The 22 November 2026 episode of *Investor's Sunday* — part two — hosts Takahiro Kato, president of Jimoty, on how a local classifieds board grew physical drop-off spots, on the 2020 IPO, and on the tender offer completed this October by CCC (Culture Convenience Club, the parent of TSUTAYA). Kato says the overwhelming majority of what moves through the service sits between zero yen and a few hundred yen; all 50 Jimoty Spots are run jointly with the local government under a formal agreement, and the company buys nothing, pricing nothing, carrying rent and staffing on its own books. Jimoty turned profitable only in 2019 and listed the following year; Kato puts the reason for going private on timing — pushing 50 stores toward several hundred needs upfront store investment that pulls against the rhythm of quarterly reporting. What follows are listener notes; per-store economics have never been disclosed, so every inference here stays inside what he said on air.

## I had been treating secondhand as a business about price spreads

My mental model for a secondhand platform was Mercari: match what sellers and buyers each want a thing to be worth, take a cut, and the denser the trading, the more efficient the price. Holding that ruler against Jimoty, the questions I asked were about average ticket, take rate, and seller supply.

Underneath that ruler sat a cruder assumption. When a listed company with real usage and real profit gets taken private, my first reflex is that growth has topped out, or that the market refused to credit it and so it withdrew. Both guesses put valuation at the center.

This episode overturned both. The first one was worse than I thought — I had not even read correctly what this business sells.

## He was looking at waste-processing plants

The biggest difference from Mercari, Kato says, is the act of giving: handing something over for free, or tagging it at a few hundred yen. The share of users trying to sell high is low; the calculation behind a branded item worth tens of thousands of yen, or an unused purchase listed at ten percent off retail, is rare on his service. The dominant mindset is that giving it away beats throwing it out.

![Two item-price distribution curves are overlaid on the same axis; one peaks right against the zero end of the price axis and then flattens out fast, while the other peaks in the mid-to-upper range of a few thousand to a few tens of thousands of yen, so the two peaks sit apart.](/figures/jimoty-price-distribution-en.svg)

Host Morimoto is a user himself. He has sold a sofa. Hunting for a Super Famicom Mini for his child's birthday and finding it nowhere, he found one on Jimoty on the first try, haggled in the chat — he was the one travelling, he pointed out, and travel costs money — and brought 7,000 yen down to 5,000, met a stranger near their place, and swapped cash for console on the spot. The family fridge arrived the same way: unable to find a white good in a color he liked, he was given one through the service.

Morimoto goes on to count what he has given away himself — a stroller, a baby cot, the sort of thing people hand on anyway — and offers the rice cooker analogy: you buy a new one, and nobody around you happens to want your old one, while the internet can find the person who wants a rice cooker at that exact moment. Kato agrees and adds tableware: smashing usable plates and binning them strikes many Japanese people as wasteful, yet the motivation to wrap plates up and ship them to a buyer is thin. Few people own a wardrobe of items with real resale value, and the sight of still-usable household goods going into burnable waste showed him a need no one was serving.

![On the left a pipe narrows step by step through four gates, entering wide and leaving as only a thin stub; on the right an equally wide pipe never narrows, keeping its full width from end to end.](/figures/jimoty-friction-funnel-en.svg)

Two things pushed the physical spots into being. The first was the ceiling on the online version: roughly ten million people use it, and people who would rather not meet a stranger simply do not, so the service needed a path where you hand something over without meeting anyone. The second was what he saw once his dealings with local governments multiplied — he reckons he has toured more waste-processing plants than most people, and between what the collection crews told him and what he saw himself, the volume of unbroken, usable goods coming through was obvious at a glance. His read on the spot: if someone nearby wanted these things, they would never have arrived here.

![A large circle stands for the whole adult population; inside it a small solid block marks the people using the online service today, and a horizontal line presses down on top of that block; beyond the line lies another area the service can never reach, and a new channel runs around the line and into it.](/figures/jimoty-reach-ceiling-en.svg)

The municipal arithmetic runs the same way. Collection-truck capacity is tight, drivers are short, fuel and labor costs are climbing, and incinerators depreciated over 50 years are heading toward rebuild dates inside the next decade or so. The other host says they often use the bulky-furniture pickup scheme — buy a few-hundred-yen sticker, wait a month — and drew their own conclusion mid-sentence: if you are paying someone to come and take it, better to carry it somewhere a person wants it.

The rules at a Jimoty Spot are strict: no buying, no appraisal, no pricing on intake. The company takes still-usable things people want to pass on, then sells them; it pays for the space and the people, because what Kato wants is a scheme that stands without tax money. Investors suggested taking municipal subsidies, and his hesitation was that a scheme which collapses without a subsidy is still spending public money. He is candid about one tension inside this: the heavier the upfront investment, the stronger the pull to sell donated goods dear — and his goal is to be the cheapest price in town on every item.

![Two bars differ sharply in height; the left one rises from the ground with an extra block of waiting time stacked on top, while the right one barely clears the ground and an arrow beside it points to a pair of hands reaching out to take the item.](/figures/jimoty-disposal-cost-gap-en.svg)

The money side was ugly for a long time. At 15 staff the site drew about ten thousand monthly visitors, and those came from paid advertising — stop the spend and the place went quiet. Then came two years at five people, fundraising where roughly one in a hundred firms would invest, and the same questions every round: how do you make money, why no transaction fee. KDDI's capital and business tie-up in 2012 brought exposure. In 2014 television came calling — someone was giving away a boat, someone else a dentist's treatment chair, the kind of material TV loves — and every appearance knocked their thin infrastructure over. Those outages taught him the magnitude: once he could see that TV-driven users stayed, he could justify paying for commercials. What he wanted was a sound logo you remember after one listen, modeled on the Yamato delivery jingle and on Car Sensor, which he had worked on himself; the recording ran to a fiftieth take before he nodded. Profitable in 2019, listed in 2020 — and his shareholders went from about ten investment and operating companies to several thousand retail holders, after which he says he started thinking of himself as something close to a public figure. The listing also made conversations with local governments easier to start.

One stretch of this episode made me stop. A mother came in with two small children and gave them 200 yen, saying that was the limit. The children wandered the shelves, and what one of them picked was a plate the family eats off; the toys stayed where they were. Kato says he is now working on an idea: with permission, put a photo of the child and that plate up in the store, so that the person who donated it — donors tend to come back — sees that the plate they let go is in daily use.

This October, CCC's tender offer completed. Kato gives two reasons. The problems that show up on the way from 50 stores to several hundred, or a thousand, are problems CCC has already hit once, having opened more than a thousand stores; and both sides frame their aim around durable local culture. The target number, he says, has not changed since the internet-only days: he puts Japan's adult population at about 70 million and wants a service used by 60 million — the kind where people ask how they ever lived without it.

Reaching that volume means the store has to change shape. Most of today's 50 run 50 to 100 tsubo, a bit larger than a convenience store, and inside Tokyo's 23 wards there is one, in Setagaya, with the others out in Hino, Hachioji, and Chofu — the space cost inside the wards, he says, settles the question as soon as you run the numbers. So they ran an experiment in Yokohama: mini-spots placed inside two chain stores, taking goods in with no sales floor. Once intake and selling come apart, the 23 wards no longer require a 50- or 100-tsubo shop, and the ways of collecting things may have many more variations left in them.

## I now read it as a business about the cost of collection

When a good company goes private, something must have gone wrong — that is where my head goes first, and this episode offered another reading.

Start with the business. I would now put the bottleneck on what it costs to get usable goods in the door: how much each tsubo and each staffed shift can take in, and what share of that sells. A negotiation sends away the person who just wants the thing gone, which is why he refuses to buy or appraise. Read along that line, those two Yokohama mini-spots do more than save rent; they test whether collection can grow apart from selling.

Then the delisting. Morimoto says on air that he looked at Jimoty as an investment, liked the profitability, uses and likes the service, and in the end did not buy — and he raises the particular pain of a good business the market will not credit. Kato's framing is about timing: he explicitly rejects the reading that economic growth had become hard, and says what he wanted was to dig the upfront store investment deep in one go, which is hard to do while listed. Laying those two together, the lesson I take is that a public market and a single large shareholder answer the question "should we spend now" differently, and that layer sits apart from whether the business is any good. Next time I see a tender offer, I will look first for what management says about capital spending over the next few years, then check whether that collides with the disclosure pressure of recent quarters.

![On the left a saw-toothed line oscillates back and forth near a horizontal line and never dips far below it; on the right a line first dips deeply and then turns upward, going much deeper than the left one and also ending much higher.](/figures/jimoty-capex-shape-en.svg)

Several things I remain unsure about, left as they are. Per-store economics have never been published, so whether 50 stores scale to several hundred along the same curve is invisible from outside. Space cost in the 23 wards is a hard constraint, by his own account unworkable on the same terms. The split between collection and selling is two stores old, with no evidence at scale. And disclosure stops after delisting, so anyone tracking this has to fall back on proxies — store count, the number of municipal agreements — with wider error bars than financials.

## One thing to take with you

One idea: things move when the cost of dealing with them exceeds the cost of handing them on. Price is friction here — the moment it takes a negotiation, packaging, and a wait for a buyer, plenty of people would rather bin it. Once you see that, you start noticing a whole category of things around you where what has them stuck has nothing to do with what they are worth.

Something I have tried: pick one item today that still works but that you have not touched in a year, write down three places you could hand it to without shipping and without haggling — the neighbor, the shop you always go to, a donation point within walking distance — and give one away today.
