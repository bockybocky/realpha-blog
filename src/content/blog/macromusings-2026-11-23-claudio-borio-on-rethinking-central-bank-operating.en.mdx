The 23 November 2026 episode of *Macro Musings* was recorded inside the vault of the Czech National Bank, where David Beckworth and the BIS's Claudio Borio talk about central bank operating systems. Borio lays out the two poles — a scarce reserve system and an ample reserve system — and the dividing line is whether holding reserves costs a bank anything. His number is the contrast between the Fed's reserve demand of roughly $35 billion back when it ran a scarce system and the roughly $3 trillion central banks now estimate. His claim runs through the whole conversation: the rate spikes and demand volatility we file under exogenous shocks are partly produced by the system in place. He draws the boundaries too — supervisory requirements do hold up a floor under the balance sheet, and on the 2020 dash for cash he says plainly that he has a suspicion and no clear evidence.

## The episode recorded in a vault

The setting has some comedy in it. The Czech National Bank was marking its hundredth anniversary with a conference; during the day Beckworth moderated a panel where the Swiss National Bank, the Riksbank and the New York Fed each described their own operating system. After the sessions the two of them went down into the vault and kept talking.

Borio pushes the technical detail aside first and starts from what the two systems share: either way, the central bank has to make the supply of and demand for reserves meet at the overnight rate it wants. That is where the common ground ends.

In a scarce reserve system the central bank deliberately limits the supply of reserves so the overnight rate sits above the deposit rate, which means an extra unit of reserves on a bank's books costs it something. In an ample reserve system the central bank saturates the market, the overnight rate sinks to the floor, and that cost disappears.

![A two-panel corridor chart: in the left panel the overnight rate sits mid-corridor, well above the lower bound, while in the right panel it is pinned to the lower bound; below them two horizontal bars of wildly different length, the short stub standing for $35B and the full-width bar for $3T.](/figures/scarce-vs-abundant-reserves-corridor-en.svg)

Once the cost is gone, what reserves are changes. In the scarce system they are a settlement medium plus whatever regulation and supervision pile on top. In the ample system, at the margin, they become an asset a bank parks money in. Borio pauses there to make the point that stayed with me: the shape of the demand curve is decided by supply.

![Two panels share one demand curve that bends down and then flattens out: on the left a vertical supply line cuts the steep stretch and the crossing hangs high above the lower bound, while on the right the supply line cuts the flat stretch and the crossing lands on the lower bound.](/figures/same-demand-curve-two-operating-points-en.svg)

## The model he learned at university came back

Borio says the first thing he learned when he started working on these things was that the model he had been taught at university was not how the world worked. That model was the Tobin–Brainard portfolio framework: the central bank pushes a fixed-yield asset into the banking system, and for banks to absorb it they adjust everything else they hold, so the whole spectrum of yields gets pushed around.

A scarce system does not work that way. Transmission is simpler — the central bank influences the overnight rate, expectations about future policy carry the signal out along the curve, and the overnight rate itself barely responds to yields on other assets, as if insulated from them. Borio argues the heavy lifting in a scarce system is done by signalling: the demand curve is so steep as to be effectively vertical, so where you end up depends mostly on where the central bank says it wants you, and open market operations only keep the aggregate inside a range that touches neither ceiling nor floor.

Then he laughs: ironically, we seem to have gone back to the system I learned at university. What the central bank pushes into the system is a fixed-rate asset, and through portfolio effects it moves repo rates and other yields — while the way people discuss the system is still partial equilibrium.

## The moment "ample" turns into an illusion

This is the part of the episode with the most weight in it.

In a scarce system, reserves left idle at the end of the day cost a bank something, so it lends them out. Borio is careful here: he means clearing end-of-day balances, I am short and you are long and we square up, which is separate from interbank trading and market making. In an ample system that incentive is gone; the money sits in the deposit facility. If an incentive to lend does appear, the cost is back and you are leaving the ample system. He calls this a feature of the system, the deliberate result of how it is built.

The consequences hide in the detail. In a scarce system the central bank only has to get the aggregate roughly right and the market handles distribution. In an ample system it has to make sure every single bank is in excess, or somebody slips back into the scarce region. So the interbank market goes quiet, the central bank has to walk the money to where it is needed, and it ends up putting far more reserves into the system, because every bank has to be holding them.

![On the left a ring of banks is wired to one another in a mesh and the central bank has a single arrow pointing at the whole ring, labelled as the aggregate; on the right the mesh lines are gone, the central bank sits at the centre and radiates out to each bank one by one, every bank carrying its own buffer.](/figures/interbank-mesh-vs-central-hub-en.svg)

Then come the episodes that look as if they fell out of the sky. Interbank payment volumes are large, deposits shift between banks in size, and some part of the system is always short on the day. A distribution mechanism idle for years is hard to wake up — institutions close desks, memory evaporates inside the banks and inside the central bank too. Overnight rates spike, and everyone asks: weren't we supposed to have ample reserves? Borio's phrase is that the ampleness turns out to be illusory, and he separates this from the Acharya and Rajan argument about banks writing commitments against that liquidity. His version comes from inactivity in the interbank market alone. The spike does not stop at the overnight rate either; it travels into repo rates and further out. It looks exogenous; the source is the system.

The second consequence lands on the central bank. If the market does not handle distribution, the central bank does, which turns it from a lender of last resort into a lender of first resort. Open market operations cannot steer reserves to the bank that is short unless that bank happens to be the one selling the securities. The more often these episodes occur, the more the central bank feels it has to step in, and Borio describes that as a slow circle of moral hazard. On evidence he stays careful: September 2019 was the first time he heard central bankers say that distribution might have been part of the issue, and in the 2020 dash for cash — when banks were rock solid and nobody doubted their creditworthiness — the sheer scale of what the Fed had to do may have had something to do with distribution, though he labels that a suspicion.

Beckworth adds a real case: Norges Bank ran a floor system from 1993 to 2011, experienced every one of these problems, and went back to a tiered system — all of it before the financial crisis.

What about the other side of the argument? Beckworth puts it bluntly: part of why reserves were so low before 2008 was that reserves paid no interest at all, and that has changed; liquidity coverage rules are not going away either, so there is no going back. Borio's answer has two layers. He agrees with the first: supervisory and regulatory requirements do make banks hold more reserves, and that sets a limit on how far the balance sheet can shrink. His second layer is the point — that is the only limit. Those requirements work like a reserve requirement, affecting the size of settlement balances a bank needs; as long as you roughly know that number, and the formulas are there so you can simply ask the banks what they would hold, the uncertainty and volatility around it need not widen. On top of that, in a scarce system banks economize wherever they can, including on the supervisory layer. Some of the volatility in reserve demand visible today comes from reserves being an asset to park money in. He offers a counterexample as well: plenty of emerging market economies implemented the Basel requirements and still run scarce reserve systems, quite content with them.

![On the left a single column stands on a dashed line marked as the regulatory minimum, with the upper part of the column marked off as the slice the rules add on top; on the right two operating points sit at the same height, one with a narrow wobble range and one with a wide range.](/figures/the-floor-sets-size-not-volatility-en.svg)

The hard part is people. Borio says that when he goes to talk to central banks about this, he finds almost nobody in the room who has operated a scarce system, so the whole thing looks impossibly complicated to them. He says the discovery horrified him and dated him, and then adds: the system was not complicated when we ran it.

## The news calls the repo spike technical — should I care?

You have probably read that headline: overnight rates spiked briefly, analysts call it quarter-end, call it technical, and two days later it is gone. So you leave it alone, and within six months you read it again.

Borio offers a way to read those events. The same spike can have two sources. One is genuinely exogenous — quarter-end, tax dates and bond settlement landing on the same day. The other comes from a distribution mechanism that no longer runs, so when payment volumes swell the banks that are short cannot find anyone to borrow from. The first kind goes away by itself; the second recurs at the same spot and needs a central bank hand each time.

![On the left a flat line throws up one spike in the middle and then settles back to where it was; on the right spikes of the same height pop up three times in a row at the same spot, each with an upward central-bank arrow beneath it.](/figures/one-spike-versus-recurring-spikes-en.svg)

So what I do is stop guessing about any single event and track two things instead: whether it shows up at the same spot again, and whether the central bank's stated reason for intervening is shifting. Borio is clear on the second one — a central bank saying "I come in when I see clear signs of dysfunction" and a central bank saying "I will always be here, so volatility stays inside my comfort range" are two different institutions. The day the second sentence gets said out loud, the system has changed.

## The central bank says it will hold the market up, isn't that good for me?

I have told myself that one more than once: somebody up there has it covered, so my risk is smaller.

The repo passage changed my mind. When a central bank operates in the repo market it has a monopoly over the cash leg and none over the securities leg, and either leg can move the rate. Its counterparties widen from banks to a crowd of non-banks. The bigger shift is that in a scarce system the central bank was a price taker in those markets, and now, operating at a fixed rate with full allotment, it is a price maker. The market it is trying to pin is orders of magnitude larger than the market for reallocating reserves, so the amounts it may have to transact get uncomfortably large.

And repo is the main vehicle for leverage. A commitment to keep volatility in that market inside narrow margins is a put written for everyone levering up in it. Leverage is sized off volatility, so when volatility is held down, leverage stacks higher. Borio treats this as another channel through which fragilities you later read as exogenous were partly grown by how the system is run. His extreme example is the Bank of Canada: its target is CORRA, an overnight secured rate, and the end-of-day transactions banks use to square balances are a tiny slice of that market, with no influence on the rate. As he puts it, the tail is being wagged by the dog, while our models still assume the dog is wagging the tail.

![The left panel shows a wide, undulating volatility band next to a stack just two blocks high; the right panel shows the band squeezed into a thin strip with the stack beneath it rising to six blocks.](/figures/narrow-band-taller-leverage-stack-en.svg)

He also touches the political economy side of the bill: the larger the balance sheet, the blurrier the line between fiscal and monetary policy, and the more loss exposure the central bank carries itself. That thought gets cut off before he finishes it.

## 帶得走的一件事

Part of what you blame on the outside world was grown by your own arrangements. The system decides what demand looks like, and it decides which events keep happening.

Here is something I tried. Take one "accident" you have complained about lately — a colleague who hands you things at the last minute, family who call during your busiest hour, a kid who mentions unfinished homework at bedtime — and do one thing only: write down which of your own moves made that path the cheapest one for them. Maybe you always answer fast. Maybe you only reply in that one window. Write it down; no need to fix it yet.
