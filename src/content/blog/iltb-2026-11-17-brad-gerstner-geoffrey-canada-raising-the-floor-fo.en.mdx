In Invest Like the Best EP.501, released on 17 November 2026, Patrick hosts Brad Gerstner of the venture firm Altimeter and Geoffrey Canada, founder of the Harlem Children's Zone, to talk about the Invest America Act signed into law on 4 July 2025. Brad says on the show that roughly 70 million accounts have already been opened automatically for every child in America under 18, each newborn starting with $1,000 invested straight into the S&P 500 — and that the $1,000 is an appropriation, which a new Congress has to reauthorise every four years, with funding currently running through 2029. Canada, who has spent fifty years educating poor children, arrives at a conclusion education cannot carry alone: for a household with no savings, a broken-down car takes the whole thing down.

## Who is talking, and about what

Patrick opens by remembering a phone call at the height of the pandemic in 2020. Brad described the idea to him for the first time while he paced around his kitchen island, and his read was that it was conceptually interesting and far too hard to pull off. Brad's reply: he wasn't the only one who thought that. Five years later the bill is law. Brad grew up poor in rural Indiana; Canada grew up in the South Bronx, founded the Harlem Children's Zone, and has been profiled on 60 Minutes. The legislation is the Invest America Act, the accounts it creates are 530A accounts in the statute, and most people call them Trump accounts — I'll use 530A account throughout.

The mechanism is short to describe. A child is born, gets a Social Security number, and the Treasury automatically opens a 530A account holding $1,000 invested in the S&P 500. Brad says about ten thousand babies are born in America every day, so ten thousand accounts get created every day. On top of that sits what he calls a big red easy button: parents putting in $5 or $10, companies topping up like a 401(k), a grandparent scanning a QR code.

## Fifty years in education, and the conclusion is that education isn't enough

This is the part of the episode I keep coming back to. Canada says education is the key that unlocks opportunity everywhere except one place: you can get the good schooling and the good job, and with nothing in the bank a single emergency bankrupts you.

![Two parallel paths run into the same break; the upper one has a plank laid across it and continues onward, while the lower one bends down into the pit at the break, leaving only a grey dashed line for the right half.](/figures/education-opens-the-door-savings-crosses-the-gap-en.svg)

He's watching the third generation now. A grandchild calls — Grandpa, the car broke down, I need $1,500 or I can't get to work — and he can cover it. Then he thinks about the kids he has spent his life on: the car breaks and there is nobody to call; an opportunity shows up and you need $500 for a plane ticket to the interview, and there is nobody to call. The Harlem Children's Zone has graduated more than two thousand kids with college degrees and has about a thousand in college right now. One emergency, and a whole life's worth of work is gone.

Patrick concedes the point on air. What he took away from that 60 Minutes segment was that education is the answer, and Canada rewrote that conclusion with his own life: a Bowdoin degree and Harvard, no idea how financial markets worked, raising kids one paycheck from homelessness. How exposed an educated person with no money is, he says, is something most Americans don't understand.

## Opening the account automatically is where the design turns

Brad calls the friction the cold start problem: setting up a custodial brokerage account for a child is hard enough that even the most sophisticated people who worked for him never did it. He borrows Buffett's framing — a small snowball and a long hill — and notes that most people miss the first third of the hill. So the design opens the account and funds it without waiting for the parent. He says that was the architecturally critical decision: a single mother working two jobs who never gets around to claiming shouldn't cost her child ten years of compounding, and if she claims two or three years later, those years still accrued. Claiming itself means downloading the app, entering a Social Security number, verifying identity.

![On a long slope rising from lower left to upper right, two curves bend upward: one starts at the far left, the other starts later and ends clearly lower, while a pale band at the far left marks the missed first third.](/figures/the-first-third-of-the-hill-en.svg)

The parents' first question is the interesting part. Brad describes visiting a school of three thousand kids in the Bronx last week, where they added $250 to each child's account, and the first thing a mother asked was whether she could just Apple Pay $5 or $10 in — she was asking whether saving is really that easy. He also mentions a Jamaican seamstress in New York who stopped him with a stock question because she'd seen him on CNBC, and who was keeping cash under the mattress for her kids. Canada supplies the other side: free money sounds like a scam. When the Harlem Children's Zone ran Wealth Build and asked kids to do something to get the money, nobody showed up, and he thought of those fake lottery stings where everyone who turns up to collect is on a bail-jumping list.

## One penny of interest, and he walked away from capitalism for forty years

This is the best story in the episode. In the 1950s Canada was in grade school and the class was told to save a quarter a month, put it in a bank, and watch it grow. Six months later he had $2, with about a penny of interest. His reaction: this is the stupidest idea I've heard, give me my $2 back, I'm buying potato chips and a soda. That, he says, is where capitalism lost him — and that is how most poor people meet capital.

![On the left, a bar stands for two dollars and the interest on top of it is only a hairline sliver; on the right, two blocks of the same total height are labelled chips and soda, where you can actually see what you got.](/figures/one-penny-of-interest-en.svg)

Which is why the size of the balance decides whether the education means anything. Brad runs the numbers: $1,000 at the start, $50 a month, a bit from a philanthropist and a bit from an employer, and by seventh grade the account holds somewhere around $12,000 to $13,000 — and nobody can pull it out, not the child, not the parents. At that number people start asking questions. Canada names the risk inside the same design: on the day an eighteen-year-old can touch the money, the first thought may be the Rolex he's been eyeing, and the parents may come asking too. Average savings for African-American families, he says, run around $500; a kid with $8,000 or $10,000 in an account can easily become the family's savings account. His fix is to push the education back a generation and teach the parents to save first.

![On the left, a very short bar holding only the seed deposit; on the right, a bar built up in layers from the seed deposit, small monthly contributions and outside matching funds, whose top rises past a dashed threshold line.](/figures/scale-makes-the-lesson-land-en.svg)

## Which half is permanent, and which half gets fought over every four years

This section carries the most information in the episode. Brad splits it in two. Account creation is permanent once written into law, like Social Security, requiring no reauthorisation; repealing it would take a bipartisan majority willing to say they don't want to help kids plus a president's signature, which he puts at roughly the odds of repealing Social Security. The $1,000 at birth, though, is an appropriation, and Congress doesn't write open-ended financial grants, so a new Congress has to reauthorise it every four years. It's funded through 2029, and he wants that half made permanent.

![A thick solid line at the top runs all the way off the right edge of the figure, while the row of blocks below is solid only through 2029 and turns into empty dashed outlines after that.](/figures/permanent-half-and-renewable-half-en.svg)

The other half is the difference between a 530A and a 529. California's CalKids used 529 accounts, where the money can only go to college. A 530A can be used for anything: at eighteen you can take 25% out for a home, a business, college or a trade, and the rest rolls into an IRA. Brad says his advice to Newsom was to block-grant the $200 million going into CalKids straight into the kids' 530A accounts — parents shouldn't be managing five accounts. His reason is scale: build something small and you can't even A/B test it, so you never find the thing that works.

![The box on the left has a single exit leading to college, the box on the right has four exits leading to different uses, and below the right box runs a proportion bar split into one quarter and three quarters.](/figures/one-exit-versus-four-exits-en.svg)

## Philanthropy is stuck on plumbing

Patrick asks who is against this, and the answer runs somewhere I didn't expect. Brad says Republicans were the more suspicious side at first, because it sounded like an expansion of the welfare state, and what won them over was framing it as independence from the state: the accounts are privately owned with right, title and ownership, so if something happens to the child the family keeps the money, where Social Security pays out nothing. Canada adds that some people walk away at the name alone, and compares that to what happened with Obamacare.

Then comes the philanthropy part. Brad says Michael and Susan Dell came to him — public reports put them at $200 billion — wanting to give all of it away within ten years of their passing, and that getting this passed solved their biggest problem: there aren't many organisations in America that can absorb $6.25 billion, which is what 25 million children aged two to ten at $250 each comes to. The process now is a one-page agreement with the Treasury and a wire, and the Treasury distributes it into the accounts. Brad calls it Giving Pledge 2.0: the gap between the pledge and the giving gets squeezed out.

Named on the show: the Dells targeting the bottom 75% of zip codes, Brad's own family foundation putting $250 into every account for under-fives in Indiana, Ray Dalio in Connecticut, Frank Slootman in Montana, Neil Mehta giving $100 to every child in San Francisco. Brad says Gwynne Shotwell gave away 2 million shares of SpaceX to children aged ten to eighteen in the bottom 50% of income zip codes, and that Elon said he would give one share of SpaceX to all 70 million kids, about $11.5 billion. Brad's forecast is that $25 trillion changes hands in America over the next ten to fifteen years, and 10 to 20% of it could land in these accounts.

## The poor get all of the risk and none of the reward

Canada asked the young people he works with what they'd heard about AI: that it might kill everyone, that it will take all the jobs. Nobody had heard it would make life better. When the leaders of the companies themselves say there's some chance it kills us all, he says, you start to wonder whether someone is risking your life and your family's life to make a great deal of money. He lays the trade out plainly: give me $20 million and tell me it might kill us all, fine, I'll take that risk; give me nothing and tell me it might kill us all, and that's a problem. Right now the poor get all of the risk.

![A two-by-two grid of blocks: the risk column is filled in both rows, while the reward column is filled only in the top row and left as an empty outline in the bottom row.](/figures/all-the-risk-none-of-the-upside-en.svg)

Brad's answer detours to 1930. Keynes wrote "Economic Possibilities for Our Grandchildren", coined the phrase technological unemployment, called it a temporary phase, and predicted that living standards in progressive countries a century later would be four to eight times higher. Brad says the actual figure is 8.2 times, at the top of that range, and that 80% of today's jobs didn't exist in 1930. His analogy: the Invest America Act of 2025 is to the age of AI what the Social Security Act of 1935 was to the industrial age — back then the only asset people owned was their labour, so the system insured labour; now people need to own assets. He also says the job displacement is coming, and the bridge has to be built before you can walk people across it.

![A band rises from 1930 toward the upper right, bounded by eight times above and four times below, and the actual outcome of 8.2 times sits just outside the upper edge of the band.](/figures/keynes-four-to-eight-times-en.svg)

## "A policy headline — should I care?" — watch whether the money moves on its own

My first reaction to a policy headline is to discount it, because the path from the announcement to anyone actually benefiting is usually too long to see. This episode handed me a usable test: does the money need a human decision before it moves?

That's where CalKids failed. Newsom told Canada he couldn't give the free money away and didn't understand why. Brad's diagnosis is that someone had to open the account, the money could only be spent on one thing, and the whole pipeline bottlenecked at the parent. The 530A design strips out every step that requires a decision: the account opens at birth, the money goes in by itself, there's no investment choice, cash swept into the S&P 500 the next day, compounding running whether or not anyone has claimed it.

![The upper stream narrows further at each of three gates it passes through, while the lower stream of the same starting width runs at full width all the way, with only dashed empty outlines where its gates were removed.](/figures/does-the-money-need-a-signature-en.svg)

The same test works on companies. If every dollar of a newly claimed demand requires a customer to sign off once, the curve will be a slope; if the flow of money is written into the default and happens without anyone deciding, that's a different animal. When I look at a policy story behind one of my own holdings now, I ask first whether that money's default is to arrive or to wait for a signature.

## "The speakers have skin in this — can I still listen?" — separate the mechanism from the forecast

Both guests have positions. Brad drove this legislation, runs a venture fund, and is long-term optimistic on AI; Canada's organisation is raising money against this exact work. With episodes like this I sort the content into two piles.

One pile is mechanism: the bill signed on 4 July 2025, the 530A withdrawal rules, the $1,000 needing reauthorisation every four years, the 529 restriction to college. This pile is checkable, and once you check it, it's either right or wrong.

The other pile is forecast: $3 to $5 trillion in the accounts in ten to fifteen years, 10 to 20% of a $25 trillion wealth transfer flowing in, AI's benefits outweighing the harms. This pile rests on a chain of premises — philanthropists keep wiring money, Congress keeps reauthorising, the index keeps rising, claim rates get high enough. I hold it as a hypothesis under observation, and for each premise I can name a signal that would break it, like a claim rate that stalls at some number and stops moving.

The mistake I make most often is lending the first pile's checkability to the second. Someone explains a mechanism clearly and accurately, and I slide into believing their numbers about the future, when the evidence behind those two things differs by an order of magnitude.

## The one thing worth taking with you

**More people are stopped by the step from zero to one than by not having the money.**

Canada's $2 story and Brad's cold start problem are the same observation. So is the story Canada closes the episode with. In 1969 he was a high school junior; his social studies teacher and her husband were taking rich kids on a trip through seven European countries and wanted to bring a couple of poor kids along, and it needed $1,000. Someone told him to go talk to a man who might put up the money. He explained why he wanted to go, and the man paid. He went, and the countries he chose were Czechoslovakia and Yugoslavia, because he wanted to see the Iron Curtain with his own eyes — he wanted to know how the train got through it. To this day he doesn't know who the man was or why he did it. He says that trip changed the trajectory of his life, and that after everything he's done he still doesn't think he's paid it back.

Here's something I've tried that works outside investing too. Think of one person you know is stuck — what's stopping them is usually the shape of the first step rather than money — and then do that step for them until only their signature or their tap on "send" is left: fill the form down to the last field, look up the phone number and text it to them along with the first sentence to say, take the registration link all the way to the payment page. Don't ask them first; hand it over done.
