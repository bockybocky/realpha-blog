## What This Episode Is About

Episode 564 of the Caibaogou podcast, released 15 September 2026, is titled "I think every straight guy should get counseling," with clinical psychologist Huang Tien-hao as the guest. He chairs the Chinese Ericksonian Hypnotherapy Association. The host has been in counseling for three or four years and treated this recording as a ninety-minute private lesson.

One line near the top is worth keeping. The host says that over the past week or two he has grown more cautious about the claim in his own title. The reason surfaces later: he has been reading posts by some psychologists online and thought, I would not want to see you. A recommender walking his own recommendation back half a step is rare on air.

## The Main Points

**Guidance, counseling and therapy are three words with three origins, not three severity levels.** Guidance comes from the education system: it assumes nothing is wrong with you, you are simply growing, and someone walks alongside. Counseling addresses being stuck — a career that has stalled, a couple who keep having the same fight, grief after a death. None of those is an illness. Therapy maps to things whose names contain a disorder or a disease. Taiwan's regulations draw this line harder than you would guess: a counseling practice may not legally provide therapy, and any psychologist who assesses a neurotic or psychotic condition must refer the person to a physician — a requirement audited annually by the health authority.

**On when to seek help, Huang offers a set of physical and functional markers.** If one area of life is stuck, go talk to a psychologist; either license works. If the signals lean physiological — sleep changes noticeably, appetite shifts, you turn listless or agitated — start with a psychiatrist or a clinical psychologist for an assessment. The question running through both: has this begun to affect your social and occupational functioning? Errors creeping into your work, a student who can no longer read, a household that stops running, relationships taking damage.

**Dual relationships are barred because three layers already run in every session.** First, the therapeutic alliance: two people willing to face this together. Second, transference and countertransference — you look at him and see your brother, or someone else who mattered, so a sentence of his lands in a way that has nothing to do with his intent. Third, two human beings reacting to each other as people. Holding all three at once is complex enough; add "he is my relative" or "he manages my money" and the working relationship buckles.

**There is no good way to pick a therapist, and both men sigh about it.** Huang says they seriously considered building a matching platform and still have no answer, because quality here is invisible — even colleagues often do not know how each other actually works. Within the profession, seeing clients, speaking, and writing form three circles that rarely overlap, so the psychologists with public followings have mostly stopped taking cases. Looking at the therapeutic approach does not help either: there are four hundred to six hundred named approaches, a dozen or two at the category level, and the fit between a therapist and their approach already carries an element of accident. A therapist matched to their approach is not thereby matched to you. Where the two of them land: start with someone close to home.

**The usable criteria that survive are plain ones.** Travel has to be sustainable, or the process stops. You have to be able to listen to the person — many practices post video, so watch a few minutes first. And through the sessions, your standing as a person has to remain intact: your feelings are treated as yours, you get to choose, and if you ask what he is doing he is willing to explain. Huang adds a boundary: the process will not always feel comfortable, and the discomfort you can still bear is often where growth comes from. The thing to separate out is discomfort from the sense that your own agency is being erased.

**Of everything that makes change happen, the relationship carries the most weight.** The therapist's traits and chosen approach account for less than people assume; the client's own traits and circumstances account for a fair amount; the most stable factor is the therapeutic relationship. A good one does not mean the conversation feels pleasant — it means both sides know the other will put in effort.

**Huang's own framework is "state transition."** A state bundles together feeling, thought, bodily experience, relational stance and the action you want to take. His test: if you can articulate a different thought but nothing moves emotionally, nothing resonates in the body, and no new action suggests itself, then one element changed. That is a change, not a transition. This is where he places the value of hypnosis — it manufactures experience inside the consulting room at low cost, and once a state has shifted, the person learns that their state can shift. The host offers a comparison: knowing how to ride a bicycle and being able to ride one are two different things.

**The line between resilience and numbness is flexibility.** A coping method built from numbness usually works on one predicament only, and it tends to grow the next predicament by itself. A child who learns to go invisible and read faces in a house where he gets hit carries that pattern into adulthood after the house is gone — and there it becomes the source of the problem. Resilience looks the opposite: handling this predicament raises your capacity for the others.

**On AI he splits the answer in two.** The shallow layer: AI is easy to talk to and available at three in the morning when no psychologist is, so more people will start putting psychological material into words. The deeper layer uses football. A professional sport exists because enough amateurs kick a ball around in the street; the base population is what the top rests on. By that logic AI widening the base is a tailwind for the field. But he expects people to eventually find it insufficient, because AI has no body. Experience is grounded in the body — heartbeat, gut, balance, proprioception, the whole class of interoceptive signals that contemporary psychotherapy takes seriously.

## Going Further

### "I know I should cut the loss, and I still can't"

This is where I recognised myself. Huang's line — a person easily repeats a thought picked up from someone else or from a book, then says I know all this but I can't do it — transfers to markets without editing a word. Discipline, scale in, don't average down a broken thesis, don't fall in love with a stock. Everyone who has lost money can recite these.

His explanation: those thoughts sit at the intellectual layer. To land, a thought has to be lived out once, in some scene, with emotion, body and action moving together. Looking back at my own habits, the ones that actually changed did not come from reading. They came from losing money in a way that left a residue. What I read afterwards supplied the name for the stupid thing I had done.

If the mechanism holds, the way I write post-mortems needs to change. "I should have cut sooner" is a thought; writing it ten times leaves it a thought. The alternative is to write the scene back in: where I was that afternoon, what number I was staring at, what my hands felt like, what sentence I said to myself. Put the situation back on the page, and the next time that bodily feeling shows up you recognise it. I am testing this; the results are still open.

### "The mechanism was wrong, and it worked anyway"

One stretch of history in this episode is a pleasure. Hypnosis entered the modern world through Mesmer, whose proposed mechanism was animal magnetism and celestial influence on human beings, in a paper substantially lifted from a friend of Newton's. The French Academy of Sciences investigated, ruled animal magnetism false, and he was pushed out of Paris. The phenomenon survived — others picked it up and replaced the explanation with suggestion. Huang says EMDR now faces the same class of criticism: the treatment effect is recognised, the proposed mechanism is not.

For research work this is the most portable passage in the episode. Whether an indicator works and whether someone's account of why it works is correct are two separately testable claims. The trap I have fallen into is welding them: believing the numbers because the story is coherent, or discarding something still functioning because the story got knocked down.

Once they are separated, the handling is clear. Effect goes to out-of-sample data, in periods you never tuned against. Explanation earns its keep by telling you when to expect failure — a wrong explanation means wrong failure conditions, so you stay in when you should be out. Which is why something that works without a trusted mechanism can be used, at a smaller size, because you are operating without a map of where it breaks.

### "How do you choose something whose quality you can't see"

Picking a therapist is structurally the same problem as many others: results take a long time to appear, the process is unobservable from outside, privacy blocks word of mouth, and fit is personal, so another person's endorsement does not settle it for you. Choosing an advisor, a research source, or whether to keep following someone all look like this.

The episode's approach transfers directly. First, concede the part you cannot judge in advance instead of straining to pick the best one up front. Second, fall back to the lowest observable bar: can you listen to this person, do they respect that you get to hold your own judgement, will they explain what they are doing when asked. Third, run a trial period, and at the start of it agree on the goal and on what improvement would look like.

That third step is the one most often skipped. When the host asks how he would know whether he is making progress, Huang's answer is to ask your psychologist how he evaluates it. Translate that into the research version: has the person you follow ever described how he decides he was wrong? If he has, you have something to check against. If he has not, all that is left between you is a feeling.

Worth keeping too is Huang's take on progress itself. The host's revenge bedtime procrastination has survived four years of counseling. Huang's response: many people hold a perfectionist standard where any remaining symptom means it did not work, which hides the fact that the intensity has dropped, recovery is faster, and frequency is down. Then he asks something sharper — if this thing does not trouble you at all right now, what makes it a goal worth working on.

## The One Thing to Take Away

A changed thought does not count. A changed state does.

Here is something I have been doing. Pick one thing you know how to do and keep not doing, and stop reading anything about it. Instead write down the most recent moment you failed to do it: where you were, what time it was, what was in front of you, where your body was tight, and the first sentence that showed up in your head. Only those five. No critique, no plan for next time. Put it away. The next time that bodily feeling arrives, you will recognise it.
