> Fathers and elder brothers cannot be leaned on forever, and a home region cannot be kept forever; once you are scattered abroad with nobody to shelter you, you must look for it in yourself. As the proverb says: a thousand strings of cash laid by are worth less than a small skill carried in the body.
> —— Yan Zhitui, *Family Instructions for the Yan Clan*, "Exhortation to Study" (Northern Qi, c. 6th century; my own translation)

Yan Zhitui quoted that proverb to push his sons toward their books, on the grounds that a skill in your hands travels and a patron does not. Fourteen hundred years later, the company that puts the world's electronics together was on its own show floor arguing the same thing.

In the 10 November 2026 episode of Supply Chained, "Hon Hai Tech Day: What's Foxconn Working on These Days?", Tim Culpan of Culpium talks with Jon of Asianometry about the Hon Hai Tech Day he had just attended. Tim says that in past years you walked into the Nangang hall and a row of Foxconn-developed electric vehicles met you at the door, while this time he had to go hunting around the side for them; the entrance instead held Dell, Cisco and HP routers, desktops and laptops, with a wall beside them where every single component had been taken out and pinned up as an exploded view. His reading is that the company is rewriting how the world defines it. There is a boundary on that reading: being able to put something on display is not the same as having the business — the same hall also held a long array of server racks, and the product managers Tim asked told him straight out that the racks were empty shells, there for show.

## Somebody else got the entrance

A word first on what Hon Hai Tech Day is. It is Foxconn's annual public showcase, and it used to be held on 18 October — Terry Gou's birthday, although the founder did not start the event. Young Liu, who took over from him, did, and chose that date for the first one as a nod to Gou. Tim says the way the event has morphed over the years mirrors the way Foxconn itself has moved away from the company Gou built.

The old layout was unmistakable. You walked in and the cars stopped you. The lineup grew from one or two vehicles to a bus, a small truck, a van, and something with a sporty look to it. Electric vehicles were the headliner every year.

This year the headliner was replaced. The cars went to the edge of the floor — Tim's phrasing is that you had to hunt for them — and the first thing you saw was three customer names and their physical products. The part he found most interesting was that wall: a Dell desktop pulled apart into motherboard, chassis, chassis components and screws, each piece nailed up like a Lego or IKEA parts diagram, except with the real objects. The Cisco router and the HP machine got the same treatment.

![Two show-floor plans side by side: in past years the entrance faced a large electric-vehicle display while customer products sat deep inside, whereas this year the entrance faces real customer hardware and a teardown wall, with the vehicles pushed into a side corner.](/figures/entrance-swap-floorplan-en.svg)

## The sentence that wall is arguing against

Taking the machine apart in public is an answer to a label Foxconn cannot shake. When Jon describes what the world thinks of the company, he says the image stuck in most people's heads is still the iPhone plant with a hundred thousand young men and women gluing things together — he adds that he knows this still goes on, though a lot of it has been roboticised. That picture is what the wall is up against.

Tim's counter-argument runs along the order of the process. The last step of pretty much any device is final assembly — iPhone, iPad, desktop, AI server, satellite, Cisco router, PlayStation — and most of the hard work happens well before it. He calls final assembly the easier part, then hears himself and walks it back a little: "it's a bit of a stretch to say that's the easiest part." His conclusion is that Foxconn's skill lives in all those components that go inside, and Tech Day is the occasion that lets them say so out loud.

![A long manufacturing bar in which parts and modules take up almost the entire length, final assembly is only a short slice at the end, and outside impressions land solely on that short slice.](/figures/assembly-is-the-last-slice-en.svg)

So the wall's sentence is plain: everything inside a Dell desktop is made by us, and here are its innards. That claim is harder than any self-description like "we are a manufacturing services company," because it can be taken apart, counted, and caught out on the spot.

## Every line at a corporate showcase hangs together, so which one can I believe

This was the question actually running through my head while listening. Presentations, earnings calls and show floors all sound coherent, and I have no way to check them line by line. The episode handed me three tests I can use on the day, and I wrote them down.

First: does this require somebody else's consent? Tim mentions that showing a customer's product means getting the customer's permission first. That HP, Cisco and Dell are Foxconn customers has been public for a long time; letting your own logo and product stand in somebody else's doorway is a different matter — it means those three bought into the story. Saying what you do costs nothing, so it carries nothing. Getting a customer to stand at your door costs that customer risk. I now take this to any company's public claims and ask which sentence needed a third party to nod before it could be said.

Second: does the thing move? In the same hall, the server racks were hollow, and the product manager admitted to Tim there was nothing inside. The button-installing robot, by contrast, moved — and had been deliberately slowed down for him. Tim watched it and said it was slow; the engineer told him they had reduced the speed so he could see it, and at full pace it picks up a button, installs it into the iPhone chassis and picks up the next one in under a second: 60 a minute, with accuracy and quality control they say hold up. A moving exhibit can be interrogated for numbers. A shell can only be looked at.

![A four-step ladder rising from an empty shell exhibit, to saying what you do yourself, to a working exhibit, to a customer putting its logo at your door, with a side note that the cost of a claim rises from zero to someone else carrying the risk.](/figures/claim-cost-ladder-en.svg)

Third: do they sell it? Tim says that as far as he knows these robots are not sold, they are for internal use, and he suspects there may be partners who take them and feed usage back. His phrase is that Foxconn is in the robot business to eat its own dog food, not to be a robot maker. That test corrects the first two: a technology that works, and runs on your own line, is still some distance from being a revenue line. He also says that if Elon knocked on the door asking them to build a humanoid they would entertain the order, but it is not their focus; among their humanoids was one that hooks a patient up for chemo, and Tim, who has been through chemo himself, says this is where the last frontier of human contact sits — you do not want a robot giving you chemo.

## The thing he called coolest was the thing furthest inside

When Jon asks what his favourite was out of everything he saw that day, Tim's answer is precision engineering; the glasses and the robots both rank behind it. He calls it the nerdiest corner of the show: they can make things at nano scale — machine a hole through a piece of metal so that a rod slides through it perfectly, the kind of part that ends up in a server or a car.

Then the sensor. He says it is about the size of the eye of a needle, that you could probably thread it through a standard sewing needle, and that it is a multi-megapixel image sensor. His own words are that looking at it scared the Jesus out of him, because you could insert it into a layer of fabric — the lapel of your shirt — and nobody would notice it was there. He adds later that in use it still has to connect to a module tucked discreetly elsewhere, but the sensor itself is that small.

![An enlarged sewing-needle eye holds a small square sensor that just passes through it, with its true size marked beside as a tiny dot and a larger module tucked away elsewhere.](/figures/sensor-through-needle-eye-en.svg)

I treat that passage as the hardest evidence in the episode. It is the layer that is most difficult to outsource or copy, and the one least amenable to public relations: a nanometre-scale hole and a needle-eye sensor either exist or they do not. Tim has disbelieved VR since HTC first showed it off, and the Apple Vision Pro confirmed that scepticism, yet he came out of this one saying he is becoming something of a convert to the Foxconn robot story. What moved him was the things you can see and measure.

## The rival reading: this year the headliner is servers

Reading the floor plan as a return to essence has a competitor: AI server demand is strong enough this year that the headliner changed with it, and positioning has nothing to do with it.

That reading has support. On the day of Tech Day, Young Liu was due to hold a midday press conference, then gave a few hours' notice that it was pushed back three hours, dragging everyone to the end of the day. The reason was that Sam Altman of OpenAI was in town. Altman and Foxconn go back: he came to Taiwan some years ago under the banner of Terry Gou's charitable foundation and gave a stage presentation. Tim's inference is that this trip was about locking down servers, and media reports put him in Korea a day or two later; Tim also guesses he may have gone to Hsinchu to see C.C. Wei about chip supply, and perhaps met Delta, though both of those are guesses. What is settled is that he met Young Liu, because Liu said so himself.

The thing to notice is that the two readings do not exclude each other. What supports "the headliner follows demand" also supports "the floor is arguing about components" — cashing in on server demand is exactly what the parts capability on that wall is for.

![Two overlapping circles label a rewritten positioning toward parts and a lead role shifting with server demand, with parts capability in the overlap; below, two crossed-out boxes mark the already-excluded explanations of floor space and the EV wind-down.](/figures/two-explanations-overlap-en.svg)

Two other explanations I ruled out. The venue: it is the same hall, the same big room, and in past years the cars stood at the door in that same space, so floor area was not the constraint. And the EV line being quietly dropped: Tim says they are still on their EV bent, nothing has been shut down, and Jon says he still loves the Luxgen n7.

So how would you tell the readings apart? By the share of components made in-house, and by how that whole string of companies develops. Hon Hai itself is the flagship, with FIT, FII, FIH and Ingrasys underneath it, close to a dozen listed entities in all, plus a batch of unlisted ones that are already separate businesses and look like they are waiting to go public. Jon's take is that the trouble with this company is that it does so many things that it cannot say what it is, and he mentions revenue somewhere around US$200 billion — a rough figure of his, not a reported one, though it makes the point about scale making a story hard to tell. The episode gives no number at all for in-house component share, so I am filing this reading as an inference waiting to be checked, not a conclusion.

## A few other things worth remembering

**The AR glasses.** Tim previewed AR glasses Foxconn is building for a client he says he is not supposed to name, then names it: Amazon. He also says they are chasing orders from another company that will do AR glasses in future, which is Apple. His verdict was better than he expected: the popup display was clear and readable without being distracting, head movement worked as a mouse, and the internal battery gave two to three hours of actual active use in a very thin device. Display, glass, battery and componentry were all done front to back by teams inside Foxconn.

**Sharp.** Jon asks whether Foxconn got what it wanted out of Sharp, and Tim says they turned it around well at first and then failed to keep it going forward. His reason is that Sharp is in the end a display company, and the last five to ten years have not been the era of displays; you had to be Samsung-deep in OLED on size, scale and yield, and Sharp was late on all of them. The two of them joke that the company went from sharp to blunt. There is also a lawsuit attached: a senior executive who went to run Sharp is now in a dispute with the company because he feels he deserved more for turning it around.

**Public relations.** Tim says that under Terry Gou there was no PR team, and the only spokesperson you could talk to was a crusty old chain smoker who barely wanted to speak to reporters — every listed company in Taiwan must have a spokesperson, and he drew the short straw. Since Young Liu took over, former journalists have been hired into the PR team and real time and energy go into ESG. Tim's judgement is that the company cares about its image now, and still has a long way to go to repair it. Jon adds a structural reason: as long as it stands behind its customers doing their work, it takes the arrows first, which makes the image hard to repair.

## The One Thing to Take Away

One idea: whose products sit at your entrance is an organisation's definition of itself, and the version of it that carries weight is the version somebody else has to agree to. Saying what you are costs nothing, so it tells you almost nothing. Getting a customer to lend you their product with their logo still on it, at your door, means they are carrying the risk of you going wrong, and that single act argues better than a deck. What makes Foxconn's teardown wall useful is that it does two things at once: it comes apart and can be counted, and the name hanging beside it belongs to somebody else.

Something I have tried that works today: write down three identities or achievements you tend to mention when you introduce yourself, then put one person's name beside each — who would say that thing about you unprompted. The lines where you can write a name are the ones that stand up. The lines where you cannot are the ones only you have agreed to so far. One of mine came out blank, stayed blank for two weeks, and I took it out of my introduction.
