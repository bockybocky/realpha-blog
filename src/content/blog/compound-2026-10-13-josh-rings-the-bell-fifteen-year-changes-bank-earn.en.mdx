On the October 13, 2026 episode of *What Are Your Thoughts* (from The Compound), Josh Brown rang the NYSE opening bell to mark fifteen years on CNBC's *Halftime Report*, and then tallied up what those fifteen years did: total US market value went from $11.9 trillion to $75 trillion, while the US economy went from $15 trillion to $32 trillion. In the same episode he and Michael Batnick worked through that morning's bank earnings and the structure beneath the surface — Goldman Sachs' Michael Sani says that by their preferred measure, breadth has deteriorated to its weakest level since the dot-com bubble, and he still favors a higher S&P. The condition that bullish read rests on comes from Fidelity's Denise Chisum: since 1926, weak participation has usually been followed by strong returns, provided no recession follows.

> Year upon year, the blossoms look the same; year upon year, the people are not.
>
> —— Liu Xiyi, "Lament for the White-Haired Old Man" (Tang dynasty, c. 7th century; translation mine)

## His Hand on the Bell

The show didn't open on markets. Josh put up a stack of photos first: himself on the balcony above the NYSE floor with his hand on the opening bell, then host Scott Wapner signing the wall, then his own signature. The exchange brought out the book of everyone who has ever rung the bell, and they signed that too.

All of it was for one thing: the first *Halftime Report* aired in October 2011, and this week made fifteen years. Josh was 34 on the premiere and he's 49 now, which led to the line I kept thinking about afterward — the market opinions of a 34-year-old and a 49-year-old are a long way apart.

Then came the receipts. Market value up sixfold, the economy up twofold. The Dow up 360%, the S&P up 573%, the Nasdaq from 2,479 to north of 27,000, a gain of 1,005%. The Russell 2000, the small and mid-cap index, up only 329%. Gold up 154%, silver 90%. Crude up 8% across fifteen years, and Henry Hub natural gas down 6%. ETF assets went from $954 billion to $16.3 trillion.

![Four bars stand side by side: the market-cap pair jumps from very short to the tallest of all, while the economy pair only rises from short to middling, so the gap between the two pairs is obvious at a glance.](/figures/market-cap-vs-economy-fifteen-years-en.svg)

Batnick got stuck on the first number and said he couldn't believe it three times running. Later he added the most honest line in the episode: across those fifteen years, there was never a day when he felt the market had a lot higher to go. Josh's answer was that his whole contribution to the show has been one sentence — stop crying and buy more stock.

![Eight horizontal bars run from longest to shortest: the tech index is the longest, gold and silver leave only a short stub, crude oil is barely visible, and natural gas crosses to the left of the zero line.](/figures/fifteen-year-asset-returns-en.svg)

## "The Next Apple Is Apple"

Then Josh put up the top five companies from August 2011: Apple, Exxon, Microsoft, IBM, Walmart. Apple was $350 billion, and he said they thought the number was outrageous at the time. Today Nvidia alone is worth four times those five combined.

![On the left, five equally sized small squares sit in a row; on the right, a single square covers four times their combined area, its sides clearly twice as wide.](/figures/top-five-2011-versus-one-today-en.svg)

Batnick told a story here. In 2013, with Apple at $500 billion, a family member who had held the stock for years told him, "the next Apple is Apple." He thought it was the dumbest thing he had ever heard, and it made him wonder whether this relative understood anything at all. Apple went on to 10x.

He told it against himself. I stopped there and thought: his reaction followed common sense — $350 billion already felt absurd, so $500 billion felt more so. The slip was reading "already large" as "can't get larger," which skips one step, the step where you check whether the company's earnings are growing faster than its size.

A second set of numbers from the same segment attaches to this. Technology went from about 20% of the S&P to 37%, above the 34% dot-com peak; energy fell from 12% to 2%, down to the size of real estate and utilities. Josh added a note to his future self when he got there: you're watching energy stocks run now, so remember this can go further and last longer than you think possible.

## The 401(k) Column

This was the segment I sat with longest. US millionaire households went from 10 million to 23.6 million, with 440,000 added last year alone. Batnick's split: 85% of that growth from the stock market, 15% from real estate and everything else.

Then Josh put up a number that turns the same story over. The share of Americans who own stock hasn't climbed in a line: 57% in 2011, down to 52% between 2013 and 2016 — the lowest Gallup has recorded — then back up to 62% in 2024 and 2025. The top 1% of households own half of all stock. The bottom 50% own 1%.

![Two horizontal bars of equal length sit one above the other: the top one is split by number of households and the bottom one by stocks held, and their dividing lines line up nowhere, so a sliver on the left faces a large share and a large share on the right faces a sliver.](/figures/who-holds-the-stocks-en.svg)

One column is the exception, and Batnick pointed at it: the 401(k). Retirement balances went from $2.9 trillion to nearly $11 trillion, 70 million people contribute every month, and everyone gets the same annual limit. He said the equality inside that column is about as close to even as he has seen in this market.

![The three bars on the left differ wildly in height, while the three on the right stand level and press against the same dashed ceiling line, making the contrast between the two sides stark.](/figures/the-401k-column-en.svg)

## Back to That Morning: Good Earnings, Ugly Tape

With the bell segment done, the show turned to bank earnings, and Josh's first words were that it felt like Groundhog Day — every quarter sounds about the same.

Citigroup fell 5.6% that day, the one outlier of the four. The reasons listed: investment banking fees down 13% from the prior quarter, the largest quarterly decline among the banks; a reserve build bigger than last quarter's; non-interest revenue down 4%; a revenue beat that barely beat, against a stock that had been up as much as 38% on the year. Josh added the chart read — it failed at the 50-day, then violated the 200-day.

Goldman Sachs was the blowout: $17 a share against $13.65 expected, revenue of $18.68 billion, the second-highest quarter ever, equities revenue up 72%, equity financing up 96% to a record, advisory fees of $1.7 billion also a record. JPMorgan raised full-year net interest income guidance to $106–107 billion, money that comes straight from higher rates. On the other side, analyst Mike Mayo pressed on where the AI benefits show up in the release, and CFO Jeremy Barnum said they aren't visible yet, while correcting the cost figure — roughly $700 million for the year, not the $2 billion Mayo cited.

Goldman gapped up 4% and closed on the lows of the day. Josh's summary: bank earnings were good to very good, and the market's reaction that day sucked.

## Index at Highs, Stocks Dropping Out One by One

This was the densest stretch of the episode. Sani's words: by their preferred measure, breadth is now at its weakest since the dot-com bubble, and the headline index masks how difficult the tape has been underneath. Morgan Stanley's Mike Wilson supplied a mechanism — rising Treasury yields rewrote the broadening script, and the de-rating in the average stock has run well ahead of any deterioration in its earnings.

The numbers: the equal-weight index sits 4.5% off its all-time high while the S&P is at one. The top five stocks are worth $25 trillion, equal to numbers 67 through 500 combined. So those four hundred-odd names in the middle can take turns falling apart while the index prints highs. Among stocks above $2 billion in market cap, 146 technology names are down 30% from their 52-week highs, 48 of them down 50%. Consumer names fill a list of their own: Flutter down 70%, then Lululemon, Nike, Chewy, Cava, Celsius, Las Vegas Sands. In Europe, LVMH is 57% off its high. Domino's posted same-store sales growth of 0.3%.

![Five large squares sit on the left and more than four hundred small dots are packed into a block on the right, and the two blocks look equal in total area.](/figures/five-blocks-versus-four-hundred-dots-en.svg)

Batnick offered an angle I hadn't considered: part of what's hitting these brands is that consumers now have more choice. Lunch in New York used to mean Chipotle by default; now the street is full of new places and people care more about what they're eating. The other side of the same list is working: Amazon, Best Buy, Marriott, Hilton, Williams-Sonoma, Urban Outfitters.

Then came Chisum's research, which flips the intuition. She tested the belief that shrinking participation means the bull market is ending against data back to 1926, and found the opposite: forward returns after weak breadth skew strong, and they're strongest at both extremes of participation. She also flagged what's odd this time — historically, when breadth got this weak, the market had already fallen 5% or more about 97% of the time. It hasn't here. Bloomberg's Jessica Menton measured the same thing another way: the S&P has gone 53 sessions without a 1% decline, the longest stretch since January 2020. Josh's own stance is that he expects a shakeout in October and doesn't think this is the moment to turn bearish.

## Follow-Up One: Earnings Were Fine, So Why Is It Down

This is the grievance retail investors run into most: the headline reads well, the numbers beat, and the position is red. Goldman was exactly that on the day — up 4% intraday, closed at the low.

The episode offers two layers. The first is price having already run: Citi had been up as much as 38% on the year, so the expectation was written into the price, and when the actual print came in merely good, the difference came back out. The second is yields as a common factor. Wilson's line — de-rating running ahead of earnings deterioration — means part of this leg down didn't come from businesses getting worse, it came from the discount rate moving. Those two layers call for different actions: if price ran first, go back and check what you assumed when you bought; if the discount rate moved, go check whether earnings estimates followed it down. Wilson thinks they haven't, which is where he sees room to catch up.

The mistake I've made is netting those two together. I saw red, assumed something broke, cut the position — and found later that the quarter's earnings estimates had never been revised down once. Now I check one thing first: how much of this drawdown is shared by everything in the same bucket, and how much belongs to this name alone.

![A long downward drawdown bar is cut by a horizontal line into two segments, the upper one marked as shared by similar stocks and the lower one as belonging to this name alone, and each segment leads to a different course of action.](/figures/split-the-drawdown-en.svg)

## Follow-Up Two: It's Down 40% — Buy More?

The episode closed by grading a year-old exercise. Twelve months earlier they ran six names through "value or value trap": Lululemon, Salesforce, Nike, Chipotle, Airbnb, Adobe. A year on: Lululemon down 43%, Nike down 43%, Adobe down a third, Chipotle down 23%, Salesforce round-tripped to flat, and only Airbnb up 40%. Josh put it plainly: turn this into a system — buy whatever got killed — and you'll lose money over time. He buys winners instead, and he says in the same breath that his way doesn't always work either.

![Six bars sit on a zero axis: five point downward at varying depths and one points upward, with only the rightmost one above the zero line.](/figures/one-year-later-six-names-en.svg)

Batnick's addition matters more to me: apart from Airbnb, not one of those six staged a meaningful rally all year, not even the appearance of a comeback. Down 30% doesn't mark a bottom, and a single-digit P/E doesn't either.

What I do now is split the self-soothing into two questions. First, is there a nameable thing that would reverse this decline — new management, a new product, a rule change, a different cost structure? If I can't name it, it doesn't count. Second, if I can't name it, what am I waiting for? I held one name for eighteen months, and the honest answer was that I was waiting to get back to my cost basis, which has nothing to do with the company.

## The One Thing to Take With You

What this episode is really about is how "already large" and "can't get larger" get treated as the same sentence. That's the error Batnick made in front of a $500 billion Apple, and everyone waiting on a stock that's down 40% makes its mirror image — treating "already fell a lot" as "won't fall more." Both take a level and read it as a direction.

![Two mirrored panels sit side by side: in one the dot rests at a very high level and in the other at a very low level, each with a dashed arrow pointing up and another pointing down, so the level itself points nowhere.](/figures/a-level-is-not-a-direction-en.svg)

Here's something I've tried, if you want it: pick one thing you're currently waiting to turn around. It can be a stock, or it can be a friendship that went cold, a project parked in place, a job you're enduring. Then write one sentence — the specific event that turns it around, who does it, and when. If you can't write that sentence, the level itself is what you're waiting on. I've done this twice. Once I could write it, and the thing did move. The other time I got to the third line and realized I was waiting on my own cost basis, and I dealt with it that week.
