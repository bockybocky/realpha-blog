> —— Bai Juyi, "Letter to Yuan Zhen" (Tang dynasty, 815; my translation)

On the 23 October 2026 episode of Bloomberg's *Odd Lots*, Tracy Alloway and Joe Weisenthal talked to Jeanna Smialek, Brussels bureau chief at *The New York Times*, about her book *The Invisible Hand of Maria Edgeworth*. Edgeworth was a Regency novelist whose heyday ran from 1795 to 1815; she outsold Jane Austen, and Byron grumbled in his letters that she had replaced him as the lion of the season. Today even people who have read a history of economic thought have mostly never heard of her. One stretch of the conversation stayed with me: Edgeworth and David Ricardo traded letters arguing over whether Ireland should specialise entirely in potatoes, and in those letters Ricardo attached a risk caveat to his own theory of comparative advantage — a caveat that never appeared in anything he published, and so never entered the canon.

## Who this episode is about

Smialek used to cover the Federal Reserve for Bloomberg and now covers the European Union. She found Edgeworth about a decade ago, while trying to fill in a background she lacked — she had no economics degree at the time — by reading Robert Heilbroner's *The Worldly Philosophers*. That book marches through Adam, Bob, John, Marx: a procession of men. Buried in it is one aside saying Ricardo was close friends with a woman named Maria Edgeworth, probably the first woman to think about economics. The aside appears only in later editions, so plenty of readers never saw it. Smialek googled the name, discovered this unknown woman had outsold Jane Austen, and went down the rabbit hole.

Tracy's description of the book: Bridgerton for nerds. She said the one thing Bridgerton was missing was agricultural productivity and rent-seeking by the wealthy.

## She put economics in novels because she was barred from papers

In Edgeworth's day a woman could not publish in academic journals. Her workaround was to write her arguments as fiction. Her first book, *Letters for Literary Ladies*, is a set of invented letters debating whether women should enter the labour market, built directly on *The Wealth of Nations*, published not long before.

For harder material she borrowed men's names. Her one known piece of academic writing was published anonymously — we know it existed only from surviving letters, and she insisted on not being credited. A draft essay on education at Oxford was meant to go out under her father's name: the manuscript is in her handwriting with his notes, and the notes suggest the ideas were hers. She never used a male pen name. She used real men's names.

![Three channels run rightward from "her ideas," but only the top one reaches a box bearing her name; the other two end at "anonymous" and at a real man's name.](/figures/edgeworth-three-byline-paths-en.svg)

## Her famous title was lifted from Adam Smith

*Castle Rackrent* takes its title straight from *The Wealth of Nations*, where Smith uses "rack rent" for a rent set too high for the tenant to make a profit. Smialek says the book is in large part a restatement of Smith, and that literary historians mapped the borrowings long before she did.

What Edgeworth added was ground. Smith wrote abstractly; she applied him to running an Irish estate, which turns the abstraction into a list of operational questions — where do you set rents, how do you pay wages, how do you get workers to produce more, how do you avoid squandering the estate into the ground. She was Anglo-Irish gentry writing about the thing she managed daily.

Tracy drew the line to now: she and Joe both live in New York and walk past shopfronts that have sat empty for years, which makes you want to ask the landlord why he doesn't just cut the rent. Joe said he has asked many times, always gets told it's banks and taxes and valuations, and still doesn't understand it.

## The potato letters: Ricardo's caveat on his own theory

Edgeworth visited London often and was friends with both Malthus and Ricardo. Smialek describes her correspondence with Ricardo as friendly and permanently argumentative, and the central argument was whether Ireland should go all in on the potato.

The positions are the interesting part. Edgeworth argued for total specialisation, using Ricardo's own comparative advantage: Ireland grows potatoes well, so grow potatoes and trade for everything else. She handed his theory back to him. Smialek thinks she may have been playing devil's advocate, and if so it worked, because Ricardo wrote back unconvinced — the potato is prone to failure, and the profit from that much specialisation isn't worth leaving yourself able to starve a nation.

![Two people stand on opposite sides with two arrows crossing between them: she takes his theory and goes all in on potatoes, while he writes back that the risk is too great.](/figures/potato-letters-crossed-positions-en.svg)

In private, Ricardo complicated his own idea, and conceded in effect what Malthus had been arguing at him for years. Then he caught an ear infection and died. None of that reasoning appears in his published work; what entered the canon was the simple view of trade. A few years later Ireland had its famine.

## The famine changed her mind, eighty years before Keynes

Up to the famine Edgeworth's writing shows a committed free trader and laissez-faire believer who opposed handouts to the poor and read Smith close to literally.

Then people in her part of the country started dying. Ireland had two famines in succession; the first began shifting her views and the one everyone knows about moved them a long way. She came round to the idea that in an extreme crisis the government has to spend, and sometimes hand things out directly, to get the economy moving again — an early version of what we'd now call Keynesianism, about eighty years before Keynes developed it.

![A timeline begins with famine and her change of position, runs through a long blank stretch marked eighty years, and only then reaches Keynes at the right end.](/figures/eighty-year-gap-en.svg)

Her deathbed scene made me stop. Asked whether the most famous author in the English language wanted to write an autobiography, she declined, but offered to dictate projects she never got to. The first was that somebody should study the potato as a national food for Ireland. She suggested starting with Ricardo's letters to her.

## She was right about value, and her nephew got the credit

She broke with Smith on one thing. The classical economists, Ricardo and Malthus included, treated labour as the basis of value. Edgeworth backed utility: lawyers and accountants produce nothing physical, and because they make people's lives better they create value anyway. That is the answer economics uses today.

In the fiction she says it quietly — the character who ends up winning in *Castle Rackrent* is trained as a lawyer. Her father's correspondence says it plainly, including a line to one of the Benthamites that this concept could correct some errors in Adam Smith.

Then the uncomfortable turn. Her nephew was F. Y. Edgeworth, of the Edgeworth box in every intro micro course. Smialek notes academic work suggesting his utilitarian ideas probably originated with her.

![An idea starts from her, but a dotted line routes around her grey box while the solid arrow of credit lands straight on the nephew and the framework named after him.](/figures/utility-credit-bypass-en.svg)

## How economics turned into mathematics

Joe asked when economics stopped being political economy — stories, something close to moral instruction.

Smialek points at the marginalists of the mid-1800s, the group that imported utility theory, her nephew among them. They wrote down models and claimed the models described the world. The motive she names is physics jealousy: scientists were being taken seriously, economists wanted the same, and they wanted university posts — Malthus was one of very few academic economists at the time. They founded the Political Economy Club and invited none of the women then working in economics. By Alfred Marshall's textbook, the "political" had been dropped and political economy became economics.

![A circle holds many small dots standing for club members, one lone dot sits outside it for her, and an arrow below the circle points to a box marked canon.](/figures/the-club-that-became-the-canon-en.svg)

Her own conclusion is restrained: economics has rules, and they are not physics. Every few years a model that was supposed to hold absolutely starts breaking down and everyone reappraises. She thinks we can lean too hard on DSGE and forget the subject is philosophy at the root.

## Reader problem one: why won't the market credit what I got right

The investing version: I did the work, I verified the thesis, and the market won't price it, while a simpler story next to it runs up.

Edgeworth's case argues against "good work gets found eventually." Several causes stack: her didactic style aged badly once fiction turned naturalistic, her hardest work went out anonymously or under other names, and she was excluded from the club that became the discipline. None of those is a quality problem. They are **record-keeping** problems — who writes it down, which ledger it lands in, and whose ledger later becomes the canon.

What I do about it is treat "is the thesis right" and "can the thesis be traced to me" as two separate jobs. The first takes research; the second takes timestamped records — what you judged, when, at what price, under what assumptions, and how it turned out. I used to do only the first, so every time the market proved me right I had a memory and no record, and after a while I couldn't tell whether I had actually thought it through.

## Reader problem two: how much should I trust a clean-sounding principle

"Specialise where you have an edge." "Hold for the long run." "Diversify." These share a property: the version that travels is the version with the caveats removed.

The potato letters are clean evidence. Comparative advantage reached us as one sentence. Ricardo's own caveat — specialising to the point where a crop failure starves a nation isn't worth it — stayed in private letters, never reached his books, and never reached a textbook. Simplification is what lets an idea travel, and caveats don't travel, because a caveat has no slogan shape.

So when a principle sounds clean I now do two extra things. First, ask what its failure condition looks like — under what circumstances does following this hurt me — and if I can't answer, I only have the slogan. Second, go read the author's letters, interviews and late revisions, which is where the reservations they never published tend to sit. Edgeworth spent one of her last requests telling people to read Ricardo's letters to her. She knew what the canon had dropped.

## Reader problem three: which layer of the AI wave to watch

Smialek says this moment feels like 1800. The Industrial Revolution hasn't arrived but you can see it coming: division of labour spreading, factories getting efficient, London industrialising, agriculture freshly more productive. Meanwhile the French Revolution has just happened, everyone fears uprisings, Ireland rose in 1798. Economic force and political rupture collide, the world needs a new language to explain itself — and modern economics grew out of that need.

Today, she says, AI shows up in the data without having caused the seismic shift yet, while it is already changing how economic actors relate to each other, populist waves run across Europe and the US, and the global order reshuffles.

![Two parallel columns run side by side, with three paired rows covering new technology, political upheaval and the reordering of the existing order, one box on each side per row.](/figures/eighteen-hundred-and-now-en.svg)

What I keep thinking about is the weight of her next point. Edgeworth's answer to upheaval was comprehension: if you understand what is happening around you, you have some mobility. Smialek maps that onto companies pushing everyone to learn AI. My own read is that the transferable part is reading the structure, not catching up with the tools. Tools turn over every six months; structural questions move every few years — which layer is the bottleneck, who collects the rent, whose job goes from judgement to repetition. The people of 1800 couldn't pick the winning factory, but the ones who understood division of labour had more choices.

## One Thing to Take With You

**Whether an idea survives depends on whether it sits in the record later generations open.** Ricardo thought more carefully than he published, Edgeworth wrote more than she could sign, and what both of them left out vanished together for eighty years.

Here is something I've tried that you might want too. Take one judgement you made recently — a job change, a school, an expensive purchase, a conversation you finally had with someone — and spend ten minutes writing three lines: what you knew at the time, what you were betting on, and what would show you were wrong. Anywhere works; a phone note is fine, as long as you date it. Come back in six months and you'll know whether you saw it or got lucky. I started doing this two years ago, and the hit rate I found on rereading was a long way from the one I remembered.
