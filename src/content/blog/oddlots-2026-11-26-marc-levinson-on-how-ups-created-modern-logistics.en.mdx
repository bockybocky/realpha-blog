In the Bloomberg Odd Lots episode released on 26 November 2026, historian and economist Marc Levinson discusses his new book *The UPS Man*, about UPS founder James E. Casey and the creation of modern logistics. What put the company on its feet, he says, was a central sorting station — an organisational arrangement. During the First World War the federal government counted roughly 200,000 Americans delivering goods for retailers, and UPS could move the same parcels with fewer people, which is why retailers handed the work over. UPS stayed private from its 1907 founding until it listed in 1999, and it still keeps outsiders out of its archives; Levinson's request was refused. He also draws a line: he has not studied UPS's business today.

## In 1907, posting a parcel of books meant hitching up the horse

Marc Levinson is a historian and economist based in Washington, D.C., and he has been on Odd Lots before to talk about *The Box*, his history of container shipping. Tracy Alloway opens by saying James E. Casey is no household name the way a Sam Walton is; Joe Weisenthal adds that if logistics has a household name at all, it is FedEx's Fred Smith. Levinson's reason for picking him is simple: the man built a company central to globalisation, and nobody had ever written his biography.

The difficulty was material. A private company answers to neither investors nor the public, Casey kept a low profile, and UPS does not like outsiders in its archives. Levinson's comment on that stayed with me: a closed archive lets a company shape its history to suit the culture it wants, so a good deal of mythology has collected around UPS, and clearing it away is what the book sets out to do.

Joe asks him: back in 1906, living in another town, what were my options for sending Tracy five or six pounds of books? The answer is that it was hard. There was no parcel post; the post office was limited by law to items of a couple of ounces. What remained were express companies like American Express and Wells Fargo Express, or finding somebody to carry it for you. The railroads moved things only as far as the station, and most Americans lived in the country, so you hitched up your horse and rode into town to collect it. Tracy asks how you knew the parcel had arrived — no text message — and the answer is that you went and checked every day, or had a neighbour look in. Farm groups pushed the government on this for 20 years before Congress agreed to a parcel post in 1912. The Sears and Macy's catalogues existed before that, but their business was small, because their goods also stopped at the railway station; once parcel post started, the catalogue business exploded.

## The real breakthrough was a sorting station

Tracy puts Casey's move plainly: gather parcels from everywhere into one place, sort them, then send them out along routes, replacing the one-item-one-trip run from A to B. It looks almost comically simple now, but the messenger companies of the day worked exactly that way — a store telephoned to say there was a parcel for an address on such a street, and a messenger carried it there. As the company grew, that approach came apart.

![On the left, five origins each run a separate line to five destinations and the lines cross into a tangle; on the right, the five origins first gather into one box, which then fans out along two routes.](/figures/oddlots-ups-one-trip-each-vs-central-sort-en.svg)

Making the central station work meant building a physical sorting floor and then persuading everyone involved to accept something counterintuitive: your parcel would not go straight to the recipient's house but into the station first, mixed in with other people's parcels, and handed to a driver covering a whole route that day. Levinson says Casey leaned this way partly because he was fascinated by scientific management — the business buzzword of the early twentieth century, Frederick Taylor's idea that there is one best way to do everything and you have to find it scientifically. It started in steel and reached small companies through a large literature. Casey's version was to break the day's motions apart: what does a wagon driver do all day, and which part of it can be cut?

Applied to vehicles, the detail is fun. Tracy mentions the passage where Casey's partner insists their Model Ts carry the best tyres, because that is what brings maintenance costs down. Levinson says this is scientific management too: how often a tyre went flat, whether to rotate side to side or front to back, how often to check the grease cup so it never ran dry — they studied each one.

## A dozen stores' parcels on the same truck

Joe asks a lovely question: how much of UPS's early success was feeding on the fact that cities had some density? Levinson's answer sets out the economics cleanly. From the 1910s to the 1960s, most of UPS's business was delivering for retailers. Many department stores had been delivering for themselves, but one store sold to perhaps a dozen people along a route, while UPS carried parcels from a dozen stores and had several times as many customers in the same area. More packages per mile, and the unit cost comes down.

Joe turns it into a picture at the end of the episode, easier to remember than any cost formula: ten people on a street all have parcels due today, and sending ten companies down it with one parcel each makes no sense; one truck delivering ten parcels for ten stores does.

## The First World War drained the retailers' delivery men

This step was pushed from outside. The United States entered the war in 1917 and the federal government needed people — for the armed forces, for the shipyards — so it went hunting for waste, and one of the first places it looked was retail delivery. It estimated that about 200,000 Americans, all men, were delivering goods for retailers. The population was far smaller then, so this was a large block of labour to free up.

![On the left, a tall column stands for the 200,000 people who delivered for retailers, with two arrows draining from its top toward the army and the shipyards at the upper right; on the right, a much shorter column stands for the people UPS needed to move the same volume.](/figures/oddlots-ups-wwi-drains-delivery-labor-en.svg)

Retailers were squeezed from both sides: their delivery men were drafted or left for shipyards that paid far better, while the stables, garages and mechanics still had to be kept. UPS could move the same goods with fewer people, so the labour shortage hurt it less, and the business came to it. After the war everything turned again: recession, a weaker labour market, and unions that had grown strong in wartime pushed aside in the big cities. Casey and his partners used the moment to expand — Seattle was the only large city in Washington state then, so they went first to Oakland, then to Los Angeles, the fastest-growing metropolis in the country, bought small trucking companies, converted them into parcel companies and built a large sorting hub.

## The contract said: if the parcels aren't ready, the driver leaves

One key to UPS's early footing, Levinson says, was that it made customers sign contracts, and alongside rates those contracts wrote in the conditions that kept the system running. Pickup time is the example: UPS trucks came round to collect retailers' outgoing parcels, and the store had to have them ready at the agreed hour; if they weren't, the driver's instruction was to move along, because the whole operation could not wait. That was not routine practice in 1920.

What the retailer got in exchange was a lower rate, which Tracy pins down. The layer Levinson adds is worth more: for a retailer, running deliveries is a distraction — merchandising is the business, yet money goes into garages or stables and people have to be hired to tend the horses and the vehicles. What made them reluctant to let go was advertising: a truck with the store's name on it pulling up outside a customer's house told the whole street where that family shopped. It was an expensive form of advertising, so one by one they gave it up. And once the stables and garages were sold, going back to self-delivery was no longer easy; with five-year contracts on top of that, UPS admitted it had next to no competition in the cities where it operated.

## He asked California's regulator to forbid him from carrying freight

This is the most counterintuitive stretch of the episode. UPS was not subject to economic regulation early on, because what the states regulated were common carriers — truck lines holding themselves out to the public — while UPS ran contract deliveries for particular retailers. A common carrier needed approval to start, approval for what it carried, which routes it ran and what it charged.

The exception was Southern California, where the trucking companies UPS bought already held common carrier licences. Casey's move was to go to the state regulator and ask to be prohibited from carrying certain cargo. His reasoning: only small parcels were efficient, because a single driver could handle them without a second man on the truck, so he did not want large freight. Tracy's reaction made me laugh — you rarely see anyone say "we don't want to carry these, we're a parcel company." Most trucking companies were fighting to widen their authority.

The argument that persuaded the regulator was that parcel delivery is an essentially different business from trucking, and that sentence bought him something nobody else had: other trucks were assigned routes and had to stay on the highway, while Casey's trucks could deviate several miles into residential streets to deliver to the door. California recognised it first, and as UPS expanded, other states followed.

## Promotion from within bought loyalty, and a company with no computer people

In the 1950s Casey began worrying about the decline of downtown shopping, and UPS sought permission to carry general parcels across state lines, moving toward what it is today. Newer companies such as Federal Express came up around then with more advanced technology — barcoding, for one. UPS fell behind for institutional reasons rather than financial ones: Casey believed in promotion from within, so when a position opened they found a driver who wanted to move up into supervision and management. Many people stayed with UPS for decades, which was the benefit; the cost was a company with nobody who understood computers, while technologists looking at a trucking company could not see an opportunity there and did not want to come.

![Inside a closed circle a staircase climbs from driver to manager, while outside the circle three arrows point at its rim and each is stopped by a cross on the boundary.](/figures/oddlots-ups-promote-from-within-closed-circle-en.svg)

Casey himself is still a mystery by the end of the book. Charity mattered to him, but he did not get personally involved — he set up the Annie E. Casey Foundation and funded it, and that was that. The reason he gave most often was that the company had to grow, because growth meant more opportunities for the people who worked for him. Why he was so obsessed with parcels, Levinson says he still does not know after writing the book: parcels were Casey's whole life, he had little social life, stayed out of politics, and joined no museum boards after moving to New York. Joe says the man would have made a great guest — run into him at a party and just say "parcels, go"; Levinson replies that he might not have wanted to come on the show, since he was a wooden speaker who was happy by himself.

## "The company keeps getting bigger — where do I see the advantage?"

A company's revenue grows, the press writes that it has scale advantages, and yet you cannot find the advantage in the filings and the share price refuses to cooperate.

The yardstick this episode offers is packages per mile. UPS's trucks were the same trucks the department stores ran, down the same streets; the difference was how many stores' goods were on board. Revenue is the numerator, and it tells you nothing about whether the denominator has been spread — items per trip, customers per station, stops per driver per day.

![A horizontal fraction line runs across the figure: above it two bars rise from short to tall to show revenue growing, and below it two bars of equal height show the denominator unchanged.](/figures/oddlots-ups-numerator-grows-denominator-flat-en.svg)

The trap I have fallen into is reading "high market share" straight across as "low cost." The UPS story reminds me there is a door between those two: its cost advantage came from volume on a route that nobody else could get, and that volume was held in place by five-year contracts and by the stables the retailers had sold. Let the contracts lapse, or let someone do the same job at lower density, and the door loosens. So when a company talks about scale economics, I look first for what its "packages per mile" is, and then ask what holds that number in place.

## "The company looks after its people — doesn't that make it safer?"

The other sore spot: a company with good culture, long tenures and low turnover looks like a plus. This part of Casey's story made me take that instinct apart.

Promotion from within was a real commitment at UPS. It bought decades of loyalty, and it also bought a company that outside expertise could not get into, which is why it arrived late to barcoding. The same culture was written down in the first policy book in 1929: drivers turned up with shoes shined and trousers creased; UPS trucks never had the right of way and were expected to yield at every intersection. Levinson says this made the company strongly top-down, and in the modern era that became a problem — UPS bought a small German company and transplanted the UPS way, and it was a disaster, so they had to step back and rethink, because what was taken for granted in the United States did not work in Germany.

So the question I now add about culture is: what problem is this culture solving, and is that problem still here? Casey's promotion ladder solved "how do we get delivery people to stay," which mattered in the 1930s; by the time the company needed computer people in the 1970s, the same answer was an obstacle. A culture will not tell you when it has expired, and the bill arrives only when the track changes. This section is my own inference — Levinson said clearly that he has not studied UPS's business today.

## The one thing to take away

**How far someone gets often depends on what they are willing to put in writing as the things they will not do.** Casey's move stands out in this episode: while other trucking companies fought for wider operating authority, he asked California's regulator to prohibit him from carrying large freight, because only loads a single driver could handle would support the efficiency of his sorting station. What he worked to win was a line that tied his own hands, and that line became his moat.

Something I have tried, and you can do today: take a piece of paper and write down one thing you deliberately don't take on — the project you turn down, the event you skip, the kind of work you won't touch — and then write the sentence you use to decline, in the version you would actually say out loud. If you can't write that sentence, you probably haven't decided whether to decline; if you can, next time there's one less round of hesitation.
