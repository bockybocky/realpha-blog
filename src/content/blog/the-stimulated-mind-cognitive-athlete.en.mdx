> *I built my hut among the crowds of men,*<br/>
> *yet hear no noise of horse and carriage.*<br/>
> *You ask how this can be? When the mind is distant, the place turns remote of itself.*<br/>
> —— Tao Yuanming, "Drinking Wine, No. 5" (Eastern Jin dynasty); translation mine

Eleven at night. You close the last window. The day started at eight and never stopped. Thirty emails answered, four meetings, two documents revised, messages until your thumb ached.

Then you cannot remember what you did.

None of it left a shape. You know you were busy for fifteen hours, and you cannot point to one stretch and say, that is the thing I finished today. Next morning the loop runs again.

This tiredness is unlike the tiredness of a marathon. A marathon has a finish line.

## A book published in March 2026

Tommy Wood is an associate professor of pediatrics and neuroscience at the University of Washington, and chief scientist for motorsport at Hintsa Performance, where he advises Formula 1 drivers. He read biochemistry at Cambridge, took a PhD in neonatal neuroscience in Norway, and spent two years as a junior doctor in London handling fractures, overdoses, and end-of-life care for people with dementia.

It is an odd combination: infant brains starved of oxygen on one side, racing drivers on the other. He says both ask the same question. How long can a brain hold up under load, and what happens when it cannot.

The book is *The Stimulated Mind: Future-Proof Your Brain from Dementia and Stay Sharp at Any Age*, published by Harmony on 24 March 2026, 464 pages.

Where to buy: the [publisher's page](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/751292/the-stimulated-mind-by-dr-tommy-wood/) (hardcover, ebook, and an audiobook Wood narrates himself, sixteen and a half hours), [Amazon hardcover](https://www.amazon.com/Stimulated-Mind-Future-Proof-Brain-Dementia/dp/0593797817), and [the author's site](https://www.drtommywood.com/stimulated-mind).

I picked it up expecting a book about preventing dementia. Half of it turns out to be about how you spend this afternoon.

## Three gears

Wood borrows a framework from James Hewitt and Aki Hintsa that sorts cognitive work into three gears.

High gear is writing, research, learning — full attention, one thing at a time. Low gear is reading for pleasure, talking to someone, staring out of a window: the brain runs, but without strain. Middle gear sits between them. Meetings, admin, email, switching between four windows.

His argument is to schedule high gear and low gear first, then let middle gear fill what remains.

Most days run the other way round. Meetings claim the calendar, email fills the gaps, and by the time you sit down to write something it is nine in the evening and your head is sludge.

![Two full-day time bars compared. The top bar is nearly filled by middle gear, with only a narrow block of high gear left at the far right; the bottom bar carves out several blocks of high and low gear first, pushing middle gear to the tail end.](/figures/three-gears-day-layout-en.svg)

The trouble with middle gear is that it drains you without restoring you. High gear is costly but produces something. Low gear produces nothing and recharges you. Middle gear gives you neither — a whole day of thinking with nothing that lasts, and a whole day without rest.

![A four-quadrant chart where the horizontal axis is whether anything is left behind and the vertical axis is whether you are recovering or depleting. Low gear sits top left, high gear sits bottom right, and middle gear lands in the bottom-left corner that neither produces nor restores, marked with a box.](/figures/middle-gear-worst-quadrant-en.svg)

He compares this to an amateur athlete. Professionals know when to add load, when to cut it, when to stop. Amateurs assume training should always be hard, and then they break.

Wood quotes an orthopaedic surgeon he worked under: only amateurs get stress fractures. He later says this is not accurate — experienced runners get them too. But the point holds. Amateurs are likelier to put themselves there, and the difference lies less in the training hours than in the other twenty or so hours of the day.

## Fifty seconds

Wood cites Gloria Mark's records of how people actually work. Two numbers.

Twenty years ago, people stayed on a single work task for an average of two and a half minutes before switching. Now it is under fifty seconds.

On average, twelve minutes pass before you move to a different project entirely. Once you leave, you typically detour through two other work areas, and more than twenty-five minutes pass before you return to the original.

The second number matters more than the first. The cost of an interruption lands in the time it takes to get back, not in the seconds it took.

![A main work track is broken partway through, the line detours into two other work domains and then folds back to the original track, with a gauge above marking the detour at more than twenty-five minutes.](/figures/interruption-detour-cost-en.svg)

One more pairing: email and messaging apps interrupt work about four times an hour, and after replying it takes ten to fifteen minutes to return to the task. At least half of all task switches are self-interruptions.

That last clause stings. Not the boss, not a colleague, not the blinking red dot — you, mid-sentence, moving your own hand to another window.

## Rest has to leave the work

What I used to call a break was switching from a spreadsheet to a news site.

The research Wood cites says that does nothing. Five to ten minutes of meditation, a short comedy clip, getting out of the chair — those reduce fatigue and restore energy. Breaks spent on another cognitively demanding task increase fatigue instead.

Set against the Pomodoro rhythm of twenty-five minutes on and five off, Wood's adjustment is that the interval should come from how long you can reliably focus, not from a number in a book. Start where you are, take the break, extend from there.

Following the same line, he takes apart a familiar claim: that a natural ninety-minute rhythm runs through the day, so focus blocks should be ninety minutes. The idea traces to Nathaniel Kleitman's observations in the early 1960s, but the evidence that cognitive function varies on that cycle is inconsistent.

The same passage handles the ten thousand hours. Anders Ericsson observed that the strongest music students practised two to three times a day, eighty to ninety minutes a session. Ten thousand hours was the average the very best among them had accumulated by age twenty — and at that point they were not yet experts. The number was never a threshold.

## The misunderstanding about flow

The line that landed hardest for me is the one about flow.

When we talk about focus we tend to picture that frictionless state: time disappears, the work moves itself. So when you sit there feeling slow and stupid, rewriting one sentence five times, the easy conclusion is that today is a bad day.

Wood's reading is that flow belongs to skills you have already mastered, fully expressed. When you work at the edge of your ability, learning something you cannot yet do, the process is effortful by nature, error-prone by nature, uncomfortable by nature.

His sentence:

> All of this to say that even when it doesn't feel easy, that doesn't mean you're not doing it right.

Put that next to investing and it still holds. A company you understand reads smoothly. One you do not understand reads like gravel. The gravel is where the things you do not yet own are kept.

![Three concentric rings: the inner ring is the flow zone of what you have already mastered, the middle ring is the edge where you get stuck, make mistakes and strain, and learning happens in that ring, while the outer ring is too hard to attempt.](/figures/flow-versus-learning-edge-en.svg)

## What this has to do with twenty years from now

Everything above concerns this afternoon. The spine of the book sits further out.

Wood's central idea is cognitive headroom. He uses a lifting analogy: if daily life asks you to carry ten to twenty-five pounds in each hand, and you have trained until you can pull a hundred pounds off the floor, the daily load becomes light and you have capacity left for the unexpected. Headroom is provisioned for the unusual day.

Two sets of numbers make the analogy concrete.

The Rush Memory and Aging Project examined the brains of more than a thousand older adults after death. Nearly everyone showed some pathology, whether or not they had been diagnosed with dementia, across more than 230 combinations. A Swedish study followed over two thousand people for about a decade, grouping them by blood markers: in the highest-risk group, only 43 percent received a dementia diagnosis, more than half did not develop it; in the lowest-risk group, 5 percent still did.

Pathology does not equal symptoms. The layer in between is headroom.

![Two containers hold pathology at the same level; the short one overflows into symptoms, while the tall one still has a gap above the waterline, and that gap is labeled reserve.](/figures/cognitive-reserve-overflow-en.svg)

Where does headroom come from? Wood's 3-S model names three things: stimulus (cognitive challenge), supply (energy, oxygen, nutrients), and support (the repair that happens during rest and sleep). Stimulus gives the brain a reason to change, supply provides the materials, support lets the change take hold.

And the stimulus comes from those high-gear hours.

## The dose is lower than you think

If that sounds like rebuilding your whole life, the numbers offer some relief.

A study of more than seventy-five thousand adults, mostly aged fifty to seventy, found that walking four thousand steps a day was associated with a 25 percent lower dementia risk than fifteen hundred steps. The benefit extends to around ten thousand steps, but the point is that it does not begin at ten thousand.

![A curve rising from the lower left to the upper right, steep at first and flattening later, with the 4,000-step mark picked out by a dashed line, showing that most of the benefit falls in that early stretch.](/figures/dose-curve-front-loaded-en.svg)

In adults in their sixties and seventies, brisk walking three times a week for forty minutes produced a 2 percent increase in hippocampal volume after a year, rather than the expected 1 to 2 percent shrinkage. That programme started at ten minutes per session and added five minutes a week.

Smaller still: three hours of uninterrupted sitting lowers cerebral blood flow, executive function, and attention, and fifteen squats every twenty minutes improves those measures.

Near the end of the book Wood quotes the neurologist Sanjula Singh. Her meta-analysis identified more than twenty lifestyle risk factors linked to dementia, stroke, and depression. Her advice is to work on one at a time.

Because the factors are connected, changing one tends to move others. An activity that demands movement, coordination, and company at once — dancing, say — reaches several of them together.

One line I copied out:

> But when you only have the capacity to do one thing, focusing on two makes it likely that zero will happen.

## What your day looks like

Back to eleven at night.

The problem with that day was not the fifteen hours. Some people work fifteen hours and feel full. Others work eight and feel hollow. The difference lies in whether any stretch of those hours was whole.

The division of labour raises output — the idea goes back to Adam Smith and the pin factory, where splitting one job into eighteen operations multiplied production several times over. That observation held in the factory, and has held for two and a half centuries.

But Smith divided motions, not attention. The pin maker performed one operation all day; his concentration ran continuously. What we now call multitasking divides the continuous time of a single brain, chopping it into fifty-second pieces. That is the opposite of division of labour.

![The top half shows three unbroken color bands, one worker to one step; the bottom half shows the same band chopped into more than twenty short segments, standing for one brain whose time is diced up.](/figures/division-of-labor-versus-multitasking-en.svg)

A machine that restarts every fifty seconds does not get more efficient just because it never stops turning.

## One Thing to Take With You

What decides whether a day leaves you drained is whether any stretch of it went uninterrupted.

Tonight before bed, take a sheet of paper and sort today into three piles: work that needed full attention, meetings and email and window-switching, and rest. A few words per pile is enough. Then count what share the second pile took.

Keep that sheet. Count again in two weeks. Put the two numbers side by side and you will know which way you have been moving.
