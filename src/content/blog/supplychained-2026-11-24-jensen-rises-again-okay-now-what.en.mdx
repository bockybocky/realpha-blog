> What a ruler's dominance begins from is this: people are the root. Order the root and the state stands firm; disorder the root and the state stands in danger.
> —— *Guanzi*, "Ba Yan" chapter (Warring States; my own translation)

Guanzi's "root" means people — a state's population and its talent. Order that layer and the state is steady; disorder it and the state is at risk. Put the line on a company today and the root narrows down to one man, a man who was removed last month and is back in the chair this month.

In the Supply Chained episode "Jensen Rises Again: Okay, Now What.," released on 24 November 2026, Tim Culpan of Culpium and Jon of Asianometry talk through Jensen Huang being removed by NVIDIA's board and then reinstated as president, CEO and board director. Tim's read is that reinstatement erases none of the allegations, and that during the weeks he was out, the United States tightened the loophole letting blacklisted buyers rent NVIDIA compute through Southeast Asian data centres. The episode has exactly two hard numbers: Huang holds under 4% of NVIDIA's shares with voting power to match, because the company was founded before dual-class stock was a thing, so whether he keeps the seat is the board's call; and Rene Haas renewed his own contract through 2033, which took a name off the succession list. Jon raises the one that matters more — the circular financing behind the neoclouds (companies that buy GPUs, build data centres and rent the compute out) is coordinated by Huang personally, while two conditions were still open at the time of recording: how much leash the board gives him, and whether the bill covering Southeast Asian transshipment passes.

## He's back, so is everything back to where it started?

No. What Washington moved while he was gone, he doesn't get to move back.

Here's where it moved. The US keeps a blacklist of Chinese entities that aren't supposed to get NVIDIA chips, and those entities had a way around it: rent compute from a data centre in Southeast Asia. One of the sites Tim names sits in Malaysia, across a strip of water from Singapore, and Jon visited it a few years back. During Huang's absence, Tim says, that route looks to have been shut. Jon's addition: the bill hasn't passed, people had been assuming it would, and now maybe it won't — he says he doesn't know.

![Two panels: in the top one, the direct path for listed companies to buy chips is blocked, but the path that loops through Southeast Asian data centers is still open; in the bottom one, a gate has been closed across that detour as well.](/figures/blacklist-detour-gate-closed-en.svg)

Then comes the hardest line in the episode: if nothing changes as a result of the exit and the reinstatement, that is a huge failing. His reasoning is that these workarounds have been reported many times over, he has known about them for a long time, plenty of people have known and talked about them, and nobody cared — Huang was untouchable and NVIDIA was king. Once the man has been removed even once, the issue is out in plain sight and nobody gets to bury their head in the sand again.

What I took from this part has nothing to do with personnel. An industry problem that everyone knows about and nobody handles is usually held in place by a shield, and the shield is often one person's unassailability. Break the shield once and the things it was holding back start moving on their own, even after the person returns.

## Why could they push him out, and why couldn't they keep him out?

They could push him out because he has no voting power. They couldn't keep him out because nobody else wanted the chair.

Tim's first move on seeing the press release was to WhatsApp the company's senior spokesperson and ask whether the thing was legitimate — his first thought was that the website had been hacked, and he figures there are people out there who would enjoy hacking it for the fun of announcing a comeback. His second move was to check the SEC, where the same release was filed. Jon's comparison is the Bible line about the tomb being empty on the third day and the man returning to life, and the two of them sit there laughing about Jensen Hong, Jesus Hong. Tim's own comparison is Sam Altman's weekend at OpenAI; the two episodes share a feature, which is that the decision to get rid of the man was hasty in both.

On the ownership side, Huang holds under 4% of NVIDIA with voting power to match. He can't vote himself in and he can't vote himself out. Tim's phrasing is that he sits there at the graces of the board, and he doesn't control the board either. Tim calls this Huang's weakness and his folly: he started the company so long ago that dual-class voting stock wasn't yet a thing, so the structure Zuckerberg and Musk later enjoyed passed him by, and Dario Amodei's leadership team at Anthropic has something like five classes of shares. Tim adds a detail worth keeping: vocal shareholders back him, but his biggest holders are passive funds like Vanguard and Fidelity, which tend not to get political, and a large slice is simply ETFs, where the holding is whatever the market says — an ETF manager has no reason to want him in or out.

![Three pairs of bars: in the first, stake and voting power are equally short; in the second, the stake stays short while the voting bar runs much longer; in the third, the voting bar is longest of all and the stake bar is still just as short.](/figures/share-vs-vote-bars-en.svg)

On the succession side, Jon heard that day that Rene Haas had renewed his contract through 2033, and Polymarket (a market where people bet on event outcomes) had Haas as the number-one contender. Renew the contract and the list loses a name. Jon's conversation with a friend landed on nobody wanting this job, and the one person who ended up wanting it was the guy who had left, so the company was stuck with him. Tim thought Ian Buck was a good option, except Ian would have had to go to Huang and get his blessing first. Huang put out a post within the past week saying he would be honoured to take the job back, and Tim says that read to a lot of people as pressure.

## What can Congress and the China hawks actually do next?

Congress has thin tools. The hawks' momentum won't go to zero and won't get everything either.

Tim says the Senate and the House can't boot him unless they pass some anti-Jensen law. Law enforcement has a theoretical route — there are laws barring a person convicted of certain felonies from holding a directorship — but that doesn't stop him being CEO. NVIDIA restored all three roles, president, CEO and director, and he could hold the first two without the board seat, which happens often enough. So the legal avenues are few unless he is convicted and sentenced to hard time. As for hearings, Tim's description is grandstanding: a hearing hauls someone over the coals in public while the interrogators enjoy the privilege and protection of their position, and it rarely leads to anyone resigning. Zuckerberg sat through significant interrogation and kept his role; Tim calls him the poster child for surviving it. Jon's addition lands well — Zuck also has super-voting shares.

Tim says the single most important line in the press release is this one: in reaching its decision the board received input from senior levels of the United States government and, based on that input, is confident the reinstatement poses no national security risk — national security having been one of the reasons they ousted him in the first place, and Tim adds that it wasn't the only one. Jon's view is that once the president starts saying he wants Huang back, he can't see how long the other faction holds him out; Tim says what surprised him is that the president didn't throw Huang under a bus.

On the hawks, Jon's read is that during the week Huang was gone, the Washington hawks looked like they were winning everything, with people imagining a full export ban on all chips. His question is whether bringing the man back amounts to someone saying they don't want to go that far. Tim splits it in two. First, Huang won't accept being told who he can sell to; business people in general don't like being assigned their customers. Second, NVIDIA has never put China in its projections, on the grounds that it's a swing factor nobody can be sure of, and the company has taken write-downs before on inventory it couldn't sell there. So Tim's conclusion sits between zero and one, with no return to how business looked the month before the storm. He adds a reminder: there are enough people in the US government, the intelligence community and other agencies who will say that Huang being back or not, this stuff still needs controlling.

## Will the new chairman and the compliance machinery have teeth?

Tim doubts it, and the reasons he gives can be checked.

Announced alongside the reinstatement were a few things: Tim says Harvey Jones takes the chair and that he previously wasn't on the board; a compliance officer is coming; and there's a committee meant to keep an eye on Huang. Tim doesn't know whether any of it has teeth, and his reference point is the advisory body Facebook set up, which he doesn't think ever had teeth and which Congress never really cared about. His call on NVIDIA is that the leash won't be short, because this board hasn't been strong to date. The new chairman is the variable — Tim says maybe Harvey Jones tells him here's the deal, and Huang says all right, fair enough.

The exchange that closes the episode is clearer than any governance analysis: "Or you get something like OpenAI and he clears out the whole board except for a few people." "Except he only has 4% of the shares, so he doesn't have that power." "Not yet."

Three visible things are what I'd watch: who appoints the compliance officer and who that person reports to; whether the committee ever publishes anything; and whose signature is on the next China-related sales decision. Nothing new on all three, and the machinery is decoration.

## So what can I do with this myself?

Two sore spots, and one action each that I'm keeping.

The first sore spot is the news saying the man is back and the storm is over, while nothing on my own worry list got shorter. The split I use: personnel can flip in a day, terms and institutions can't. The episode's material divides cleanly — things that flip in a day (who's CEO, who chairs, when the hearings get scheduled) and things that moved irreversibly while he was out (the transshipment controls, the compliance role, that committee, the press writing the workarounds out in the open). So my action is to count the second pile. How much got permanently altered during the storm is what the storm leaves behind, regardless of who ends up in the chair.

![Three points on a timeline: the personnel items in the top row are pulled back to where they started by a curved return arrow, while the institutional items in the bottom row are held by a ratchet pawl and can only keep moving to the right.](/figures/ratchet-personnel-vs-institution-en.svg)

The second sore spot is how to size the risk in a chain held up by one person. Jon relays their friend Ben's line that Huang was a one-man coordinating function: opening up the supply chain as far as he could, pushing all those Asian companies, and getting seven big financial institutions into compute investment — and Jon says that last piece wasn't settled when he left. Tim thinks this matters more than the China story, and the mechanism he describes goes like this: Huang has been handing neoclouds all but free chips in return for equity, plus loan guarantees and other arrangements, which leaves NVIDIA on the hook for a lot of what the neoclouds are doing. Roll that back and the AI compute buildout slows: NVIDIA sells fewer chips, Foxconn ships fewer servers, the PCB makers ship fewer boards, CoWoS demand — TSMC's advanced packaging — follows, and the whole chain moves together. Some of it gets replaced by Anthropic's and OpenAI's in-house chips, while the neoclouds themselves land in serious trouble and the ripple runs through the supply chain. Tim says when SemiAnalysis cut its medium-term forecast for the buildout, his sense is the neoclouds were what they had in mind.

![On the left a central circle connects by three lines to three outer nodes; on the right a stepped row of bars descends from long to short, each one further downstream shorter than the last.](/figures/one-person-hub-descending-chain-en.svg)

So the question I'm keeping is whether there's anything on the chain that only one particular person can sign. If there is, then when that person hits trouble, the thing to calculate is how long it takes before somebody else can sign it.

## The one thing to take away

**The most fragile point in a system is the thing only one particular person can sign.** The episode circles this the whole way through: Huang holds under 4%, nothing in law protects him, institutionally he can be replaced at any time — and they brought him back anyway, because of the people only he can move and the arrangements only he can close. Paper governance power and real dependence are two separate things at this company.

Here's something I've tried. Pick a system you lean on daily — something at home, a process at work, a club you belong to all count — and write down which single person's absence would stall it next week; then write the three specific things that happen if that person disappears for a week. Get three down and you can see how long the chain really is. Can't get three, and the person isn't as load-bearing as you assumed, so the attention can go elsewhere.
