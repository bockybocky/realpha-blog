In the War on the Rocks episode of 20 November 2026, host Ryan Evans — about to hand over the podcast and step back from week-to-week coverage of the war — asks Michael Kofman, Dara Massicot and former armour officer Amos Fox what nearly four years of war in Ukraine has actually taught us. All three answers route around drones and AI: Kofman says Russia is procuring over 120,000 large one-way attack drones this year, and that what Russia and Ukraine did was make a strategic decision to move manpower out of manoeuvre formations into new reconnaissance-strike units and shift logistics to support them. Fox says precision solved some problems and created many more, and that the sheer number of people and equipment on the ground, and attrition, still count. One condition sits under all of it: the battlefield we have watched since 2023 is no guarantee of what day one of the next war looks like. Every number is as stated at the time of recording.

## February 2022: drones were the artillery's eyes

Kofman starts by doing something worth copying — separating observations from lessons. Every war has its own context, and the evolutionary tree of military technology is full of dead branches: the Turkish Bayraktar TB2 looked decisive in Nagorno-Karabakh and mattered far less in the next war. The second variable is which phase of the war you are looking at.

So he goes back to how this war began. In 2022 two land forces built around artillery used drones for reconnaissance and to spot for the guns; then came light bombing; first-person-view drones appeared in early 2023 but did not define the war until the end of that summer. Through the first year and a half — offensives, counteroffensives, mobility still working — something else was the main character.

The implication only landed for me afterwards: the Ukraine in our heads, the one made of drones plus artillery plus traditional capabilities, is Ukraine from year two onward. Using it to picture day one of the next war is porting the last phase of the last war into the opening of the next. Someone adds a historical check: armies fought very differently at the end of the First World War than at its start, and that much technological change inside a single war is historically unusual.

## Late summer 2023: mobility gets shut down

From then on, freedom of movement collapsed. Kofman calls the current way of fighting mass precision, and insists mass means concentrating sensors, networks and strike capability at a decisive point on the battlefield. It lets the numerically inferior side hold: Ukraine is holding terrain with an incredibly low density of forces. But it only denies control of the ground to the opponent — taking and holding terrain still needs people standing on it.

Fox's metaphor will stay with me: land forces today are an M&M without the hard candy shell, melting in your hand before you can get them in your mouth. The shell has to go back on, and what needs restoring is mobility; restoring combined arms manoeuvre across a static front, he thinks, is not happening. He also flags the reverse risk: sell off your land force to buy drones while your opponent keeps both, and you have built a hollow force — tie up the drones and the ground is theirs. He says Ukraine is near that position now, short of the force needed to push an enemy off ground and then hold it.

![On the left is a circle with a hard shell, the shell labeled as mobility; on the right the same circle has no shell and its contents melt and drip downward.](/figures/mm-shell-mobility-en.svg)

Kofman's addition is the cleanest inference in the episode: extensive mining, traditional artillery that is used sparingly but prevents any concentration the moment it appears, and prepared defences — those three stacked under the drones are what produces the current effect. Remove artillery from both force structures, leave only point damage from drones, and large-scale dismounted infantry assaults become a serious problem for a drone force. So no force should optimise itself against a single capability, drones included. And the most effective counter to drones so far is drones: the engagements look like First World War aerial combat, with interception nets and guns mounted on airframes.

![Two columns of bars are compared: on our side the drone bar is long but the ground forces bar is almost zero, while the opponent has both, and an arrow runs from the opponent straight through our empty ground column.](/figures/hollow-force-tradeoff-en.svg)

## Four years of Western learning: tech first, and don't mention the war

Massicot is hardest on this point: the US military has no forward observers. Being in Kyiv falls short of frontline observation; you have to be embedded, watching how Ukrainians fight. So the system lives on knowledge filtered through several hands, and spends its energy on "tell me which technology I need, then I'll work out how to use it" — skipping the step where you ask what the problem is and what force employment has to change. Evans says relying on a small group of talented analysts who freelance to the battlefield each quarter has been hugely damaging. Massicot wants it institutionalised: forward observers, and a single learning centre at the chairman level instead of each service and command doing a disjointed piece.

![A chain of knowledge runs from the front line to the institution, and the embedded observer link in the middle is an empty dashed box, so the chain breaks, leaving four fragments at the right end with gaps between them that cannot be fitted together.](/figures/secondhand-knowledge-chain-en.svg)

Fox lays out what he saw at Army Futures Command: top-down pressure to integrate technology into everything, so technology drove the concepts, and the lessons pulled from Ukraine were tied to what tech could do for us. And something stranger — an internal "first rule of Fight Club" atmosphere about Russia-Ukraine. You were integrating lessons into a concept while not naming who the concept was aimed at.

## The Iran war: the lessons sat there for four years

Massicot says the run-up to Operation Epic Fury was distressing to watch for anyone who had been learning from this war. Thanks to commercial satellite imagery — she says it came from China and was released daily — they watched squadron stacked upon squadron of high-value aircraft at bases in the Middle East. Those bases had some air defences and essentially no drone defence, no dispersal, no hardened command and control, nowhere to go underground. She says it was luck that Iran did not take the shot, and she recalls the AFCENT commander raising it in an interview afterwards.

Fox is blunter: those bases have been in the Middle East for twenty-five years, so finding them was not hard. And the shelters, several of which he has used, are a concrete barrier designed for 107mm Katyusha rockets — against swarms of Shahed drones they do not hold.

Her next question is the one I like: you watched Russians and Ukrainians spend four years hardening bases, digging underground, dispersing people, putting up drone nets and decoys, and none of it was done before this started. That was a choice; choosing to do nothing is a choice. Now the Air Force says base force protection is a priority — fine, at least it is happening. So what else are we still not doing?

![The left frame shows a row of aircraft parked nose to tail in one concentrated line on a runway with counter-drone capability near zero; the right frame shows the same number of aircraft dispersed, some drawn below the ground line, with interceptor nets and decoys above.](/figures/basing-concentration-vs-dispersal-en.svg)

Evans pushes it up a level: thousands of targets struck in a few weeks is a real accomplishment, but the line between force and political aims went missing, and the campaign CENTCOM and the Secretary of Defense were supposed to plan was absent. Fox says this is what he has written about — campaigns replaced by kill chains, kill webs and target lists, with strike packages treated as the path to victory.

## Where it stands, and who to watch next

Evans asks whether anyone is doing better. North Korea gets named: it is taking experience directly from Russia and reorganising its forces around drone warfare. Kofman worries China will take the smarter lessons, while plenty of people here look at a map, see land on one side and ocean on the other, and conclude there is nothing to learn. Fox guesses China may already be working the hard-candy-shell problem — occupy an island, then move forces under a sky full of drones.

Fox also warns that many people are learning bad lessons. At a conference in London in April he watched a British general pull out an app, open with how many strikes had been conducted today and how many Russians killed, then pull it out again at the end to report fifteen more. In the Q&A Fox asked whether that got Zelensky any closer to his policy objectives, and the answer was "I don't think about strategy." His comment: bro, you're a general. He calls it task completion bias.

Someone on the panel relays an unnamed friend's observation, which I think is the line to keep: these lessons come from a weaker party fighting a stronger one, and we are not that party, so copying them risks giving up what makes us strong. Kofman's version: adopt unmanned systems, but not by surrendering existing advantages. Then he uses armour — the trouble lies in the vintage of the optimisation. There was a full armour revolution from 1945 to 1985; across the forty years since, the Americans, British, Germans and Russians are all still driving 1980s platforms. Fox says he published this argument and the armour community was furious: who are you to question our tank.

Massicot brings back one detail that stopped me. Presenting in Sweden, she described infantry pinned in position for long stretches, relying on telemedicine, sometimes without medics, with helicopter casualty evacuation impossible — a man alone in a foxhole treating himself. A Swedish officer listened and said: then infantry will have to be trained almost like rangers, because they are effectively alone. She says it is a professional military education and training problem, and she has not seen her own side pick it up.

Kofman closes by splitting the lessons: the United States is an expeditionary power fighting away games, so what it should learn differs from what an Estonia or a Taiwan — the stand-in force that holds the line on day one — should learn. He says air power is the first thing that can move, but localised air superiority lasts only briefly and does not solve an opponent's fire control over your forces.

## "We would never fight like that" has the same shape in investing

The question I most want to ask for readers here: I got the trend right, so why didn't the money follow?

Kofman's phasing warning transfers intact: the common error is taking what you saw in the late phase of the last war as the opening of the next. The investing version looks identical — treating the inventory levels, pricing and margin structure of a late-cycle quarter as the normal state of a cycle's opening. The eighteen months when mobility still worked and the years when positions froze are two phases of one war, read with different logic. Which phase you are in decides which indicators still mean anything.

The other half is "we would never fight like that." In a military it functions as permission not to look; in markets it sounds like "our industry is different" or "that can't happen here." Massicot's follow-up is worth stealing: fine, you have air superiority — now how do you go after drone teams dispersed on the ground? Dumping your survival problem on someone else happens in portfolios daily: dumping the explanation for a drawdown on macro, dumping position risk on "it comes back long term."

## Are you buying the platform, or the connective tissue?

The other pain point: a theme gets talked to death, so how do you separate structure from noise?

The test this episode offers is useful. Kofman says Russia and Ukraine made a strategic decision — reallocate manpower from manoeuvre formations into a new reconnaissance-strike formation that later also did air defence, shift logistical support over from traditional artillery, add organisational capacity and the enabling technologies. Only then can you fight this way at scale. A few fibre-optic drones used against the IDF and thousands of personnel built into a new force structure are two different orders of magnitude. Fox's armour experience is the same point: a tank does what it does because of a mass of connective tissue behind it.

![On the left a lone small circle stands for the platform; on the right the same circle is wrapped by linked nodes for people, logistics, force structure, training and maintenance, forming a whole supporting structure.](/figures/platform-vs-connective-tissue-en.svg)

So with any "this new thing will change an industry" story, I now ask three things first: where do the people come from, who funds the logistics, and has the org chart changed. Those take years, not a purchase order. Which is why the base defence example stings — the technology was proven in Ukraine four years ago, the US government is Ukraine's partner, and the orders came only after the war started. Massicot notes a drone threat against RAF Fairford in the UK last week: with no detection capability, the only option was to loft the bombers and get them out. The capability exists; the order wasn't placed. My own use for this is to date the moment I say "I saw it coming," then look back six months later at what the company actually did — the gap between visible and done is often the whole time cost of the investment case.

## One thing to take away

**The real way you fight is the one your situation forces on you.**

Fox was talking about war: the way you will have to fight outranks the way you prefer to fight. Lean on precision strike and the enemy goes high, goes underground, goes into the cities, and you end up massing on the ground and fighting attritionally anyway. The force of the line is that it separates my plan from my situation, and most people treat those as one thing.

Here's something I've tried that holds outside investing too. Pick something you currently know only through second-hand reports — a part of your team's process that keeps breaking, how your kid is doing at school, a pipeline you manage by weekly summary — and go stand in it once this week. Don't intervene, don't call a meeting, just watch one full cycle, then write one line: the thing the reports didn't carry. The first time I did this I went and watched a scheduled job I had written myself run end to end, and found it waiting every day at the same step for a file that had stopped existing, while the report said "success."
