In the episode of The Circuit dated 23 November 2026, recorded on Friday 20 November, Ben Bajarin and Jay Goldberg go through Nvidia's earnings from that Tuesday. Jay says the headline should simply read: Nvidia reported incredible numbers, and no one cared. The company raised next year's revenue outlook by five billion dollars, pointing at 75% growth with profits close to doubling, and the stock fell the next day. Both of them put the reason on the unfilled CEO seat, and Ben expects that uncertainty to run six months or more. He is equally firm that demand for compute itself has not changed; what is open is who fills the gigawatts. Every number is as stated at the time of recording.

## Three weeks earlier: the neoclouds got squeezed by rates first

Jay rewinds three weeks. The layer that cracked first was the neoclouds — companies that buy GPUs and rent them out for AI work — and the cause had nothing to do with Nvidia. Rates were moving up, credit investors started going through these financings deal by deal, and private lending had problems of its own. People were beginning to notice that the neocloud capital structure was not what everyone had pictured. So borrowing conditions in that layer had already tightened before Nvidia reported.

## Earnings day: a room full of people looking down at their phones

Both hosts were at an investor summit called Tomorrow X when the numbers came out. Ben says the whole conference took a break to check phones, and one meeting he was in stopped mid-sentence to wait. Profits were good, supply chain commitments were doubled down on, and there was little about the CEO. Then the stock fell.

Jay points at a date: the quarter closed on 30 October, before Jensen Huang left. Every raised commitment and every good number was signed off under the old management, and none of it tells you what the new company will do. On the employee side, ten to fifteen thousand people have signed a petition to bring Huang back, which Jay says is now a number with weight behind it. The Asian supply chain has been polite and evasive — they have dealt with Huang for years, they rate him highly, and they are more tolerant of his style than most. Both hosts think a successor with the same ease there is unlikely.

## The next six months: the chessboard gets read differently

Ben's framing is a chessboard. Nvidia led by Huang is one reading of the board; Nvidia run by someone else is another. Structural demand has not changed and the technology roadmaps have not changed, but the question of who is competitive in three or four years has to be worked out again. So most people moved their ratings to wait and see. Ben adds that the uncertainty itself will not be a catalyst — it does not resolve on its own. Jay has been following the PolyMarket odds on candidates, says a few of them would run a very large semiconductor company well and a few would be bad, and will not name names. Nobody, he adds, is going to be Jensen.

## Who picks up those gigawatts

The sharpest stretch starts from how many neoclouds die. There are more than two hundred GPU-as-a-service players, and both hosts expect the majority to disappear, because consolidation is unavoidable. Michael Dell, at the same summit, supplied a piece of evidence: Dell already vets anyone who wants to order GPUs, and does not sell to all comers. Ben's read is that anyone Dell won't ship to does not have a business.

Then he names the beneficiary. These neoclouds are mostly imitation hyperscalers — the hyperscalers being the handful of companies building their own large clouds — and most of their demand comes from the hyperscalers too. If neoclouds fail, the land, power and shells get picked up cheaply by the hyperscalers, whose capital costs then improve. So the biggest beneficiary of Huang's departure is the hyperscalers.

The second layer is harder. The overwhelming majority of neocloud GPU demand is training-related. If the industry's centre of gravity moves from training loops to inference loops, these companies do not catch that work — Ben puts it bluntly: the only inference clouds that exist are the hyperscalers, and when an enterprise CTO places workloads, that is where they go. Any excess training capacity also ends up taken by the hyperscalers at a better price.

The third layer is bad for semiconductors. Over the last five years the customer base widened and hyperscaler bargaining power was broken up; back to three big customers, prices come down. Ben refuses the bearish label — he published a report that week whose thesis is that we need a lot more gigawatts and that will not change. What is open is what compute fills those gigawatts: hyperscaler custom silicon, or merchant GPUs. Google is already pushing TPU V9 and V10 with Broadcom and MediaTek, and the supply chain rumours are out. His conclusion is that this is bad for merchant GPUs and not bad for compute infrastructure; what changes is who the biggest beneficiary is.

## The same week: Anthropic lists, then a gap

The other thread is Anthropic's IPO. Priced at $83, it ran to $104 on the first day and closed at $93; on the morning of the recording it was $84.27, hugging the offer price. It was reportedly seven times subscribed, and the read on the show is that the book the underwriters put together was concentrated, which left a bad aftertaste. The growth rates got picked over too: up 50% quarter over quarter, then 40% — fine numbers on their own, except the same quarter last year was up 140%. That is a steep drop. What the hosts find strange is that "we don't have enough compute" was sitting right there as an explanation, it would have satisfied everyone, and the company did not use it. The first earnings call lands in January, in front of a different audience: the pre-IPO meetings were with investment banks and venture investors, who negotiate hard but want the price up, while the people across the table after listing can short you. The show quotes an Evercore memo for the mood — guilty until proven innocent.

So where is the next catalyst? Everyone assumed the Anthropic listing would be it, and it wasn't. Both hosts expect the rest of the year to be quiet, with institutions sitting on higher cash balances waiting to place bets at the start of next year; the capital comes back, though not necessarily to the same names. Jay thinks Nvidia's CEO announcement won't come this year, and the 10-Q isn't out yet — he'll bet five cents it drops on the Wednesday night before Thanksgiving, since this is a company that put bad news out on Christmas Eve last year. A few weeks after that is AWS re:Invent, where Ben guarantees Amazon will arrive with the most optimistic enterprise demand story in years; his phrase for it is blood in the water. Cybersecurity earnings were also strong — Palo Alto did well, CrowdStrike demand is climbing — and they expect that to be the next narrative the market buys quickly.

## If the numbers were that good, why did the stock drop

I have paid for this lesson more than once, and this episode gives a clean way to cut it. An earnings report answers what happened last quarter. It cannot answer who is running the company or whether it is still strong in three years. Once the second question is open, however good the answer to the first one is, it is old news — and the quarter closing on 30 October is the most concrete footnote in the whole episode. So when good news lands and the price falls, work out which question got opened that day before doing anything.

One layer down is the catalyst. Ben says this uncertainty will not be a catalyst, meaning that as long as the CEO seat is unfilled, the discussion stays where it is, with nothing forcing anyone to re-decide. I used to treat wait and see as bearish and sold early. They are two different things: one is a conclusion, the other is the absence of one. With no conclusion, the thing to adjust is position size, and the direction stays where it was.

## Companies all sound strong, investors are all negative — who is right

Jay admits mid-episode that he is confused. He uses a lot of AI himself and Anthropic's pricing supports strong demand, yet people he knows who understand this well insist there is a GPU surplus right now, and he does not know who is right. The two of them land on the same answer: both sides are correct, because they are describing different things.

One is the level of demand. Ben runs an extreme assumption: suppose models stop getting better and stop getting bigger from today, frozen, and all that is left to do is deploy AI to the global workforce. Even then you need far more compute than exists now. So the level is not really in dispute.

The other is time. Enterprises move slowly. Anyone who has lived through a Windows platform migration has seen how long it takes — testing, process, making sure nothing breaks. Ben says they have studied adoption cycles for two decades and the finding is one line: it always takes longer than you think. Jay asks how long. Longer. The iPhone is a usable ruler: it launched in 2007, and growth only really showed after the iPhone 4, around 2011 and 2012. He describes the shape as a gradual uptrend sliding to the right, with no hockey stick in it; today's capital spending assumes a faster rollout than the one that happens.

Turned into something usable next quarter: keep two columns on a company. One for how big the demand is — contracts signed, capacity booked, whether the customer names are there. One for how much landed this quarter — revenue recognised and guidance. Both improving means level and timing are both working. The first holding while the second lags means the valuation takes the discount and the story stays. The 20% line from the show is the same ruler: Ben says the top 20% of enterprises have not yet deployed AI at scale in a managed way, so a revenue projection that only needs those 20% to pay is credible, while one that needs the other 80% to hold it up he would call unlikely.

## One thing to take with you

**A forecast that gets the direction right and the timing wrong looks exactly like a wrong forecast on your statement; to tell them apart, you have to write down what you would see if it were merely slow.**

That is the argument running through the whole episode. Companies are looking at the level five years out, investors are looking at what landed this quarter, neither side has done the arithmetic wrong, and they are reading different clocks. Jay asks how long it takes. Ben gives one word: longer.

Here is something I have tried that works outside investing too. Pick one thing you predicted a year or two ago would be widespread or finished by now — at home, at work, with your kids, any of it. Write three lines. First: the date you predicted. Second: where it actually stands today. Third: if it is merely slow, the one concrete sign you should already be able to see, with a name, a number or a date attached. If you can write the third line, keep waiting. If you cannot, the direction was wrong. The first time I did this, mine was "the whole house switches to voice control," I could not name a single sign, and the direction was wrong.
