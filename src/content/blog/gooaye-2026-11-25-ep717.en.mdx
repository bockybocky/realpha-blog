> In affairs, the prize is to control others, and not to be controlled by them.
>
> —— *Guiguzi*, chapter "Mou" (Warring States, attributed to Guiguzi; my own translation)

In Gooaye EP717, released on 25 November 2026, host Hsieh Meng-kung splits this drawdown into two columns. What changed: Jensen Huang is back at Nvidia, so the optical-interconnect, advanced-cooling and CCL substrate roadmaps have someone pushing them again. What didn't: the US clampdown on Chinese access to offshore compute, and the sell-side call for a pushout — roughly 20% of 2028 datacenter buildout sliding into later years. His read is that the trade built on shortages and price hikes stalls here, and with NAND, the tightest material on the board, already falling in China, a stock that has halved is not automatically cheap. The premise is that the deferral lands near the size the sell side is quoting; he adds that a 10% pushout would point the same way.

## The starting point: a personnel story lit the fuse

Hsieh says that if someone had told him two months ago that AI names would sell off at crash scale, and that the fuse would be a controversy around Jensen Huang, he would not have believed it. The version he tells on the show: the story breaks, the market demands an investigation, and then the man is reinstated before the hearing is even held. Bessent had been blunt about Huang earlier, and then, asked in an interview whether Nvidia was looking for someone from outside the company, answered that Huang is now an outsider. Hsieh's comment is that even GTA's writers would not dare script it this way. On the day of the reinstatement the market ripped; the next day most of the gain was given back, and then some.

What he draws from it first is to go easy on yourself — with a script this absurd, getting a few calls wrong is part of the deal. The second thing is leverage. Every time the VIX, the market's fear gauge, spikes, it looks in hindsight like a decent entry; sized up with leverage in the moment, one drawdown takes you out. Friends messaged him about the forced liquidations: wasn't it a shame, with those names limit-up two days later. His answer is that there is a party after every big round of liquidations, and the people who got liquidated can't attend it.

![Two price paths dip into the same trough, but the unleveraged one recovers and completes the whole journey, while the leveraged one is cut off at the bottom and never touches the rebound that follows.](/figures/gooaye-717-leverage-cut-off-en.svg)

## After the reinstatement: the two columns

What changed. With Huang out, the thing Hsieh worried about was the optics, advanced cooling and CCL substrate roadmaps that Huang had personally driven — without him, nobody in the market pushes them hard, and the ASP, the average selling price, uplift of each product generation shrinks along with them. With Huang back, that particular uncertainty comes down.

What didn't, in two parts. One is political: after the reinstatement, US politicians lined up to say they want more investigations and reviews of the episode and of the Trump administration, and datacenter buildout could catch some of that. The other is the BIS — the Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security — clampdown on Chinese entities renting compute offshore. Hsieh says the show covered that loophole a year or two ago, and the read then was that Washington left it open on purpose: let them use it, collect their money, and maybe watch them while you're at it. Now it gets closed, and the market is arguing about scope. His anchor is that Nvidia's indirect China revenue exposure runs around 20%, and that 20% is already a high number to quote. He mentions the Moonshot report doing the rounds: with the new rules out, Chinese buyers have pulled back on renting compute and on datacenter procurement. He says he isn't sure about that outlet's track record and treats it as a data point; the volume pulled back is more than American buyers can absorb, though the handover brings a choppy stretch — orders cancelled, others filling in, lead times and price expectations moving with them.

## What it leads to: 2028 work slides into 2029

The sell-side pushout number is 20% of the 2028 volume deferred. An optimist says it all happens eventually, so who cares. Hsieh works through the supply chain's own split instead: short materials are the ones that are tight right now, long materials are the ones that aren't. Move half of what was due in 2028 out to 2029 and 2030, and the purchasing manager's arithmetic changes immediately — you were a long material to begin with, your lead time is short, I can take delivery in 90 or 180 days, so I may still carry your volume, but I am not granting another price increase.

![Two of the ten units of 2028 volume are carved out, and two arrows move them onto the 2029 and 2030 bars, so the total is unchanged while the timing slips later.](/figures/gooaye-717-pushout-2028-en.svg)

He explains it with concert tickets, which I found clearer than any supply-demand curve: everyone is fighting for Jay Chou tickets, the scalpers have bid them to the moon, and then two extra shows are announced. Those two shows will probably sell out too, but the moment they're announced, the scalper price stops going up.

What matters more is that even the tightest material is being marked down. NAND prices have come off in China, while DRAM can hold a while longer. He relays one camp's explanation — China can't get GPUs or ASICs, so it pauses the expensive components first — and then says that after he shared it, a friend told him the volume estimates had simply been too high and this is a real cut. He leaves both readings on the table without picking one.

## Where we are now: half price is not cheap

Things that looked untouchable in June and July are going for half. Hsieh says this is the moment to re-examine, and then spends his time on the filter rather than the discount. Take the old bottleneck trading — chasing the pinch-point component — where the story has already played out once and the sell-side reports have drawn a beautiful multi-year projection, the kind of model that only produces that EPS if prices keep rising every quarter from here. A 50% drop in that name doesn't make it cheap. Mark the ASP down and the denominator falls with it, the stock gets more expensive, and there is room to re-rate lower.

![On the left the price bar is ten blocks and earnings per share one block, giving a P/E column of ten times; on the right the price is cut to five blocks but earnings fall to a quarter of a block, so the P/E column grows to twenty times.](/figures/gooaye-717-half-price-not-cheap-en.svg)

He gives his own example. He bought equipment names on a price-to-earnings ratio around 10 with long lead times, which read as the safe end of the board. After he bought, it went to 5. "The boss isn't home, nobody's minding the shop" — he looked left and right and there were no friends, just him buying. So he caps the position and runs risk control, so that no single trade can be the one that finishes him, because there is always something he has read wrong.

The direction he wants now runs the other way: value add, the names that benefit in substance as the market pushes toward newer and better technology. Stories built on shortage pricing are what he wants to dodge. The old problem with value-add names was the dream multiple — revenue arrives a year and a half to two years out, so nobody wanted to look. With prices down, the arithmetic works again. A newly formed bottleneck is still worth a look: robots suddenly ramping, some component going badly short.

## What to watch next: the price of rented compute

On the day he recorded, a report said xAI and SpaceX had cut their compute rental prices, and the market's association was that AI demand had cracked. Hsieh's read is that Musk's compute was priced above the market to begin with, so a cut can't be read straight as weak demand — and in a bearish tape, short essays fly around, most of which turn out to be fiction once the market recovers.

He still works through the uglier scenario. If compute prices keep sliding, Musk goes from a one-year payback to two or three years, he still gets his money back, and his cash position is far better than the field's. For the people who borrowed to build, it isn't a question of slower payback. Two numbers he has looked at: for the neo-clouds — the newer outfits renting out GPU compute — he puts the survival line around 4 to 5 dollars, and below that the debt they issued comes back on them; SoftBank needs to hold its LTV, debt as a share of asset value, under about 25%, so if the ARM shares or the OpenAI stake it posts as collateral falls to a certain level, it has to sell assets to keep the balance sheet healthy. His worst case is Musk playing the role the Korean memory makers played in a downcycle, expanding like mad, squeezing the competition out, and collecting their assets once they go under.

## Down 50% and stuck: cut it or hold it

This is the question I get most. On the show Hsieh mentions a morning conversation with a friend from industry who says he can't read what's happening, but by his industry judgment the component price-hike story may be in trouble while the long-term technology roadmap is fine — and yet the prices have fallen this far. Because he thinks like an industry person, he can't bring himself to sell here.

Hsieh's answer is to shrink the circle, and I think it's the most useful line in the episode. His image is magnetic tiles: you built the house a bit too big, stuck one piece on here and another there, the earthquake hits and a pile of them fall off. Try to save every tile in every corner and the whole body collapses. The move is to protect the core first — the part you know best, where you know you rarely make mistakes — and cut the position size. Once it's smaller, most people find the whole thing doesn't feel like a big deal, and the state of mind gets healthier.

![On the left a magnetic-tile structure is spread far too wide, with several outer pieces tilting and falling off while the main body is pulled out of shape; on the right only the shrunken core remains, intact with nothing coming loose.](/figures/gooaye-717-shrink-the-circle-en.svg)

Why does he put state of mind ahead of position? Because a lot of the damage comes from the extra moves you make after your composure goes. You hand down a judgment to explain every day's move, and stretched over a month the index sits in roughly the same range while your money is gone. So I'd turn "cut or hold" into two questions. Is this name inside the circle you know best, and how many times have you changed your mind about it in the past three weeks. If the first question has no answer, shrink regardless of how low the price is. If the first has an answer and the second has a big number, the problem sits in your position size.

## The news says shortage, so why does the stock keep falling

This one hurts differently. Every article you read is about tight supply, longer lead times, higher prices, while the position gets lower by the day. The episode makes the gap plain: what the market buys is the change in expectations, and once a shortage story has been written into reports as rising every quarter from here, the good news of the next several years is already inside today's price. What the market is reading now is whether it's shorter than the last report said. The two extra shows sell out, and the scalper price still can't go up.

So I'd reorder the reading. First ask whether the story is carried by price hikes or by a technology transition. Price hikes: go look at the spot quote for the tightest material, the NAND the episode mentions — if the tightest is loosening, everything queued behind it is looser. Technology transition: check whether the person driving it is still in the chair, and whether customer buildout schedules have been moved. Then ask whether the sell side has already drawn the story to the maximum; for those names, good news out is often the exit. Neither question has anything to do with the headline, so the answers won't contradict what your account is showing you.

## The one thing to take away

The number of times you change your mind is itself a cost. A tape can circle in place for a month and your money still disappears, because every re-decision charges you commission, charges you the spread, and spends a state of mind that was calm until you spent it — and the thing that made you change was usually that day's move, buried by the next day's.

Here's something I've tried. Take one thing you changed your mind about three times this week, and it doesn't have to be an investment — whether to change jobs, whether to have the overdue conversation with someone, whether to drop a kid's class. Write the first decision and the current one on the same sheet of paper. If they match, those rounds in between are cost you paid and got nothing for. If they differ, write the new fact that moved you next to them, and only count it if you can state it in one sentence. I've done this a few times, and the two answers are usually the same.
