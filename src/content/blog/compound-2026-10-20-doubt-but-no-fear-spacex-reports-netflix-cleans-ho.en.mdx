> When the thunder comes, there is trembling; then there is laughter and talk.

On the October 20, 2026 live episode of *The Compound and Friends*, Josh Brown and Michael Batnick named the prior week's selloff "doubt, but no fear": semiconductor equipment stocks fell nine sessions in a row, the longest streak in ten years, the VIX touched 21 intraday, and the S&P 500 was down only about two and a half percent. The same day Netflix posted 11.8% revenue growth, its slowest since 2023, and cut its 2026 advertising revenue outlook from roughly $3 billion to $2.7 billion, while SpaceX posted $14.6 billion in revenue, up 161%, with its superintelligence segment jumping from $2.5 billion to $7.5 billion in a single quarter. Their conclusion rests on two conditions: the AI labs have to be able to go public and raise money, and the hyperscalers have to keep delivering cloud revenue growth. Break either one and the whole argument stops working.

## The bull case: the market is taking out its own trash

Let me put their argument in its strongest form first.

Josh offered a frame I kept turning over after the episode ended: last week's selling contained doubt without fear. The evidence is that it was selective. Software names came through relatively unscathed, the hyperscalers fell modestly, and the damage concentrated in semiconductor equipment — the segment wired most directly to interest rates. Yields on the worst-rated AI-related paper were pushed toward 17%. Borrowing gets expensive, new capacity forms more slowly, and the demand side of the equation never changed.

That leads into Ed Yardeni's framing, the most useful stretch of the hour. The bond vigilantes who normally show up in sovereign and municipal debt have appeared in the credit of AI issuers, doing the one job nobody else wanted: pricing good risk apart from bad risk. Want to own Meta's bonds? Probably. CoreWeave's? Not as much as before. Yardeni's own phrasing is that this discipline "should deflate the AI bubble without bursting it," and that if some AI credits do blow up, the godfathers of the AI mafia and their protectors in government will clean up the mess. Michael looked at the speculative-grade spread chart and said the market was sobering up. I read it the same way — the light blue line blowing out means somebody is applying the brakes on everyone's behalf, and while that happened, money rotating toward quality stopped the rout in Treasuries.

The episode also produced a counterintuitive finding. Adam Parker ran the McClellan summation index — a cumulative measure of market participation — through a rolling ten-year regression against forward one-month S&P returns and found no predictive power. Against volatility, he found a statistically significant negative relationship: weak breadth leads to higher volatility, strong breadth to lower volatility. Josh translated it into plain English: good breadth helps stock pickers and does little for index owners, and most people just want the index to go up. So the popular line that bad breadth foretells a market-wide disruption fails when you measure it.

![A single line splits partway across: the upper branch spikes to 17% while the lower one stays almost flat, marking where spreads begin pricing bad borrowers apart from good ones.](/figures/credit-starts-sorting-en.svg)

Then there's SpaceX. Josh was blunt about it: Elon says annual recurring revenue lands between $500 billion and $1 trillion by the end of 2027, and he doesn't have to hit it — he only needs people to believe it's possible. Over the past few years SpaceX became, in practice, a data center business, while plenty of analysts kept comparing it to aerospace and defense or to telecom. Wrong comparison, wrong conclusion. Josh's observation is that Elon keeps running the same play: open a new category, execute enough that people can envision the market size, and whatever you were bearish on stops mattering. He tied that to Anthropic. The revised S1 reported by the *Wall Street Journal* commits $111 billion to Google Cloud contracts, $110 billion to AWS, and $31 billion to Microsoft regardless of actual usage, plus up to $86 billion renting NVIDIA chips from smaller cloud providers. The market needs those numbers to happen. SpaceX trading at 150 instead of 80 is good news for the rest of the year.

## The bear case, built from the same episode

The strongest objections came from material the show itself supplied.

The first one is Netflix. Financially nothing broke. Margins are healthy, the buyback hit a record share count, nobody doubts the earnings power — and the stock is down 52% from its high, while they fired content chief Bela Bajaria, studio head Dan Lin, and roughly a thousand other people. Josh put his finger on the reason Netflix stopped talking about hours spent: it can't win that one. Competing with Paramount and Disney for streaming hours was winnable. But the conversation moved venues, and now the comparison set is TikTok, Instagram, YouTube — and reading books and playing pickleball. That is what attention-based demand looks like. It signs nothing, it can leave at any moment, and when it leaves, no line on the income statement moves.

![Three concentric rings grow outward: the innermost holds only streaming rivals, the middle adds short-video platforms, and the outermost takes in reading and pickleball too, so Netflix's slice shrinks accordingly.](/figures/attention-arena-widened-en.svg)

So which kind of demand is AI demand? The show says there's no demand problem anywhere in sight, and that statement is made using current usage. But the Anthropic figures say, in plain language, *regardless of actual usage*. Claiming no demand problem while citing contractually locked spending as the proof conflates two things. A contract guarantees the cash flow happens. It guarantees nothing about whether that money looks well spent three years from now.

The second objection comes from Michael's takeaway from the Steve Eisman conversation: either all of it works or none of it works. Clean line — and also a confession of risk, because it concedes this isn't a set of independent bets you can diversify but one large position resting on a shared assumption. Josh added that the nuance doesn't matter until some future date, and that date isn't now. That part made me uneasy, because every argument sustained by "this doesn't matter yet" fails all at once. The price war Eisman worried about — labs cutting what they charge consumers, triggering some kind of spiral — hasn't happened. Hasn't happened isn't the same as can't.

![On the left a pipe is clamped shut by a padlock and water keeps flowing; on the right the same pipe has a valve that can be closed at any time, and the water coming out looks identical.](/figures/locked-vs-voluntary-demand-en.svg)

Third, about the spread chart that made Michael say the market was sobering up. Credit discipline travels from loose, to sorting good from bad, to shutting the window entirely, and there's no gate between those stages. A 17% yield on the worst-rated AI paper reads as pricing, and it also reads as a group of borrowers already being cut off. To tell which, you have to watch whether healthy issuers start getting turned away over the next few months. That's the signal that sorting has become closing.

Fourth, the other side of the breadth finding. Adam's statistic is real, but Josh described how perverse the mechanism is: rates and oil rise, people dump all the twenty-billion-dollar companies, buy Apple and Microsoft, and the index holds up. Good news for index owners, and quiet damage for most people holding individual names. The index of speculative names is in a 44% drawdown while the S&P is off two and a half percent. Whether "the market is fine" holds depends on which market you're asking about.

![On the left five blocks each stand on their own small foundation; on the right the same five blocks all rest on one shared foundation, so a single crack brings all five down together.](/figures/one-foundation-not-many-bets-en.svg)

Last, Bending Spoons — the Italian company Berkshire bought in the IPO and has since added to. It buys declining consumer internet services (AOL, Vimeo, Eventbrite, Evernote, WeTransfer, Meetup, Airtable, Miro), cuts most of the team, runs them with AI, raises prices on existing users, and routes the cash flow to the parent. Since early 2023 it has spent $6 billion on 15 acquisitions, each required to clear 25% annual returns before debt and 65% after. Airtable cost $1.3 billion, 90% below its peak value. Last year: $1.3 billion revenue, up 100%; operating income $613 million, also doubled. A beautiful model — and the bear math sits on the same page. Long-term debt approaches $5 billion, Q2 interest expense was $109 million, triple year over year, consuming 29% of adjusted operating income, and organic growth in Q2 was 3%. The growth comes off an acquisition conveyor belt, and the belt runs on debt. It stops when the deals run out, or when there are so many deals that interest eats the return.

## Where I come down, and on what condition

I'm on the bull side, with a narrow condition.

What holds up is the mechanism: prices rise, supply slows, demand doesn't change. That relationship works in any buildout with physical constraints, and the path it produces is deflation rather than a burst. The detail that persuades me is the flow — AI corporate paper getting sold while the Treasury selloff stopped. Money changed seats; it didn't leave the building.

The condition: the labs have to raise money in public markets, and the hyperscalers have to keep posting cloud growth. Fail either and the argument has nothing underneath it. Clear both and the nuance can wait. Which is why the thing worth watching over the next few weeks is whether the raise closes, not the share price.

Three things I personally get stuck on, where this episode gave me a better angle.

**Earnings looked good, so why did the stock fall?** Netflix answers it. The market buys the probability of the next leg of growth, not this quarter's profit. When nobody doubts a company's earning power and the growth story runs out of road, the stock gets repriced to what cash-flow buyers will pay, and that price is usually uncomfortable. Josh's "what have you done for me lately" says it plainly: you can report EPS and buyback figures till the cows come home. My own lesson is blunter — I've averaged down into names like this, always because they got cheap, and the reason they got cheap was that the growth numerator had been swapped out. Averaging down there means adding to an assumption I never re-tested.

**The index is flat, so why am I losing money?** The breadth discussion answers that one. Rates jump, money leaves small and mid caps for a handful of megacaps, the index sits still, and the twenty-billion-dollar companies in your account bleed. If your holdings don't resemble the index weights, the index isn't your thermometer. It also explains why bulls and bears were both unsatisfied: the bulls never got their breadth, the bears never got their decline.

**The news says the bubble is bursting — should I believe it?** The episode supplies a test you can run yourself: check whether credit is sorting. If spreads widen for good and bad borrowers alike, that's panic. If the bad end widens while the good end sits still, that's pricing. Michael called the chart a sobering-up, and I find that test more useful than any bubble declaration, because it produces fresh data daily and doesn't require you to guess at a top.

## 帶得走的一件事

**One idea: to judge whether something will hold, ask first whether the money is locked by contract or renewed voluntarily every day.**

Anthropic's hundreds of billions in cloud commitments are payable regardless of actual usage, so that spending will happen — whether it was worth it is a separate question. Netflix has no contract locking up anyone's evening, so when people gave those two hours to something else, the income statement showed nothing for a quarter while the stock lost half its value. These two kinds of demand look alike in the financials and differ enormously in durability.

Something I tried myself, with nothing to do with stocks: open your phone's subscription list, find three monthly autopayments, cancel one by hand — don't quit the service, just let it lapse for a month. After that month, see whether you go resubscribe on your own. The one you resubscribe to is voluntary demand. The one you forgot was being held up by the billing habit. The exercise gives you a firsthand measurement of the gap between locked demand and wanted demand, and that line doesn't show up in a financial statement. It shows up on your credit card bill.
