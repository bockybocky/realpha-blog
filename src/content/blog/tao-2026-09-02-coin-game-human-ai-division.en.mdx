## What this is about

Last week I wrote about Terence Tao calling AI mathematicians "slightly drunk geniuses" whose scores come with numerators but no denominators. This week the same series (AI 101 channel, uploaded 2026-08-26, about 14 minutes) dropped another episode. No metaphors this time — he lays out one real case, a coin game, and names exactly which steps humans did and which steps AI did.

I copied down his division of labor and checked it against the rules I use every day for delegating work to AI. To my surprise, the two lists are almost the same list.

## The coin game

The rules are simple. Alice splits a big pile of coins into n stacks, heights up to her. Bob takes away some stacks — but the stacks he picks must run strictly upward or strictly downward in height, never zigzag. Bob picks the combination that gets him the most; Alice arranges heights to lose the least. The question: what entry fee should Alice charge Bob to make it fair?

Someone on a forum ran linear programming on small cases and made a lovely conjecture: when the number of stacks is a perfect square k², Bob can take 1/k of the total. With 16 stacks, he walks off with a quarter. The conjecture sat unproven for two months.

![Eight coin stacks of uneven heights; four highlighted stacks of heights 4, 8, 10, 13 rise monotonically, connected by a dashed line — Bob may only take stacks that run one way](/figures/tao-coin-game-rule-en.svg)

That's the shape of the rule: Bob's constraint is monotone selection; Alice's job is to arrange heights so even the best monotone pick stays small.

## Then, who did which step

Here's how it broke open. Someone fed the conjecture to an automated prover called Aristotle — and it worked, producing a proof and translating it into Lean (a language that lets a machine check a proof line by line). Its trick was converting the coin problem into a "packing small squares into a big square" problem. The video notes this move appeared in an obscure 1959 paper, and the coin problem itself had already been solved by other means in 2016 — few remembered. Much of AI's creativity is spreading the library's unborrowed books back out on the table in front of you.

When the number of stacks grew and linear programming blew up, Tao switched to AlphaEvolve, computed up to 16 piles, stared at the resulting patterns, and made a conjecture of his own. His collaborator Boris simplified it, and another co-author, Lawrence Wu, recognized that the simplified function was identical to one from a different Erdős "square packing" problem solved just two years earlier. The pieces snapped together and the problem was done.

At the end, Tao compresses the whole episode into two lists. AI did four things: searched literature, filled missing proof steps, computed numerical tables, translated proofs into Lean. Humans did four things: posed the problem, made the conjecture, understood the proof, noticed two problems were the same one. His words: AI by themselves are already great, but there's far more potential in human-AI collaboration.

And about denominators — last week I complained AI math results ship without them. This episode actually provides some: of the 1,000-odd Erdős problems tracked, 699 remain unsolved and about 480 are solved, a large share of those being answers AI dug out of old literature that had been solved decades ago and forgotten; Aristotle's automated proving succeeds only about 1–2% of the time. Progress reports with denominators read a lot more honestly.

## What the two lists share — it took me an evening to see it

Line up AI's four tasks. Literature search: what it finds can be checked against the source. Proof steps: Lean verifies line by line. Numerical tables: recompute and compare. Lean translation: if it's wrong, it doesn't compile. All four share one trait — verification can be handed to a machine, so if the AI writes garbage it gets caught on the spot.

The human four are the opposite. Whether a problem is good, whether a conjecture is beautiful, whether you truly understood a proof, whether two problems are one — no machine can grade any of it. The output of these tasks is judgment itself, and only another judgment can review it.

![A dashed line splits eight tasks into two columns: left — search literature, fill proof steps, compute tables, translate to Lean, given to AI; right — pose problems, make conjectures, understand proofs, notice two problems are the same, kept by humans](/figures/tao-machine-verifiable-split-en.svg)

The rule I use for delegating to AI is nearly word-for-word the same. Fetching data, converting files, batch rewrites — I hand those over without worry, because acceptance can be scripted: is the file there, do the numbers match, does the format validate. But whether to buy a stock, whether to change a methodology, whether an article's conclusion holds — work whose output is the conclusion itself I never outsource to AI, because nothing can check it for me, and if it's wrong I won't even notice.

One more detail keeps growing on me. Aristotle didn't crack the coin game head-on — it pulled a forgotten 1959 paper off the shelf and found that the old solution happened to fit the new problem. That's the reversed order of work: not holding a problem and hunting for a solution, but holding a solution and hunting for its problem. I have a drawer of such old solutions myself — the technical indicators I've collected over the years. I used to ask, "can this indicator pick winning stocks for me?", and the answer kept disappointing. Now I've flipped the question: the stocks are already chosen by other means, and I walk around holding the indicators, looking for the problems they actually fit — timing an entry, trimming a drawdown. The indicators didn't change. What changed is that I'm finally finding them the right problem.

In Tao's team, Lean is the machine referee. With it, a six-page AI proof stuffed with rubbish is harmless — translate it to Lean, and what fails gets tossed. That's what I envied most watching this: mathematicians have Lean; those of us making investment judgments don't. So with the same AI, they can let it charge ahead and flail, while we have to keep it outside the judgment room. The difference isn't how smart the AI is. It's whether your field has a referee a machine can be.

## One thing to take with you

The evening I finished the video I did one small thing, and you could try it today: write on a sticky note the last three tasks you were about to hand to AI, and ask of each — when this is done, is there a machine or script that can tell me right from wrong? If yes, delegate freely. If no, the output of that task is your judgment; let AI be a staff officer, but keep the last move for yourself. One of my three failed the question, and I changed it on the spot from "ask AI to write the conclusion" to "ask AI to lay out the materials."
