## What the episode is about

In one line: **this is an episode about where the gaps are — in the market, in the supply chain, and in your own risk control.**

The first half is the market. The index barely moved, but small and mid caps were destroyed, and a lot of people went looking online for someone to blame. The host's approach is to lay out the arithmetic first: tech names down thirty or forty percent are everywhere in this move, most of those companies are still up year to date, and most of their shareholders are still in profit — so why were *you* the one carried out? The middle section turns to the supply chain, where the point is that the bottleneck has moved, from the accelerators everyone watches to the processors, and that the way it jams is not what you would guess. The listener questions come back to method: how to actually use the multiples in sell-side reports, how to break the habit of refusing to take a loss, and whether a theme everybody already knows still counts as an edge.

The Zhuangzi passage fits. Everywhere money is made in this episode sits on the "between the joints there are gaps" side — the line nobody has written into their model, the layer that is genuinely constrained, and the size you can personally carry. And every way of losing money here looks like the ordinary cook: swinging hard at the one bone everyone can see.

## The main points

**One: what a drawdown really tests is not the loss, it is whether investing has bled into your life.** The host is blunt about it: this does not only happen when you are losing. It happens when you are winning too — you finally get to Bali and you will not go in the water because you would rather sit in front of the screen. On the way down it shows up as nickel-and-diming the family and cancelling the trip you already promised, when you know perfectly well your position swings cover that spend many times over. His own method is to carry the pressure himself; on a red day the most his son gets is a joke about Dad needing some space — which led to his son asking him every morning whether today is red or green. He finds it funny, but he does not want the kid growing up thinking a bad tape means leave Dad alone. **The real test buried in that section is sharp: is your position big enough that other people have to accommodate your mood?**

**Two: the boss being flamed and your own outcome are probably not the same event.** Plenty of people went online this time to find a culprit, some of it escalating to threats. His rebuttal is arithmetic: names down thirty or forty percent over the same window are everywhere, most of those companies are still positive year to date, and therefore most shareholders are still making money. **Same candle, and one person is uncomfortable while another is liquidated — the difference is size and leverage, not that executive.** Conflating a sector's drawdown with your own outcome is comfortable precisely because it excuses you from reviewing your execution. The price of that comfort is that the same thing happens again next cycle.

**Three: leverage has inflated across generations, and the homemade top indicator that keeps working.** He jokes that he used to think of himself as a daredevil, until he realised that to the current generation 2.5x barely qualifies as kindergarten. More interesting is the indicator he keeps: every time a bull run peaks, the inbox fills with questions about leveraged index products, about how to borrow more, about going full-time. This time a listener asked about going full-time and the market promptly blew up. His conclusion is not that a crash is imminent — it is that a shake this size is only a rehearsal. **Small cycles drop you into small holes, big cycles into big ones**; this one just stress-tested everyone's stomach.

**Four: the index being fine does not mean you are fine — and the defensive rotation has its own cost.** The benchmark barely fell while the over-the-counter board and US small-cap tech were badly damaged. This is the kind of tape where the index lies to you. The money flow he describes is consistent: on the way down, park in the index and the megacap leader; once things steady, pull it back out and buy something else. The destination is consistent too — the older generation of AI hardware names that have been resting for a long time, sitting near ten times forward earnings, with visible results and a floor under them. But he immediately names the cost of that consistency: **the money arriving here is by definition the money that refuses to chase, so who exactly is going to push the price up?** The answer is that it can only be pushed slowly, one revenue print and one event at a time. Which means even when these names work, they rarely rip.

**Five: the results will be good, the prices may not follow — so he writes scripts instead of forecasts.** He says plainly that a run of good numbers is coming: a new platform ramping, a processor generation turning over, several custom silicon lines entering volume, and even badly beaten-down passive component names printing strong monthly year-on-year growth. And in the same breath: fundamentals on track, even ahead of expectations, tells you very little about the price, and names whose chart structure has broken may simply need months of consolidation. So he pre-writes the scripts — how to trade a range, how to chase if it genuinely breaks out, whether to lean small cap or large depending on which shows strength. **His description of the current mood is that everyone feels wrongfully convicted: the numbers are fine and the stock still will not move.**

**Six: the bottleneck has moved to the processor, and the way the chain jams is counterintuitive.** He argues the real constraint right now sits at the processor: foundry capacity is hard to secure and the memory to go with it is hard to assemble, so putting a complete unit together is genuinely difficult. The ratio is what matters most — it used to be roughly one processor to seven or eight accelerators; now people talk about one to four, one to two, and some are starting to talk about one to one, because agentic AI workflows are themselves processor-hungry. **And a jammed chain does not destroy revenue, it flattens it**: he has heard component makers complain that orders are excellent, but because the chain is stuck, recognition trickles in rather than arriving as one large block. The valuation implication runs opposite to intuition — demand has not weakened, but the steep curve you penciled in gets stretched into a longer, gentler slope.

## Going further

### One: the bone everyone can see, versus the line nobody wrote down

There is a clean example in the episode. A custom chip surfaced under a codename nobody had seen, and the reflex was to go read across to the cloud operator behind it. Wrong target, he says — for that operator it is an internal-use chip, and even if they eventually sell it, the revenue contribution rounds to nothing. The question worth asking is who *builds* it. And then he undercuts himself: **the market took that apart a long time ago, and whether something everybody already knows can still act as a catalyst is an open question.**

That question is worth sitting with. Why is "everyone knows" not the same as "already priced"? Because knowing and modelling are separated by quarters. A story can be worn out on the forums and still not be in anyone's earnings estimate, still not be in the multiple. The line he gives in the Q&A is the positive statement of the same idea: use the reports to learn how others are modelling it and where expectations sit — and then, **if there is something you know that not one of those reports mentions, that is the alpha**, because it implies a surprise revision later, and every dollar of that revision gets multiplied.

So the test is not whether the news is new. It is: **if this is true, will the next four quarters of recognised revenue be revised up, and is that piece in the current estimates?** A gap means there is still meat. No gap and it is just a headline. He also characterises the reports themselves honestly — the multiples are frequently plucked from nowhere, and some shops specialise in sky-high targets that will never be reached because a report needs to be talked about. A report is a map, not an answer.

One failure condition, stated honestly: if what you think is an information gap is actually something others know but consider unimportant, you are not betting on information, you are betting on judgement. That is a different wager and it needs its own evidence.

The funniest part is that the least investment-like segment in the episode makes this clearest. He spends real airtime asking listeners whether they prefer grainy watermelon or watery watermelon, and speculates that his own preference was formed in childhood by a thrifty mother who probably bought whichever kind was cheaper — so that became what watermelon was supposed to taste like. His own conclusion: everyone agrees fries should be crisp and peaches should be soft. Only on watermelon is the consensus split.

**Price is a product of consensus; disagreement is where the opportunity lives.** Where everyone nods you cannot earn an excess return, because that agreement is already in the price. What you can earn sits entirely in the cases where you think A, the market thinks B, and A turns out to be right. So research time belongs on contested questions, not on the ones everybody agrees with — the latter read better, because every report you open confirms you were right.

### Two: which layer is constrained and which layer captures pricing are two different questions

The processor is the bottleneck. That does not automatically mean it is where you make money. His discussion of the processor supply chain contains a distinction I think is genuinely valuable.

One class of components rises with the tide and collapses when it goes out — commodity-like parts where higher volume produces a cyclical price spike. Another class is tied to the specification: the socket that pairs with a processor, roughly one to one, where every generation adds pins and difficulty, and average selling price steps up. **That step has nothing to do with the cycle; it was bought with a specification change.**

So the test compresses to one sentence: **when the cycle next turns down, will this price increase be given back?** If yes, it is cyclical — you are being paid for timing and you have to trade the cycle. If no, it is a step — the baseline has been permanently raised and you can hold a longer view.

Why does conflating them matter? Because on the income statement they look identical: strong year-on-year revenue, sharply improved margins. But for the cyclical kind, the peak margin is not sustainable, and extrapolating three years of earnings from it overstates badly. For the step kind the error runs the other way — valuing it on a historical average margin understates it. **The same financial statement, misclassified, pushes your valuation in two opposite directions.**

Same honesty about failure conditions: a step is not permanent. If the specification moves to a form that no longer needs that part — a packaging change, the function absorbed into the main die — the step disappears outright. And the tighter the binding, the greater the platform owner's incentive to absorb it. "Structural" here means "this generation will not be given back," not "safe forever."

Another moment in the episode lets you test the ruler. A model discussed the previous week had been read as evidence that far less compute would be needed going forward; within a day or two the provider itself announced that compute was short and had to be reserved for existing customers. **Narratives can be falsified very quickly, and whether the falsified item is demand or the calendar determines whether you rewrite the thesis or just redraw the schedule.**

### Three: discretion is earned with a track record, not with seniority

A listener asks how to break the habit of refusing to cut a loser. The answer is honest, and easy to misread.

In theory you keep discipline: the condition triggers, you leave. But he says that over the past few years he has become *less* fond of hard stops, because he has a deeper read on his own research hit rate — he gets things right most of the time — so unless there is a clearly better use of the capital, touching the monthly moving average is not enough to make him act. In this drawdown he let several positions run down close to the quarterly line before dealing with them, and he admits the cost openly: by the time it actually got back to that line it hurt, and he gave up a large chunk of the gain.

His advice to beginners is the exact opposite: build a hard mechanism. His analogy is good — a 3.5-inch floppy will not go in backwards; a disc read the wrong way up simply will not read. **A poka-yoke is not an insult to your judgement, it is a design that makes the wrong action physically impossible**, because the judgement it defends against is not your judgement when calm. It is your judgement at the moment the pain finally breaks you.

There is a middle step people skip: why does the veteran get discretion and the beginner not? It is not seniority. **The precondition for discretion is an auditable record showing that your judgement is in fact confirmed by the market most of the time.** He has that record, and he can also price what the discretion cost him this time. Someone without that record who adopts the same flexibility has simply given "refusing to cut" a more respectable name — because in the moment the two behaviours are indistinguishable: the condition hit and you did not leave. You cannot tell them apart from the action, only from the record.

So the usable line is this: **if you cannot say how many times you broke your own rule and were later proved right, and what share of the total that is, you have not earned discretion yet.** And note that his flexibility is not unbounded either — he says explicitly that his risk control now runs mostly through a **cap on position size**, which confines "allowed to sit through it" to a scale where sitting through the worst case does not break anything. Discretion is not the removal of risk control; it moves risk control from the price axis to the size axis. Which is the same point as the leverage discussion in a different costume: being right and being able to survive being right are independent conditions.

The good cook changes his knife once a year because he follows the joints. The ordinary cook changes his every month because he spends every month swinging at the same bone. In markets, the place where blades break most often tends to be exactly the spot everyone can see, and everyone is already hitting.
