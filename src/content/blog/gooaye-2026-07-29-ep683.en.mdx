## What the episode is about

In one line: **this drawdown is not deeper than last year's — it is the one where the reference points went missing.**

The first half is the host opening up his own wound. He had been up more than 200% at the peak this year; he is now sitting on a maximum drawdown of roughly 25–28%, with net worth back at where it was in early May. And he had already rotated most of the book into the largest foundry — which he jokingly calls "Helm's Deep," the last fortress of the bulls — and still gave back that much. The middle section holds two pieces of real research: one resolves the CPU-to-GPU "one-to-one" claim that never matched the actual rack specs, the other takes apart the new ghost story about domestically built lithography tools in China. The listener questions are almost entirely about temperament: a beginner who entered in June and is already down 30%, a veteran holding the one large cap that is still standing and joking about reserving a bench in the park, and someone who sold at the exact low and now wonders whether he is built for this at all.

The Laozi passage fits. Nothing in this episode tells you how to win. **From start to finish it is about how to stay alive** — no leverage, no single position above ten percent, and a book small enough that you can still sleep. And "what heaven dislikes, who knows the reason" is the most honest part of it: the host himself cannot say what the market is actually selling off about. "Even the sage finds this hard" is roughly the mood.

## The main points

**1. What breaks people is not the size of the decline, it is not being able to see the endpoint.** Comparing this to last March and April's tariff crash, his conclusion is that the earlier one was scarier — even though many names have fallen further in absolute terms this time. Liquidity had been drained, names were limit-down repeatedly, and **at the time you had no idea whether three limit-down days would become six, or nine**. From that he identifies a systematic bias in something a lot of people do: backtesting "could I have held through that crash" against the worst historical drawdowns. On the chart you can see the bounce on the right; the people living through it could not. He also confesses something he came to regard as shameful: during that stretch he went around opening credit lines at banks — he was in no danger of a margin call — and even asked partners in an already-agreed joint venture whether they could postpone wiring the money. He has been more careful with leverage ever since.

**2. The rule he gave two weeks earlier — "if it's below the quarterly moving average I won't even look at it" — was void two weeks later.** This is my favourite turn in the episode. He had stated that filter on an earlier show, and now **almost everything is below its quarterly average, so if you keep the rule you have nothing left to look at**. Worse, the handful of names still above it are not necessarily safe. He uses the copper-clad laminate and substrate names from a few weeks earlier as the example: the whole market knew they were the shelter, so money piled in; once the shelter was full, it started catching down too. **The very definition of a safe haven guarantees it will be the next thing sold.** He describes the current tape as walking through a minefield — whatever you do, something can get you.

**3. Capitulation volume never showed up because most holders are giving back gains, not taking losses.** Historically, a decline this deep should have produced a volume spike — someone surrendering — and only then a real bounce. The volume never came. The explanation an older hand gave him: those names that have halved are only back at April and May prices. Unless you were piling in at the top over the last two months, you are still up on paper — it is just that "up a lot" became "up a little." The line he quotes lands well: **"You've had two or three months without a rally. People have been spoiled."** But he also names the frightening side of it: if everyone reasons "my cost basis is low, I can wait you out," then **everybody grinds everybody down, and the decline may simply not end.**

**4. The valuation anchor may have flipped — the most important argument in the episode.** For the past six months or so, a popular framing was that the two most central AI names (the largest foundry and the leading AI chip designer) trade at only low-to-mid-teens forward earnings while a crowd of peripheral names ran to thirty or forty times, so "the core is too cheap and has to catch up." After July, the same numbers support the opposite reading: **maybe the core multiples are the correct price, and it is everyone else who has to converge downward.** What unsettles him more is memory — the single thing the entire market agrees is scarcest, and not expensive on forward earnings or book value, and it got destroyed anyway. He has direct evidence of his own: he set a valuation filter, and when names broke below it he moved money out of his defensive parking spots to buy them — and they fell another 15% or 20% with no sign of stopping. **When something you bought because it was cheap keeps getting cheaper, you have to seriously consider that the whole table is shifting down rather than that you picked wrong.** He offers one extreme reminder: during the dot-com bust, a leading e-commerce name fell about 90% from its high, and its founder's letter to shareholders opened with a single word — "Ouch" — while noting that every internal metric was actually improving.

**5. Position size is not a discipline question, it is a physiological signal.** His test is plainly stated: **if it is affecting your life — you can't eat, you're getting up at night to check the overnight session — your position is too big.** The threshold varies by person. He has measured his own: at roughly 1.8 to 2 times leverage the pressure becomes real, so he normally keeps it under 1.3 or lower. This drawdown hurts, but it does not feel like annihilation. He also punctures a common confusion: **fully invested is 100% for everybody.** Fully invested is 100% whether you have a billion or a million; declines are measured in percentages, and size does not protect you. He has heard more than once of large accounts that combined one bad call with leverage and were wiped back to zero overnight.

**6. The answer to the CPU "one-to-one" puzzle is not inside the rack.** This is the one genuine research segment, and the best demonstration of method. Executives at both major CPU vendors have said the CPU-to-GPU ratio is heading toward one-to-one, yet the actual published AI rack specs pair one CPU with four GPUs. The mismatch is obvious. He checked with people in the industry, and the explanation is that **the one-to-one is not a within-rack ratio, it is a whole-datacenter aggregate** — agentic workflows need a large fleet of general-purpose servers alongside to absorb work, and those racks are packed with CPUs. He adds a supporting data point: a whitepaper from a memory maker and a social platform showing that offloading from DRAM to SSD is tens of times slower, so the "you want a big prep counter" logic holds, and the CPU growth shows up on the general-purpose line rather than by rebuilding AI racks at one-to-one. Following the same angle, he offers an inverted reading of the panic around a new open-source model release: **if what it drives is inference and agentic usage, then for some layers it is an accelerant, not bad news.**

**7. The year you enter the market shapes how you see stocks forever.** A listener who started in June — no leverage, fully invested in index constituents, down about 30% — asks: I value them on next year's earnings and they look cheap, but they just keep falling; am I being held hostage by fundamentals and unable to cut? The host's answer is that this is no fault of his own — **from June until now, anybody buying anything was going to lose money, and you don't get to choose the circumstances of your birth into this market.** He cites an asset manager known for distressed debt: that person's first exposure was watching the era's supposedly unbeatable large caps blow up, and he carried a deep wariness of equities for life, which is exactly why his path ran toward credit and distressed companies. **Same market, two years apart, two completely different investors.**

## Going further

### 1. Cheap is relative, so when the anchor moves the whole table has to be redrawn

The most valuable sentence in this episode is not "things are cheap." It is the doubt: **what if the ones that have to converge downward are everyone else.**

Cheap is never an absolute. It is always relative to something. The anchor used to be the low-teens multiple on the core AI assets, from which followed "thirty or forty times on the periphery is too expensive," or equivalently "the core is too cheap." But a relative relationship can close in two directions: **the outer ring rises, or the core falls and drags the outer ring down with it.** The market had assumed the first. This episode raises the second. Same numbers, opposite direction.

So how do you tell which one you are in? The most direct observable I can think of is sequence: **if the most certain names stabilise first and the periphery follows, that is a healthy convergence**; if the core keeps breaking down while the periphery falls harder, the table has not found its new anchor yet.

But this needs a "why not the other explanation," or the argument gets abused. The price of a core asset is not necessarily a valuation signal — and this episode supplies the counter-evidence itself. The host says the largest foundry has become everybody's parking lot, money simply resting there; in which case **its decline may be withdrawals, not a re-rating.** To distinguish the two you have to check whether the fall is accompanied by downward revisions in the fundamentals — orders, capex, pricing. A fall without revisions is flow; a fall with revisions is the anchor actually moving. Skip that step and you will mistake one rotation of money for a permanent drop in the sector's valuation ceiling.

For my own process the conclusion is simple: if my reason for owning something contains the phrase "cheap relative to X," I have to write down what X is, and add one line — "if X changes in this way, this reason is void." **A relative valuation with no anchor written down is not a reason at all.**

### 2. Turn "can I hold through this" from a psychology question into arithmetic

The backtest-bias point punctures a self-test many people run that cannot actually work.

Whether you can hold depends on two things, one computable and one not. The computable one is arithmetic: **your equity loss ≈ position decline × leverage.** Run it backwards — ask what percentage of equity drawdown you can absorb without doing something stupid, divide by your leverage, and that is the price decline you can genuinely tolerate. The host's 1.3x is not a moral rule, it is a measured parameter: he knows the pressure becomes too much at 1.8 to 2, so he left himself margin below it. **Turning a subjective feeling into a number is what allows it to be written into the position.**

The non-computable one is liquidity, and this has to be said clearly or the arithmetic above will give you false comfort. When names were locked limit-down day after day last March and April, **the stop you carefully calculated could not be executed** — there was no counterparty. The host running around opening bank credit lines is the physical form of exactly that problem: he was not short of money, he was buying insurance against the scenario where you cannot sell. So stops are a tool for when prices still move; **the only thing that protects you when liquidity disappears is the position size you chose beforehand.** The two are not substitutes, and plenty of people treat a stop as full coverage when it insures half the scenarios.

There is a small domestic detail late in the episode that is the same idea in another form. The golden pothos on his balcony had nearly died, and he only remembered to water it when the person who arranges his feng shui pointed it out — because he had spent the whole stretch in combat mode. **When a position is too large, the first thing it consumes is not money, it is your attention.** Not eating, not sleeping, not noticing the plant on the balcony — those signals arrive earlier than the account statement, and they do not lie.

### 3. In a selloff, verify what the market is actually pricing before deciding to be afraid

There is a common pattern in how this episode handles news, and it is worth extracting as a tool.

Start with oil. Geopolitical conflict pushing crude higher was briefly used to explain the decline — **but crude later came back down and the market did not bounce**, so at most it was one contributing factor, not the driver. That is a textbook falsification, and it can be completed the same day.

The general version he states plainly: if a piece of news hurts some people, in theory it benefits others. **So go look at whether the theoretical beneficiaries actually rallied. If they did, the market is really pricing that news; if everything goes down together, it may not be about that news at all — the tape is simply fragile enough that anything can push it over.** The virtue of this test is that it settles the same day, without waiting for a story to be written afterwards. His own conclusion is honest too: spending a lot of energy hunting for reasons during a decline is not worth much.

The same logic applied to the ghost story about domestically built deep-ultraviolet lithography in China produces something more interesting. Whether the tool can be built is not the gate: the industry view is that multi-patterning workarounds trading yield and cost for resolution have existed in prototype form for a while, and that is not news. **The real gate is on the demand side** — if the US government never opens the door for handset brands or cloud operators to adopt Chinese memory, then whatever gets built can only serve the domestic market, and China is grinding against itself. Open that door, and the dumping scenario that wrecks an entire chain becomes live. **The most dramatic headline — they built it — is not the gate that decides the outcome. A policy switch is.**

This is the same thinking as bottleneck analysis, run in reverse. On the supply side you ask which layer breaks first when demand doubles. On a piece of bad news you ask: for this to actually hurt me, what chain of conditions must all hold, and which link is weakest? Find that link and what you have to track shrinks from an entire news feed to one observable variable.

One last line, the best metaphor in the episode. A listener mentions that in souls-like games the deadliest habit is greeding for one more hit — you swing once too often and get punished. The host picks it up well: this is very much a moment to avoid greeding. A memory name hit limit-up a few days earlier, and if you saw the strength and jumped in, you got hit. And he notices something counterintuitive: **he normally picks the names with the cleaner-looking charts, but in this stretch the better the chart looked, the harder it got punished afterwards.** Why? Because the things that have not fallen yet are exactly where the money is hiding, which makes them the next ATM. **In a broad deleveraging, strength is not safety. It is just not your turn yet.**
