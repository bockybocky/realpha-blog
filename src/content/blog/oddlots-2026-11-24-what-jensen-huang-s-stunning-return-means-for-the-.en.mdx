> Once you are inside the ring of a thousand mountains, each one you pass simply lets you through to the next one blocking your way.

The Odd Lots episode published on 24 November 2026 was an emergency pod, recorded on 23 November at 12:36pm, with Tracy Alloway and Joe Weisenthal hosting Alexander Altmann, Barclays' global head of equity tactical strategies. That morning Nvidia's board reinstated Jensen Huang and the market ripped; Joe put the Nasdaq 100 up 6.9% as they spoke. Altmann's read came in two layers. A move that size can't be explained as a dead cat bounce, because the VIX touched 40 on Friday and was still at 30 during the recording, consistent with close to a 2% move every day. Yet the two bearish questions Barclays put out in August — how all this capex gets financed, and whether the revenue trajectory supports it — are not answered by Huang's return. He left his own dividing line: the language around the 9 December Fed meeting. If the Fed is out of tune with market internals, this bounce gets proved a dead cat after all.

## The strongest version of his bull case: positioning moves first, the story arrives later

He starts by separating size from direction. With the VIX at 40, the implied daily average move in the S&P is a little over 3%; at 30, it's close to 2%. The S&P fell 7% last week, which he called the worst week since COVID. In that regime, what Joe named an "up crash" and a violent sell-off are two faces of one thing, and moves of this magnitude show up in both directions. So he declines to call the day a dead cat bounce, and the reasoning stops at the volatility regime rather than running on to "therefore AI is fixed."

![Two vertical double-headed arrows stand side by side, the left one labeled VIX 40 clearly longer than the right one labeled VIX 30, and both are marked up at the top and down at the bottom, showing that one volatility regime swings just as far in either direction.](/figures/vol-regime-two-directions-en.svg)

Then he walks through what happened in those last two hours on Friday, the densest stretch of the episode. Around $35bn of levered ETF selling hit the tape. Thursday, on Barclays' numbers, was the 58th worst day for S&P liquidity of the past ten years — the examples he gave being the depths of COVID and a couple of days during the tariff episode. Dealers were short around the 7,100 strike in the S&P (by his own account pieced together from evidence rather than known for sure), so once price moved through it, gamma got shorter and every further leg down created more delta for sale. Stack the levered ETF flow on top and you have a negative feedback loop: in the final two hours, S&P vols moved the way they did during COVID. He added that he'd skip the second-order Greeks so as not to put listeners to sleep. Those sell orders were mechanical; nobody pressing the button was thinking about AI's return on investment.

Which brings him to the line I found most useful: price determines narrative, or in his own formulation, positioning determines price, which then determines narrative. Going into the weekend, vol control exposures were at rock bottom, CTAs were short, dealer gamma was negative and sentiment was bombed out — and all of those feed Barclays' quantitative framework. In August that framework told them to be bearish, and he thought there was a compelling fundamental story to back the bearishness up. Today the same framework says buy, tactically, with a big asterisk reading that the vol environment is still high, so take your wins where you can.

![A falling price line steepens after it crosses a dashed line marked 7,100, and past that crossing point three sell arrows of increasing length branch downward, with a loop arrow beside them marking the feedback of selling more as it falls.](/figures/gamma-flip-forced-selling-en.svg)

His analogy is tariffs. After the tariff relief tweet the market bounced, everyone said the tariffs weren't going away and the problem remained, and the market was off to the races anyway. Price moves before earnings do; earnings revisions always lag. Semis fell 30 to 40%, which he described, without tongue in cheek, as a dramatic reset in market expectations for the forward capex path. Joe picked up the market's gasping at SK Hynix on something like 2.2x forward earnings. Leverage got flushed too: 7709 HK, the triple-levered Hynix ETF, is down 90 to 95% (he hedged with "I think"), and the triple-short Nvidia product was zeroed out. Good things, he said — leverage and frothy sentiment coming out of the system.

![Three nodes in the top row are chained left to right along a time axis, while the arrows between the same three nodes in the bottom row all run leftward against time, showing two reading orders that point opposite ways.](/figures/positions-price-narrative-order-en.svg)

Finally, why Washington won't sit still. 35% of US household net worth is in stocks, two-thirds of US households own stocks, and on data now going back to 1900, this is the most exposure US households have had to equities since the late 1920s. His conclusion: AI has become the stock market, and this administration cares about the stock market. Put those together and what follows is hard to call a surprise.

## The other side: each mountain you pass lets you through to the next one

The sharpest objections come from him. He says the two August questions weren't answerable then and aren't answerable now, and that the problems present yesterday will be the problems present tomorrow. Huang's return changes neither financing nor revenue. He also spells out the bear-market mechanism himself: bear markets produce big rips that fool investors and flip their sentiment, and during the dot-com bust the Nasdaq had multiple 15 to 20% rallies before the ultimate low.

![A jagged line trends down to the right with three clear upward bounces along the way, but each bounce peaks lower than the one before it and the lowest point of the whole line sits at the far right.](/figures/bear-market-rallies-en.svg)

The cracks showed in credit before equities. Tracy points out this unwind started there: spreads on hyperscaler debt widened first, and investors pushed back on high yield issued by the neoclouds. Altmann adds that the CoreWeave bonds haven't ameliorated and that SpaceX's recent bond placing says the same thing about a more expensive financing environment. Barclays' October call was that bond yields peak when the AI trade cracks — place that much paper into the market to finance this build and the competition for capital is real. Then there's the other half: do we need all this compute capacity, who are the weakest links in the chain, which neoclouds get hurt. He says the market is acutely aware of all of it now, and none of it disappears because the tape bounced 5% today.

![Two parallel timelines run one above the other: on the upper credit track, all three event markers sit toward the left, while on the lower equity track the markers sit noticeably farther right, and arrows between the two tracks mark the time gap.](/figures/credit-cracks-lead-equities-en.svg)

The Fed section is what worries him most. On a rolling one-month basis this has been the fastest tightening of financial conditions since the December 2018 analog, almost as large as March. Waller floated 50 basis points; Barr and Hammack joined a whole row of officials on an aggressive hawkish footing, and the market is now pricing roughly three and a half hikes over the next year. On Joe's screen the odds of a hike on 9 December were above 100%, against about 91% on Friday. Altmann says the hike itself is a foregone conclusion and the sensitivity sits in the messaging alongside it: whether the Fed sounds alive to financing conditions for all corporates, or tone deaf to them. The picture that bothers him is specific — in 2018 Stan Druckenmiller and Kevin Warsh co-wrote an op-ed warning about the risks of over-tightening, and the man who co-wrote it now sits inside the Fed; repeating that error would be ironic to the point of almost improbable. That is also the test he sets for the dead cat bounce.

The political wild card is the hardest to handle. Tracy lays the tension out: the Trump administration looks all in on propping up the AI trade, and last week brought a headline about requiring US companies to disclose their use of Chinese open source models, and fighting the government is usually a bad idea. In the same breath she says plenty of people now describe Trump as a lame duck, and a president personally involved in the corporate structure of a major US business is a giant red flag to any Democrat; Joe says there will be investigations out the wazoo, in the final two years of this administration and after 2028. Joe asks Altmann whether to believe in a White House put, and his answer goes only as far as the administration caring about the stock market, never to the protection. What he does say is operational: it's very hard to deploy risk units when vol is this high and sentiment flips on a post, in a year that has already been difficult for most strategies, and you don't bet the farm on flippant narratives this late in the year.

As for cheapness, he closes that road himself. Using valuation or P/E as a yardstick for when to buy and sell risk, in semiconductors, produces close to a perfectly loss-making track record. Valuation works as an afterthought once a bullish construct exists — I'm already bullish, and by the way these names trade on insanely low multiples, so even haircutting earnings I'm buying at attractive levels.

And one thing got forgotten wholesale, which tells you how fast this market changes channels. Among Friday's headlines was the news that someone at Anthropic had solved a proof of the Riemann hypothesis. Altmann files that in what he calls an ethereal bubble, alongside "what if AI breaks the cryptography underlying blockchain" — present, but not discussed in a trading capacity. His line: when the VIX is 40, no one cares. Tracy asks, half joking, who actually makes money from proving the Riemann hypothesis beyond the prize money, and admits she doesn't know the answer. Joe sharpens it: encryption, cyber attacks and advances in theoretical maths are huge deals, and none of it has translated into obvious business changes, with no wave of white collar layoffs yet. Altmann then names something nobody is discussing that is more tangible than the Riemann hypothesis — oil is back below 80 dollars, after months of everyone worrying about the Strait of Hormuz.

## Where I think the balance sits, and the condition it turns on

The two sides answer questions on different clocks, so both can hold. The bull case answers the next two to four weeks, and he says his job as a tactician is to compress the timeframe to two-to-four weeks and navigate the ebb and flow. The bear case answers whether this AI capital cycle gets a real clear-out first. He named the dividing line himself: the messaging on 9 December. In tune, and the asymmetry he describes holds long enough for a relief leg. Out of tune, and last week's mechanical selling runs again inside a more expensive financing environment — with nobody left to reinstate.

![A horizontal main line runs into a node and splits into two branches, one heading up to the right and one heading down to the right, each labeled with a different outcome.](/figures/dec-9-fork-en.svg)

Bringing it home, the first sore spot is this: the good news is out, so why am I still afraid to chase? The antidote this episode gave me is the positioning line. Friday's selling included $35bn from levered ETFs and dealers forced to sell more the lower it went around 7,100, and none of those people had AI in their heads. I used to work the other way — read the story, then go find the numbers — which meant I always bought on the day the story was most complete, and that's usually the day the positioning has already finished moving. Now I ask first: whose positioning was forced to move in this window? The things to check are concrete — the daily move implied by where the VIX sits, how bombed out sentiment surveys are, whether mechanical money is heavy or light.

The second sore spot: it's down 30 or 40% from the high and the P/E looks cheap, can I step in? I hold a few semiconductor equipment and software names that fell a long way from their highs, and "cheap" was the reason I gave myself at the time. This episode reverses the order: valuation is the comfort layer that comes after a bullish construct, and something else has to hold first. Tracy's closing line is the one I wrote down — you can't talk about the AI trade anymore without talking about the financing alongside it, and that looks like a cap on future valuations. That's her judgement, with no number attached, but it points at something trackable: spreads on hyperscaler and neocloud debt, which crack before equities do.

Here's the falsifiable part: if the Fed's language softens after 9 December and AI-related credit spreads keep widening anyway, then financing has little to do with rates and the problem lives in compute demand itself, and the tracking order above needs rewriting. One image I want to keep: before recording, Tracy heard that everyone at the Tomorrow X Summit in Austin was wearing leather jackets in solidarity with Jensen, and she was wearing one in the studio herself, purely because she was cold. Closing the interview, she told Altmann he was getting dangerously close to the healthy correction cliche, so they left it there.

## One thing to take away

**Explanations are selected after the fact. What moves first is positioning.**

The evidence in this episode all points one way. On Friday the S&P's vols took on a COVID shape, pushed by $35bn of levered ETF selling and short gamma around 7,100. The following Monday the Nasdaq 100 rose 6.9%, pushed by vol control exposure at rock bottom, CTAs short and sentiment bombed out. The headlines on those two days read in opposite directions while the mechanism underneath was the same one: somebody was forced to act, and the story got written to explain him. Altmann puts the causation in order — positioning determines price, price determines narrative.

Something I've tried, and it works outside markets too. Next time you hear an account of why X happened, hold off on judging whether it's right and write three lines on paper: before this happened, who was already committed to what; how much; and what would force them to let go. It can be the stock market, or a sudden reshuffle at the office, or the argument at home that dragged up old grievances. Then go back to the account and see whether it explains a mechanism or just supplies a comfortable reason for something that already happened. Three lines is enough, today.
