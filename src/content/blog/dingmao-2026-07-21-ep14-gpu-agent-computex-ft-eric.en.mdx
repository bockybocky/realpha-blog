## What this episode is about

*Dingmao Industry Notes* is a Taiwanese industry-research podcast. In this episode the host and research manager Eric compare notes from Computex 2026. They covered two venues: Nangang Exhibition Centre, home turf for Taiwanese suppliers, power solutions and components; and TICC, where NVIDIA ran its own event showing next-generation rack architectures and on-premise applications. They recorded on 16 July — which happened to be TSMC's earnings call day.

**Original episode**: Dingmao Industry Notes EP14, "From GPU Compute to Agent Execution: The Server Division of Labour Revealed at Computex, ft. Eric" (2026-07-21, in Mandarin).

I started listening to it as an ordinary trade-show recap. What made me stop was one small fact in the middle: a server CPU's companion memory module had its capacity halved. That isn't news — it's a costly action. And costly actions are more honest than any roadmap. What follows is my own reading, not a recap of the show.

## The notes I took

**The most direct consequence of agentic workloads is that the CPU has been pushed from supporting cast to lead.** Data-centre CPU-to-GPU ratios used to run around one to eight, or one to four. The show's read is that the ratio is heading toward one to two, and in some workloads the two will be roughly equal — or the CPUs will outnumber the GPUs. The logic isn't hard: a model that plans, dispatches and executes step after step puts a great deal of "decide what happens next" work on CPUs. The clearest picture on the show floor was NVIDIA's CPU rack: 256 CPUs packed into a single cabinet, where a same-generation GPU rack holds a few dozen chips. Two knock-on effects came up too. CPU vendors are locking capacity to the more expensive server parts, squeezing consumer supply; and every CPU needs memory beside it, which props up demand for DDR5 and server memory modules.

**Halving a memory module is the most honest moment in the episode.** That CPU was originally specified with a 192GB module; what showed up was roughly 96GB. Two things collided: memory supply was already short, and CPU volumes are climbing fast. The company's choice was to accept an uglier spec rather than miss the shipping window. That ordering is worth writing down: when supply is tight, delivery is worth more than a spec sheet.

**The management layer has grown a hierarchy of its own.** Once you have many nodes, the hard part isn't raw speed, it's who supervises the pile. Baseboard management controllers (BMCs) used to be one per box; the newer generation has versions that handle two nodes each. Above that, suppliers are pushing a satellite-style hierarchy — one primary BMC with a swarm of small management chips distributed around the system, monitored remotely, at lower cost. On the same product line sits a new part that folds an FPGA directly into the small management chip. The reason is mundane: those two used to sit separately, and merging them saves both effort and the extremely precious real estate on the board. **Board space is a resource that can run short too**, and nobody usually talks about it.

**The bleeding edge is slipping in two places at once.** Two items were put side by side. First, the blade-style rack architecture: with backplane interconnect, mechanical design and material qualification all still uncertain, there's a fair chance it slips to the following generation. Second, the next flagship GPU was planned with four compute dies in one package, pushing packaged area toward nine times reticle size — but with packaging problems such as warpage, the market is starting to think it may stay at two dies, in which case the generation's only real upgrade is memory moving from HBM4 to HBM4e. Both point at the same sentence: **this generation's ceiling isn't in the design, it's in whether the thing can be built and qualified.**

**At the 800V wall, people pressed their faces against the glass.** One full wall on the ground floor of Nangang's second hall was given over to power and components, all themed on 800V. Vendors were demonstrating the conversion ladder — 800V to 50V, 800V to 12V — and alongside it, rows of capacitors: five or six large snap-in parts per board, sometimes more than ten, sometimes hybrid polymer types instead. Eric offered a detail that sticks: an 800V power cable is about as thick as a grown adult's forearm, and heavy. But here's the easy misreading — **what's short is the specific high-voltage, high-capacitance parts, not the whole capacitor industry**; and what's actually shipping in volume is still the low-voltage segment.

**Some things only the show floor gives you.** Eric had been at a forum in Nangang that morning; it ran until nearly eleven, and he hesitated over whether to dash across town for the keynote. He went, and only got a seat in the highest tier. The opening film traced NVIDIA's arc and closed on a rendering of its future Taipei headquarters with a line across it: Taipei, where it all began. He says his eyes welled up — then added something I liked: the people who work in this industry are probably crying too, because all of it was made by supply-chain people working night after night. That presence isn't only sentiment. You have to stand in front of the cabinet to count how many capacitors are on a board, or to see how thick the cable really is.

## Going further

**1. Downgrades and delays are free bottleneck detectors.**

I keep coming back to one crude rule: **watch actions that cost something, ignore claims that don't.** Roadmaps, market-size forecasts, keynote visions — being wrong about those is free. Cutting a memory module from 192GB to 96GB trades product competitiveness for delivery, and that has a price. Prepayments, capacity reallocation and postponing a whole architecture belong to the same family.

This episode hands you two readings. **A downgrade tells you what's short** — whatever a company is willing to sacrifice is the thing it can't get. **A delay tells you which layer is stuck** — the blade rack is stuck on backplanes and mechanical qualification, the flagship package on warpage. Neither is a design problem; both are manufacturing and validation problems. That beats any verbal argument about where the bottleneck is, because it is already reflected in somebody's behaviour.

The kill condition matters too, or this becomes a universal excuse. **A downgrade has at least two entirely different causes: upstream shortage, or shrinking downstream demand.** They look identical and reading it backwards costs money. The tell: if it's shortage, that component's price rises and lead times stretch at the same time, and other products drawing on the same supply show similar compromises. If it's demand, you get the opposite pairing — the downgrade comes with softening prices and shorter lead times. The show reads it as the former, because memory is short at the same time CPU volumes are rising. **The nice thing about that test is that it can be falsified**: see module quotes fall and lead times shorten next quarter, and the whole argument goes in the bin.

**2. Essential is not the same as pricing power — the easiest trap once the bottleneck moves.**

Multi-node architecture has genuinely turned a batch of unglamorous parts into must-haves: management chips, power conversion, passives, server CPUs. But must-have and profitable are different claims. I run three questions over it: **how many suppliers can actually make this? What does it cost the customer to switch away? And does rising volume come with rising price?**

The third gets skipped most often. Going from five or six capacitors to more than ten is volume; if the spec is commodity and anyone can make it, volume gets eaten by falling unit prices and margins can end up worse. Conversely, one signal in this episode points toward pricing power: **when board space becomes scarce, the integrator eats its neighbour.** Folding an FPGA into a management chip raises the integrator's price and stickiness — but for the part being absorbed it means the demand still exists while the socket does not. That component's market hasn't shrunk; it just no longer needs to be bought separately. That's an opportunity and a risk in one signal, and reading half of it is worse than reading none.

There's a portfolio layer as well. The CPUs, management chips, capacitors and power designs in this episode look spread across four different industries, but they **share one assumption**: that multi-node architecture ramps on schedule. If that assumption breaks — and the episode itself says the blade rack may slip — all four go wrong together. **When a single bottleneck story explains several of your positions at once, that isn't diversification. It's one bet written on four tickets.**

**3. The same thing is happening at two scales.**

One passage went under-appreciated. Talking about the on-premise demos, Eric offered his own guess at the next stage: run a small local model first to classify the work and break down the workflow, then decide which model should handle each task — cutting out a great deal of unnecessary compute spend.

That is the rack architecture story at a different scale. **Inside the cabinet, a pile of nodes needs a management layer to dispatch them; over on the model side, a pile of models needs a dispatcher to decide who does what.** On both sides the value moves from *who computes fastest* to *who decides who computes*. Lao Tzu says it better than I can: thirty spokes share one hub, and what makes the cart turn is the emptiness at the centre. What is there gives advantage; what is not there gives use. The chips are what is there. The dispatch layer is what isn't.

One honest label, though: **this is the least evidenced part of the episode.** It's one researcher's personal projection, not something measurable on the show floor. So I file it as a question to check rather than a conclusion. The three I kept from this episode are all written the same way. Does the blade rack ship on schedule? Does the next flagship package end up with two dies or four? And what CPU-to-GPU ratio gets quoted on the next round of earnings calls?

**Conclusions expire; questions don't.** What a trade show should leave you with was never a set of answers — it's a set of things you can check the answers against later.
