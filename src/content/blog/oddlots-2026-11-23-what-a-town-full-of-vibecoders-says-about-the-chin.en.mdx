The Odd Lots episode of 23 November 2026 has Tracy Alloway and Joe Weisenthal talking with Aphra Wong, a reporter who covers AI in China and Silicon Valley, about her field reporting from Liangzhu — a small town outside Hangzhou known as China's vibe coding capital — and from an AI tutoring company in Hangzhou. She cites polling in which 83% of Chinese people expect AI to benefit their life and work, against 38% in the US; the AI education company she visited has 80,000 paying members at 3,000 to 4,000 yuan each, which she says is about half an average monthly salary in Hangzhou. When she asked Liangzhu's vibe coders how much money they actually make, most of them said they make it selling AI classes; and of AI-generated short dramas she says only 0.16% count as hits while 90% of the companies are losing money. These are one reporter's field observations and the figures she quotes, not a statistical census.

## What this episode is about

The show opens with Tracy describing the day before, when an older woman sitting next to her on the subway was watching an AI-generated soap opera on her phone — anime-looking characters getting engaged in an office building. It looked ridiculous, and the woman was watching it anyway. The guest is Aphra Wong.

She has recently published three pieces she calls her trilogy on AI and Chinese society: a trip to Liangzhu, an investigation into the AI tutoring industry that sprang up in China after DeepSeek, and a hunt for where AI-generated short dramas are actually made. Vibe coding means getting an AI to write the code in natural language instead of typing it line by line yourself. What she wants to know is how one particular person understands this technology and how they get onto the bus.

## China is betting on the diffusion layer

She says Xi Jinping only uses two phrases in public speeches: AI Plus, and AI as the highest-quality productive force, which she calls a very Marxist term. American attention sits on frontier models; what China wants is to push AI into thousands of industries — manufacturing, supply chains, education, agriculture — in the same sentence pattern as the old Internet Plus plan. That route has a precedent: the internet of a decade or so ago grew China's e-commerce and mobile internet companies, and ByteDance came out of that round.

![On the left, a triangle tapering upward with only its tip lit; on the right, a grid of blocks spread out sideways — one side competes on height, the other on width.](/figures/frontier-vs-diffusion-en.svg)

Her first reason for the 83% is counterintuitive: Chinese people have no choice. There is no organised collective way to say no to this technology, and living in that society it is easy to accept a totalising view of history — the country is heading in a direction, AI is waiting up ahead, the trend is inescapable and you have to take part. She calls that optimism a result of joylessness.

![Two bars of very different heights: the one labeled 83% on the left is more than twice as tall as the 38% one on the right.](/figures/trust-ai-83-vs-38-en.svg)

Her second reason is her grandmother. The grandmother is 81 now; in 1980s rural Shandong she had to walk five hours to buy a clock so her children could get to school on time. These days she is hooked on her Xiaomi phone, has bought voice-controlled light bulbs, loves telling the lights to change colour and switch on and off, and chats with Doubao every day. For her, the economy rising and the technology changing are one and the same thing. This is the part of the episode I remember best — once 83% lands on an 81-year-old woman who talks to her light bulbs, it stops being a poll.

Joe asks when China will wake up to the risks of artificial general intelligence, and she borrows Ilya Sutskever's phrase to say China has yet to feel the AGI. Then she turns the question over: China is running a full-stack race that counts how much electricity it generates and the whole AI supply chain, while the US is running a race China has not signed up for. The two are measuring different things, which takes apart the question of how many months behind China is.

![The upper track is made of three segments — power generation, supply chain, model — while the lower one has only the model segment, its earlier stretch left empty as a dashed line.](/figures/full-stack-vs-single-lane-en.svg)

## Liangzhu: people escaping 996, and a founder who wanted to be a Taoist monk

Liangzhu is twenty or thirty minutes from Hangzhou. The name means beautiful waterland and it is not an exaggeration — grass, marshes, lakes, rivers, low-rise buildings, cheap rent. Some of the people she met there were laid off from big tech, some got tired of 996 and left Beijing and Shanghai because they were too expensive, and some are young graduates who moved in without bothering to look for a job. She says Liangzhu lets both lives exist at once: you can lie flat, or you can go build something.

The products, she says, are quirky and whimsical, and none of them wants to be WeChat or Alipay: apps for people with ADHD, apps for people with dyslexia, a pile of mental health and focus apps, and fortune telling. The one she wrote up for the New Yorker is an AI astrology app called FateTell, whose founder wanted to be a Taoist monk ten years ago, loves the I Ching as much as he loves technology, and saw DeepSeek as the moment the two could finally be joined. The company takes this seriously enough that employees have their BaZi charts checked for compatibility before being hired; he invited Aphra to the office opening party because her chart carries a lot of fire and his carries a lot of water, so her presence would balance the room's energy. Liangzhu's municipal calendar also runs public events teaching people which colours to wear for luck.

Her explanation: the economy stalled, the property market burst, a lot of people feel they cannot get a hold on life, and they turn to astrology for some certainty. One of the most popular things to do with DeepSeek when it launched was to have it read your BaZi.

## The money comes from selling classes

She went to Liangzhu with one core question: how much money do you actually make from your vibe coding projects. She asked about users and got answers like three, seven, eight. Five to ten companies in that one town are building astrology or fortune-telling apps, and FateTell is the only profitable one.

![A row of ten cells with only one lit; below it, three small clusters of dots holding just three, seven, and eight dots.](/figures/ten-apps-one-earner-en.svg)

Most people's answer was that they make money selling AI classes. She did not understand at first, and digging in found a vast, unregulated, lucrative new industry filling every corner of the Chinese internet, from "DeepSeek 101" to "how to get rich with AI".

She visited a company branded as China's largest AI education company, on the 26th floor of a building in Hangzhou Future Tech City not far from Alibaba's headquarters. The name, she says, translates literally as "there is an art of getting rich"; the corporate colour is green, the colour of dollars, and they run a class called Use AI to Make Dollars. There are hundreds of classes and 80,000 paying members at 3,000 to 4,000 yuan, which she says is about half an average monthly salary in Hangzhou. Once you are a paying member you are encouraged to write promotional posts to recruit new members. Joe calls it an MLM outright and she agrees. The upgrade mechanic is collecting Dragon Balls: collect enough and you go from the Voyager level up to the Deep Sea Circle, a CEO-level club that meets in real life.

She reckons the members come in three kinds: proper underdogs, fresh graduates with a degree that offers no prospects or no elite-university name, who want to arm themselves with AI; smaller manufacturers who already have a business running and want AI to improve their supply chains or write their Amazon listings for overseas buyers; and professional vibe coders, who need like-minded people around them and a desk. The founder is an Alibaba veteran who says, by his own account, that he is helping young people gain dignity through learning — and in a society where education and meritocracy are held at near-religious status, she finds the scene both Confucian and absurd. Joe's first reaction to the words "selling AI classes" is that he hears preying on people's fears.

![From the payer in the middle, a thick solid arrow points to the side selling the course, while a thin dashed arrow points to his own revenue and breaks off halfway.](/figures/who-gets-paid-either-way-en.svg)

## The industrial slop complex

Tracy asks where the AI short dramas come from. Aphra says she set out to write a techno-optimistic story about AI slop — people had told her it might be a real tool for China to export soft power — and the more she reported, the more it looked like an illusion. What she found instead is what she calls an industrial slop complex.

Her account of the cause is concrete. Local governments are deeply risk-averse right now, money has to go into strategic sectors, AI has been designated a strategic sector, and every local government has to show Beijing it is putting technology first. AI-generated video is the lowest bar for entry — you can see it, and three minutes of it can be shown to someone. So Qingdao, Wuhan and Zhengzhou, cities that normally have nothing to do with tech, jumped on fastest. Tracy's line near the end is what makes it make sense: take a visitor from Beijing to a factory to see AI streamlining quality checks and they may be bored of that by now; show them three minutes of a robot fighting zombies and they might be impressed.

The result is what she calls the ghost city effect. She cites a projection of five million AI drama shows for next year that can be pushed through an app on your phone, and short video is extremely power-law — only the top content gets seen. She says only 0.16% of AIGC content counts as a hit, only hits bring in meaningful revenue, and 90% of AIGC companies are losing money today.

![On the left, only one of five hundred small cells is lit; on the right, a bar is nine-tenths dark with just a short lit stub at its base.](/figures/one-cell-lit-in-five-hundred-en.svg)

Tracy connects this to the shape she knows better: cyclical overcapacity. Her example is pork. A swine flu outbreak in the late 2010s wiped out a lot of supply, the government threw money at pork companies, the stocks shot up, a lot of them moved to industrial pig farming, and a year or two later the problem was massive oversupply, new suppliers going out of business and pork prices swinging up and down for years. Joe adds the Western version: between 2018 and 2021 a parade of politicians declared their city would be a crypto capital — Abu Dhabi, Dubai, New York all took a turn.

![A price curve shoots up to a peak, then collapses, and afterwards oscillates back and forth at a low level several times.](/figures/policy-named-overcapacity-curve-en.svg)

## The prosperity on the surface, and the numbers in the ledger

Joe asks how the leaps in batteries, cars and manufacturing capability, and the size of the export numbers, sit alongside a self-conception of stagnation. She says she does not want to destroy his China-maxing moment — Shenzhen does give you a futuristic ecological civilisation, and those cities feel more optimistic than most American ones — but she wants to translate both the share of anxiety and the share of optimism faithfully into her reporting. She recalls that 80% to 85% of China's high-speed rail lines lose money, that incomes have not really changed from ten years ago, that people are less willing to spend, that they will not put money into the stock market, and that the ones who put it into property lost it. Official youth unemployment is 18.9%, and she says she would cast some doubt on that number.

![One line keeps climbing while the other stays almost flat, the two spreading further apart toward the right.](/figures/exports-up-income-flat-en.svg)

She describes China today as a Japanified China: material abundance, a comfortable life, not much growth opportunity for an individual. Tracy asks whether she would still call China a high-trust society and she says no — post-COVID China has become more zero-sum, people fight each other for a civil service spot, the winner may get reported or resented, and people use Liu Cixin's "dark forest" from The Three-Body Problem to describe daily life.

## "The news says that place is booming — should I be chasing this theme"

Put this episode's material into a headline and it reads like "a whole country embraces AI, a whole town starts companies". My own hands itched a little listening to it, and then her question about user numbers put it out: three, seven, eight.

The question I ask first now: who in this boom gets paid whether or not I succeed. Liangzhu's answer is clear — the class sellers take 3,000 to 4,000 yuan without reference to how many users your app ends up with, and the people paying include graduates with no prospects and one-person companies that need a desk. The steadiest cash flow in a boom sits with whoever is selling shovels.

The second question is the denominator. What reporting hands you is usually activity: how many apps, how many dramas, how many cities announcing an AI push. She gave the denominator — a 0.16% hit rate, 90% of companies losing money. The mistake I am most prone to when judging whether a theme is hot is reading activity as demand. Of her three kinds of class buyers, the one I think is most likely to make money is the smaller manufacturer: the profit from diffusion has a chance of landing with the users rather than with the people building apps. The episode gives a hint and no numbers, so I hold it as a hypothesis.

## "The subsidies have arrived — do I get on this round"

A policy names an industry, the share prices move first, and whether to chase is a question I have lost money on. Tracy's segment lays out the shape: pork, tutoring, crypto capitals, the same sequence each time — the top sets a direction, everyone below competes to prove compliance, supply explodes, prices get cut to the death, and the sector gets washed out.

Two tests came out of this for me. The first is the barrier to entry: she says AI-generated video is the lowest bar for getting into the strategic sector, and the layer with the lowest bar is destined to be oversupplied. Pricing power sits with the layer that breaks first when demand doubles, not with the layer anyone can enter. The second is who the output is for: local governments want something they can show Beijing, so they pick what is visible and quick to make — and capacity built for an assessment does not stop because customers are not paying.

![Three tiers narrow from bottom to top; the widest bottom tier is marked as oversupplied, and the narrowest top tier is lit and marked as holding pricing power.](/figures/lowest-barrier-overcapacity-en.svg)

The failure condition belongs here too, otherwise this is just a bear case. FateTell is the one profitable astrology app in that town, so this round does have winners. Which turns the question into whether I can identify that 0.16% in advance. My answer is that I cannot, so what I do with themes like this is skip the single-stock bet and treat it as material for watching how a country's industrial policy actually works.

## The one thing to take with you

**When you see a boom, first find the person who gets paid whether or not you succeed.**

Her question in Liangzhu was one sentence: how much money do you actually make. It turned the town's whole narrative over — the app builders have single-digit user counts, the steady income is on the class-selling side, and whether a class sells has nothing to do with what the student later builds. That asymmetry shows up in every boom, and spotting it does not require understanding the technology.

![On the upper timeline the payment point sits right at the start, while on the lower one it sits far along, with a whole dashed stretch in between.](/figures/when-does-he-get-paid-en.svg)

Here is something I have tried that works outside investing too. Next time you see an ad, or a message a friend forwards, saying you will be left behind if you do not learn X, write down the person collecting your money and ask one question: does their income depend on you signing up, or on you having built something three months later. Those are two different things, and the question works just as well on cram schools, certification courses, personal training and online classes.
