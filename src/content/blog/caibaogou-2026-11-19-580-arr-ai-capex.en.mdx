Statementdog episode 580, released on 19 November 2026, has host Weiyu and a colleague from the research team putting three events from the same week side by side: at NVIDIA's earnings call on 17 November the chairman said the board does not intend to bring the former chief executive back and has already retained a search firm; the US Commerce Department issued new export controls the same week that write remote rental of compute explicitly into scope; and Anthropic, moving through an IPO process, is about to put its revenue on the table. The show's own estimate is that the controls affect roughly 3% to 5% of NVIDIA's incremental revenue, and that running those AI companies' revenue against their capex leaves a 2028 cash gap of about 5% to 15%. Both figures are the hosts' own arithmetic, and the first one assumes US buyers absorb the demand that used to flow to China.

## Back to 2016 first: every era has one driver

The episode opens with the damage in Taiwan. The index fell by low double digits over the past week, PCB and substrate names are down half from their highs for the year with some off seventy percent, and Taiwan no longer has a single stock above NT$10,000 — the last one broke the day before. Then they wind the clock back and ask a good question: why does this AI cycle look larger for Taiwan's supply chain than the iPhone cycle was?

Half the answer is that every era has one driver. From 2008 and 2009 the line ran through phones, with Apple pushing; the 2018 wave ran mostly through automotive semiconductors; now it is AI, with NVIDIA pushing. The show argues the most decisive moment for Taiwan came earlier — the mobile chip round positioned in 2014 and settled in 2016. The same phone model shipped with two versions of the chip, one from Samsung and one from TSMC, the market talk was that the Samsung version ran hot, TSMC took the socket, and the stock went from two digits to three. Before that, Intel, Samsung and TSMC traded the lead on the best chip actually in production, and nobody would have called TSMC the strongest; after that round nobody questioned it.

The other half of the answer is where the money lands. A large slice of the assembly and casing business in the phone cycle went to China and many Taiwanese suppliers left; now the world runs two separate supply chains and AI server assembly sits in Taiwan. Measured at the chip level both cycles matter; measured at assembly, this one is much bigger.

## From selling cards to selling racks: how the Neo Cloud entrants appeared

The next stretch taught me the most, because the question is institutional: why are the Neo Clouds — the new operators renting compute out — only appearing now?

This used to be a closed business. Cloud took hold around 2015, and the four large CSPs became the four large ones because they reached that scale, grew their own operations teams and published the OCP open hardware specifications, which is what let them go down a layer and build data centres themselves. If you were not one of the four, whitebox servers would not add up to anything in your hands — the machines arrive, the system does not grow by itself.

NVIDIA removed half of that barrier: it went from selling cards to selling whole racks, a complete system, assembled and handed over. The show keeps a caveat on it — the barrier did not drop very far — but it opened a new route, and with demand pulling, money is enough to get you in. Then the aside: money will not get you one easily right now either. The side effect lives in the same place. New entrants and whole systems are all booked to the same company, so when demand loosens here, it travels down the same route.

## That week in November: the call, the Treasury Secretary, the oversight committee

They had finished recording an episode on how much NVIDIA's chief executive matters to Taiwan's supply chain, and that same night word came that he was out. At the 17 November call the chairman said the board does not intend to bring him back and has retained a search firm; before that, the US Treasury Secretary said outright that NVIDIA should look outside for a chief executive and reshuffle its board — which drew a "what business is it of yours" on air, over a government handling a company's personnel. NVIDIA then set up a national security and export control oversight committee with independent directors, outside advisers and counsel, whose stated function is to strengthen communication with US government departments.

The hosts read it plainly: people used to call this chain of events a conspiracy theory, and from this week it is visible that the government sits inside it — otherwise the committee would be unnecessary and the Treasury Secretary would not be discussing personnel. They hold it against Huawei's experience under sanctions and call it statecraft operating on a company. On whether the former chief executive returns, their difficulty estimate is that shareholder activism here is up against a political party; they also add the inverted angle — a beaten-down share price and a stretch of ugly earnings make a return easier.

## The other blow that week: remote rental written into the rules

Commerce issued the new controls the same week, and the hosts went through the public documents and did the arithmetic themselves; their conclusion is that the impact is sizeable. The threshold is written as 12 million TPP, a unit of compute, which they convert for listeners into roughly 760 H100s, or four racks of GB200 NVL72. Their comment is that the threshold sits low — training a frontier model at today's level needs far more than that — so the purpose of this line is to keep more advanced model training out of reach. The scope is wide too: memory is in, scale-up is in, and who you ship to goes through review first. The diffusion rule issued before the previous administration left never pinned down rental; this one defines remote access to compute.

Two outside sources get held up against it. The Financial Times reported that about half of NVIDIA's revenue last year was booked through its Singapore subsidiary while under 2% physically shipped there. Bloomberg estimates that around 32% of the AI compute China can use in 2027 comes from renting offshore, and that 32% is what the rules are built to close. Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia and Brazil all require licences.

Their own estimate for NVIDIA: counting only the incremental portion, about 3% to 5%, with 3% as the conservative figure. That assumes US buyers absorb the released demand, and with supply still short, the single-company revenue line holds. A layer up it gets harder to say, because data centre approvals are slipping and application timelines are being pushed back at the same moment — progress blocked on one side, permits on the other.

## After ARR is disclosed: the haze clears and the cash gets counted

The ARR in this episode's title points here: Anthropic is going through an IPO process, revenue is about to be visible, and once it is visible you can work back to whether the whole capex plan adds up. The pleasant haze ends there — the upward estimates were fun, and the estimating continues, pointed down. The hosts run those AI companies' revenue against capex to see whether the cash covers it, and their own arithmetic leaves 2028 short by 5% to 15%. Short means raising money and borrowing more; given where these companies sit, one round is fine and two is a harder question.

![One tall bar for the capital spending due, a shorter bar for what revenue spread over time can cover, and a band at the top marking the gap.](/figures/capex-cash-gap-2028-en.svg)

The co-founder interview the show relays is the part I would most want others to hear. Asked whether open-source models and Chinese models will displace them on the easy tasks, he calls that old thinking: the old pattern is a field maturing and a cheaper rival arriving to take share, while AI capability climbs exponentially, so it keeps opening new markets instead. Three or four years ago, while everyone was watching chatbots, they were already looking at code, which is how Claude Code happened and a new market with new revenue opened.

Then he says something less comfortable: coding is reaching maturity too, price competition is starting, weaker players will come in and erode it, so they are already opening the next market — scientific research, physics. The show's verdict is blunt: it does not sound like money, the only part that smells like money is robotics, and a heavy bet on scientific research would do a great deal for humanity while doing less for the income statement.

## Where things stand now: China cuts SSDs first, 90-day slips, the bullwhip

In the supply chain, the order cuts the hosts have heard start with SSDs, and China moved fast. Why SSDs first? If the GPUs cannot be had, the expensive items go first, and with memory up two or three times, SSDs now count as expensive; the construction spend cannot be cut, since it is already committed.

Slippage is worse than cuts. Let a data centre's schedule slip and the reason for a price increase evaporates: ninety days, and material described as desperately tight a few days earlier gets waved off. That move amplifies as it travels up the chain — the show calls it the bullwhip effect and says the AI era has not fixed it — and lead times can shorten sharply, which is the leading indicator everyone reacts to.

![Four horizontal lines stacked from bottom to top: the same wave is small at the layer closest to the end customer and grows wider the further upstream it goes, swinging into a large curve at the top.](/figures/bullwhip-amplified-upstream-en.svg)

On why second-tier suppliers fell harder, the mechanism they give is clean: the first-tier firms' capacity was filled by the largest customer, which is why their NVIDIA share looks low; when demand softens, the first tier goes back out for other people's orders, and what gets taken is exactly what the second tier was holding. They cite a company shipping mainly for TPUs, with minimal NVIDIA exposure, falling just as hard.

![Before, the tier-one maker's slots are filled by its largest customer and the tier-two maker holds a few orders; after, the tier-one maker frees up slots and pulls those orders back, leaving the tier-two maker empty.](/figures/tier-one-takes-back-the-overflow-en.svg)

The multiple passage is worth copying down. Their description is that it gets more expensive as it falls: 10 times to start with, then earnings estimates come down after the order cuts, and it becomes 20 times, 30 times — you watch it drop and it keeps looking dearer. Which is also why he says research published before November can be skipped: the volumes and prices estimated then are too far from what has to be estimated now.

## Extension one: it is down forty percent, do I cut or hold

The line that tightened my chest was "if you have not sold by now, you may as well not sell". The criterion they give next is more use than the joke: look at the holding's position in its industry, then revalue it — the weight is on the word revalue. The split is that volume and price both move, and not necessarily together; volume may hold while price falls, or more precisely while the increases layered on this year come off. The ones that still look decent after that come off are the strong ones.

What I do is rebuild the valuation from scratch, starting by declaring the previous version void. The previous version's flaw was writing the price increase into a permanent assumption, when a price increase is rent collected while the industry is jammed, and it leaves the day the jam clears. Two numbers are worth writing down: whether volume falls (the kind of volume with a physical correspondence to end-market shipments), and how much of the price was added this year. I used to take the lazy route and multiply the whole valuation by a haircut, and afterwards I could not tell whether I had knocked out volume or price, which left the follow-up with nothing to check itself against.

![One bar split into three parts — volume, the original price, and this year's price increase — and the same bar at right is noticeably shorter once the top part is removed.](/figures/strip-this-years-price-hike-en.svg)

Rising memory prices reached domestic life too: someone on the show spilled a drink into a laptop, a few keys stopped going down, the repair quote came to tens of thousands of Taiwan dollars, and where replacing a laptop never needed a justification before, this time it did — and the advice was to go buy a Bluetooth keyboard.

## Extension two: the company says orders are unchanged, can I believe it

This is the most useful observation in the episode. The hosts describe a split among analysts they have talked to: Taiwanese analysts, working from company visits and order books, think the impact is small because supply is still short, while the foreign houses, looking top-down, think the impact is enormous.

Their reasoning convinces me: an event this size has to be read top-down, because transmission down to company level takes time, judgement takes time, and everyone's re-inventory takes time. There is a more practical layer too — ask the industry and they will tell you nothing has changed, which is the high-probability answer; for them to say something has changed, they first have to have gathered enough information to be sure of it, and only then does it get said to outsiders. So supply chain information gets demoted at a moment like this: it answers for today's orders, while the share price is betting on orders after transmission.

![The order line stays flat through the left half while the share price line has already fallen, and only after the dashed transmission-complete marker on the right does the order line bend down too.](/figures/orders-now-versus-after-transmission-en.svg)

So what is there to wait for? The show offers one thing: TSMC is auditing how much of its silicon ends up in China. Once that result lands, the numbers have legs, and it carries a counter-intuitive reading — if 3% of customers can no longer be served, 3% of capacity frees up to take other people's orders, which is good for the first tier and bad for the second. The moment the fog clears is often the better entry point, because a market that cannot see has only momentum to run on, and it gets pulled back once there is something to look at.

## The One Thing to Take Away

One idea: if your orders exist because somebody else is full, they are the first thing to go on the day that somebody is no longer full. This episode explains the second tier's harder fall cleanly — the first tier's capacity was filled by the largest customer, which is the only reason the second tier got those orders; demand softens, the first tier takes them back, and the volume in the second tier's hands is gone. On the way up this route has a name, the spillover effect; on the way down it has no new name, only the opposite direction.

Something I have tried that works outside investing too: write down three things currently in your hands because somebody else was too busy — the project that came to you, the role you were assigned, the job you hold in your circle of friends. Under each one, add a line: who stops being busy, and who does this go back to. Writing mine was uncomfortable, because two of them had the same name as the answer. Then pick one and think of a reason it holds regardless of how busy that person is, and spend this week making a little of that reason real.
