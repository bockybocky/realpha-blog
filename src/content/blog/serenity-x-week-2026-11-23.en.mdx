> If you can keep your head when all about you
> Are losing theirs and blaming it on you;
> If you can wait and not be tired by waiting.
>
> — Rudyard Kipling, "If—" (1910, public domain)

Serenity, an independent researcher on X, posted thirty-odd public notes between 17 and 20 November 2026, on the ground they usually cover: optics, memory, packaging and power. I am starting from the post where they took a loss in public, because that post set the tone for the week. Two numbers they cited disagree with each other: a graphics chip leader's supply commitments went from $279 billion to $390 billion in a single quarter, while hedge fund net momentum exposure fell from a peak of about 60% to 8%, according to an investment bank's prime brokerage data. Everything here comes from posts anyone can read on their public timeline; I did not touch a single word of anything behind a paywall. The figures come variously from public securities filings, public earnings calls and public news, plus a few lines relaying industry executives and a trade association, which are softer evidence and which I will flag as they come up. One limit up front: a supply commitment is a contract, contracts get revised and pushed out, and the terms are not public.

## Their week opened by admitting they had misread someone

On 17 November they posted a loss. An optical module maker had announced a $550 million at-the-market offering, and they wrote that the management team had lost all credibility with them.

They had nothing against the raise itself. The company had $956 million in commitments against $605 million in cash; the gap was there for anyone to see, and they clearly needed money. What angered them was the order of events. The CFO had said publicly that US bank financing could reach $500 million, that profits would help fund operations, that the share price mattered to them, and that they would lean harder on debt. Then the stock fell, and they sold new shares.

In the same thread Serenity offered a comparison: a peer in the same industry funds capacity expansion from customer prepayments, and convertible notes are another route. Several ways exist to close the same hole, and this company picked the one that costs existing shareholders the most.

Then came their own part. They had started buying around $25 and averaged up past $100, and this drawdown hurt badly. They wrote that the company still looks operationally compelling — the co-packaged optics programme, the module ramp, the $5 billion to $7 billion of long-term agreements said to be coming — but that they had misjudged the people, and had not foreseen the company reaching for abusive financing again after the sector sold off.

I read that post twice, because what it demonstrates is rarer than any industry datapoint: the industry call can be right and the company call wrong at the same time, and the second one eats the returns from the first. Their view on the optics chain did not change anywhere later in the week. What changed was their rating of one company.

## The same day, they pulled a number out of a filing

Shortly after the loss post, they went through the graphics chip leader's earnings call and pulled out the line "demand far exceeds supply". Every quarter somebody says that, and I was ready to skip past it, except they tagged the source of the next number — it came from the securities filing, and was not read out on the call.

The figure: supply commitments rose from $279 billion last quarter to $390 billion, and they noted the increase was driven mainly by multi-year memory procurement. In the same material, the company revised its year-over-year revenue growth expectation from about 70% to about 75%, leaning on capacity constraints easing, and mentioned new agreements with two substrate suppliers adding ABF substrate capacity.

Why single that number out? Because it is a future that has already been signed. Revenue is the past, guidance is the company's mouth, and a supply commitment is the company putting its own balance sheet behind a view — if it is wrong, that is its money. The weakness is that it is coarse: no prices, no breakdown by year, no visibility into cancellation terms. So it answers exactly one question, which is whether the volume moving through the upstream is growing or shrinking.

Two more items the same week have the same shape. One: a phone giant reportedly approached a Korean memory maker for DRAM long-term agreements, expected to start in the first half of 2027 and run four to five years — and they flagged that these contracts carry no price floors yet of the kind written into other hyperscaler agreements. Two: the substrate line they follow — a photonics silicon-on-insulator substrate maker that eight months ago had $25 million of quarterly revenue and a bank note warning that expectations might be excessive, now sitting on $1.2 billion of long-term customer capacity commitments with cash deposits attached. The indium phosphide substrate line went from a $100 million backlog to $500 million.

All of these are the same signal: a buyer put money or a signature on the table. On price floors their wording was careful — "not yet" leaves room for terms to be added later. So what these contracts support today is one thing only: somebody is willing to tie up four to five years of volume, while seller-side price protection has not shown up in public disclosure.

## The market's answer was written in a different ledger

The other half of the week ran the other way.

They cited prime brokerage data from an investment bank: hedge fund net momentum exposure fell from a peak of roughly 60% of equity down to 8%, and the weeks before 13 November saw the heaviest net selling of global tech stocks in nine years. They also noted a company whose market value once passed $5 trillion falling 46% in weeks, including a drop of more than 12% in one day on a report that a regulator was probing its accounting — on top of a vacant leadership seat and credit downgrades, with all of the uncertainty piling onto one name.

Their own description of this stretch is honest to the point of being funny. They said they have no idea how markets perform short term and anyone claiming otherwise is BSing; but tech is being sold off under forced deleveraging and degrossing, short interest is at record highs on many names, and consumer staples have been bid to valuations not seen since 1999. Their question: is the market right that the premium owed to rocket and AI companies belongs to towel resellers instead?

I want to pause here, because this is the easiest part of the week to read as emotion, and there is a defensible structure underneath it. The contract ledger counts in years — the memory agreements that surfaced this week run four to five years, with delivery starting in the first half of 2027. The positioning ledger counts in weeks, and it settles at month end. Those two were never synchronised, and a deleveraging stretch pulls them as far apart as they go.

But "it fell because people were forced to sell, so it has nothing to do with fundamentals" has a defect: every long says it on the way down, it cannot be falsified, so it only counts as one explanation sitting alongside another — that the market saw something I did not. Settling between them takes third-party evidence that has nothing to do with positioning, and this week produced two pieces of that, which I come to further down. Until then, the deleveraging explanation sits at "not refuted".

## They thought about someone else who had the direction right

On 18 November they wrote the passage I spent longest on, about another fund manager — the one who became known for a long essay on AI situational awareness and then ran their own fund.

Serenity's assessment: the early ideas were directionally right, from flash memory through fuel cells; even the recent one on passive components holds up, and Serenity themselves expect multi-layer ceramic capacitors to become a bottleneck in 2027. The failure sat somewhere else — excessive leverage and crowded longs, so the positions were liquidated before the ideas had time to play out.

Right direction, wrong time structure, and the result is zero. Put that next to their own loss post and the week knits together: they said their own lesson was to be narrower in picking longs, and the other manager demonstrates the other half — the stretch over which you are right can be longer than the stretch your position stays alive.

My feeling reading this is mixed. This kind of reflection slides easily into "I was just early", and "early" and "wrong" look identical in an account statement. What they did this week was keep two ledgers apart: whether the idea pointed the right way in one column, how the position got liquidated in the other — and the second column holds leverage multiples and how crowded the trade was.

## One line had nothing to do with the crash, and kept spreading

Three more items this week never touch the share price ledger, and they are the ones I find hardest to explain away with the "AI is a bubble" narrative.

The first is policy. On 17 November they relayed a news item: the US administration intends to press ahead with blocking imports of Chinese AI datacentre equipment, including new optical transceiver models, on the grounds that hawks in Washington worry the gear could be used to steal data, plant malware or disrupt services — with pushback from hyperscalers. The supply chain consequence has nothing to do with demand: it reallocates the same demand to Western transceiver, laser and component suppliers, while Chinese module makers and their upstream take the hit. A watershed drawn by politics sends utilisation on the two sides in opposite directions, and none of that depends on how bullish the market feels this year.

The second is printed circuit boards. They had written about this bottleneck back in January; the update is that a trade association issued an alert saying automotive, industrial, medical, defence and telecom all report longer lead times and difficulty securing supply, because AI is absorbing scarce capacity, putting production at risk. This one carries the most weight for me. AI saying AI is short is an interested party talking. A carmaker or a medical device maker saying it cannot get boards is third-party evidence, and those industries have no motive to talk AI's book.

The third is the layering inside memory. They corrected something that gets blurred together: the research house's recent downward price revisions applied to NAND, while expectations for legacy DRAM in 2027 are still being revised up. Their reasoning is that these run on different cycles — legacy memory sells into consumer products, and it is tight because makers are reallocating capacity toward AI specifications, a reallocation happening now and continuing into 2027. NAND and legacy DRAM are therefore moving in opposite directions on the same price sheet.

## If you also hold something where the facts improve and the price falls

I want to talk about the part of this week that was useful to me, because I suspect plenty of people are stuck in the same place: the fundamentals of the thing you hold look like they are improving, the price keeps falling, and you cannot tell whether you are holding your view or just refusing to admit a mistake.

I do not have a clean answer. But this week's material split the question into two that can be answered separately.

The first is "am I right". That one can only be answered with columns that have nothing to do with the share price — the three above: prepayments, delivery dates, qualifications. They sit in announcements, they update slowly, and they do not move because you are having a bad week. The substrate line going from $25 million of quarterly revenue to $1.2 billion of customer commitments belongs in that column; so does the fact that the company that did the offering still has its operational progress intact.

The second is "can I survive long enough", and it has nothing to do with whether I am right. Three things decide it: how my cost basis got stacked up, whether I borrowed, and whether this company will dilute me while I wait. The first two are mine. The third is the company's — and the offering this week shows how it works: while you wait for the industry to deliver, the company issues shares, and the payoff you were waiting for gets split among more people.

Once those two are separated, averaging down has a test. The version I have tried is to ask, before adding: am I adding because the price got cheaper, or because something in the columns I track got better? If it is only the first, I am adding to a price, and prices can keep falling. That question cannot tell me where the bottom is; it only splits "want to buy" from "should buy" into two separate actions.

## How to read someone who takes a loss in public

The other thing I want to talk about has nothing to do with the industry. It is about reading people.

Late in the week they posted a line saying they would try to make more higher-signal fundamental posts, and added that they tend to post random stuff when stressed, especially with the broader market crashing.

I like that kind of self-tagging, because it saves the reader a lot of work. Someone who tells you "my state has been poor these past few days" is attaching a time weight to their own output — the same account is not equally reliable in different weeks, and most people never say so. On 18 November they also posted something that reads as settling a score with a bank note from eight months earlier, and they laughed at their own language in the post itself. That one carries less information than the loss post, and without their own flag I would have had to spend time sorting which posts were material and which were mood.

These days when I read any high-frequency account, including my own notes, I start by splitting the content into two piles: what they are relaying, and what they are feeling. The relaying pile gets checked against primary sources. The feeling pile I keep as an observation about their state, and keep out of the inputs to a judgement. Of the thirty-odd posts this week, about ten belong in the first pile, and those ten are dense enough to carry this whole article.

As for the optics thesis they hold — they said it twice, on 19 and 20 November: across biotech, space, physical AI and cybersecurity, they still believe optics is the most compelling opportunity, because acquisitions and integration keep happening, venture money keeps flowing into new optical companies, and procurement keeps accelerating. That is their call. I am relaying it as is, neither endorsing nor adopting it.

## One thing to take with you

Next time you are about to average down on a long-horizon call, skip the price and go look at how that company raised its last three rounds of money.

Customer prepayments, bank debt, or new shares sold into the market? Those three decide the same thing: whether your slice gets cut smaller over the years you spend waiting. An industry call can stay right for years, while dilution happens at every single financing, and it does not need you to be wrong about a single industry number to eat your return. This week one company's CFO talked about banks and profits and then sold new shares, while a peer funds its expansion from customer prepayments. Two people equally right about optics end up holding very different things.

What I practised this week was adding one line at the top of each position note, holding nothing but how that company last raised money and when. Filling it in, I found several I could not answer, and not being able to answer is itself the answer — I was bullish on an industry but I own a company, and I had not been keeping those two apart.
